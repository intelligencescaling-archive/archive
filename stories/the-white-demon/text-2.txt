September 30, 3052.

White Lady Central Hospital.

Snow covered the area surrounding the hospital.

After fourteen hours, the screams finally stopped. Four-year-old Saiko had been waiting
outside the room the entire time.

The doctors came out after checking on Claire and the newborn, leaving the family alone.

The delivery had been complicated.

Exhausted, Claire held the child in her arms.

Kaito did not cry.

He only watched.

For a few seconds, mother and son held each other's gaze.

—Hello... —Claire whispered.

Kaito remained silent.

—Aren't you going to cry?

The child continued watching her.

Claire let out a small laugh.

—I suppose you've already decided how you're going to do things.

Abaoa approached the bed.

—How do you feel?

—Tired.

—I already imagined that.

—Then don't ask.

Abaoa smiled.

—Fourteen hours of labor and you still have the strength to argue.

—I'm not arguing.

—Of course.

Claire adjusted Kaito in her arms.

Abaoa extended his hands.

—Can you give him to me?

—Not yet.

—Can't I even hold him?

—Five minutes.

—Five?

—Five.

Abaoa pretended to think about it.

—Deal.

Claire smiled.

Abaoa headed toward the door.

—I'm going to register him.

—Don't take long.

—I won't.

Before leaving, he looked at the child again.

Kaito continued watching silently.

—See you in a little while, son.

Abaoa left.

Shortly afterward, at the hospital registry, he wrote:

Kaito Volkov.

Another child for the Volkov family.

Outside, the snow continued to fall.
Two years later, Abaoa began teaching his children. Before imposing a method on them,
he observed what captured their attention, how they solved problems, and what kind of
explanations they understood best. Based on this, he adapted the teaching to each one.

With Kaito, he started with reading.

Abaoa placed a sheet of paper in front of him.

—Tell me what it says.

Kaito read:

—The black dog ran quickly toward the garden because it heard a noise.

—Now don't tell me what it means. Tell me how it is constructed.

Kaito observed the sentence.

—The subject is «the black dog». «Dog» is the head, «the» the determiner, and «black»
the modifier. The predicate is «ran quickly toward the garden because it heard a noise».

«Ran» is the head, «quickly» indicates manner, «toward the garden» the direction, and
«because it heard a noise» is a causal subordinate clause.

Abaoa nodded.

—Now destroy it.

Kaito mentally separated each element.

—Determiner, head, modifier, predicate head, manner complement, direction, causal
subordinate clause, and direct object.

—You already did it.

—What?

—You destroyed it. You didn't memorize the sentence. You found what constructs it.

Abaoa took a pencil and wrote:

Structure → components → relationships → result.

—Words can change and a sentence can express something completely different. But if
you understand its structure, you can analyze others.

Kaito observed the diagram.

—So I don't have to memorize every sentence. I have to understand how they are formed.

—Exactly.

Kaito remained thoughtful.

—And does that work with everything?

—Try it.

—If I wanted to learn mathematics, I would look for the structures that repeat.

Abaoa remained silent.

—And if I wanted to understand a person, I would look for what lies behind their
decisions.

—Continue.

—If I wanted to solve a problem, I would break it down until I discovered what causes it.

Abaoa nodded.

—Now you understand why I don't want you to memorize answers.

—Because an answer only works for one problem.

—And a structure?

—It can work for many.

Kaito looked at the sheet again. He no longer saw a collection of words, but a system.

Abaoa understood that this way of thinking could extend far beyond language.

Later he would give it a name:

the Theory of Reduction.
With Saiko, Abaoa employed a different method: the Theory of Connection.

Unlike Kaito, Saiko was not supposed to reduce an idea down to its components. She
started from a premise and looked for what could be related to it.

One characteristic could lead to another, that relationship to a third, and that one could
open up a completely different connection.

Abaoa wanted Saiko to build a network of relationships, instead of following a single
logical path.

Idea → connection → new connection → network.

While Kaito learned to reduce systems, Saiko learned to connect them, creating
relationships whose trajectory could be difficult for someone external to anticipate.

During the following two years, both methods became part of their daily lives. Abaoa
applied them in exercises, games, and conversations, turning learning into a constant
practice.

Kaito analyzed.

Saiko connected.

And when they weren't studying, they spent their time fighting with each other.

At four years old, Riko was born.

Like her siblings, she did not cry. Her parents were already accustomed to it.

Exhausted, Claire held the newborn while Abaoa remained by her side. Kaito watched his
sister from the other side of the room.

—Do you want to meet her? —Claire asked.

Kaito nodded.

Claire handed her to him. Kaito held her for a few seconds and then looked at Abaoa.

—Can I hold her?

—You're already holding her.

Kaito lowered his gaze and tried to adjust her, but ended up holding her by one foot.

—Kaito... —Claire warned.

Abaoa intervened.

—Careful.

He took Riko and showed him how to hold her, placing one hand beneath her neck and
the other beneath her back.

—She still can't hold her head up by herself.

Kaito watched attentively and, when Abaoa handed her back, he imitated the position
exactly.

—Like this?

—Like that.

Kaito looked at Riko.

—Now she's safe.

—Yes.

After a few seconds, he smiled.

—I'll be your protector from now on, little sister.

Claire and Abaoa smiled.

But Saiko immediately appeared in front of him.

—You've already had a lot of time with her.

—I just met her.

—Exactly. Now it's my turn.

—No.

Saiko tried to take Riko, but Kaito pulled her closer to his chest.

—I said I'm her protector.

—Yeah, right. And pigs fly.

—Pigs don't fly.

—Then give her to me.

—No.

—Kaito.

—Saiko.

Claire burst out laughing while Abaoa shook his head.

—She was just born and you're already fighting over her.

Finally, Saiko managed to take Riko.

—I told you it was my turn.

Kaito watched her for a few seconds.

—Okay.

Saiko looked at him suspiciously.
—What?

—You can hold her. But be careful.

—I know.

Kaito looked at Riko. She remained calm, completely unaware of the argument.

He still did not understand what it meant to protect someone. He did not know what
could happen to her or how much he would have to learn to fulfill that promise.

He only knew one thing:

She was his sister.

And for Kaito, that was enough.

Outside, a few drops began to fall over the city.

Three years passed.

During that time, Kaito had progressed to the point where Abaoa's tasks began to change.
He was no longer simply supposed to learn something, but decide how to learn it.

Riko was beginning her own learning, while Saiko had become the most advanced of the
three. Abaoa needed to devote more time to Riko, so before beginning the next lesson, he
left Kaito a task.

—In one year, I want you to learn six languages.

Kaito looked up.

—Which ones?

—You'll have to decide that yourself.

—Can I choose any?

—Any.

—And do I have to learn them to the same level?

—Almost native.

Kaito nodded and went to the living room.

The problem wasn't how to learn six languages.

It was which ones to choose.

He sat in front of a table and wrote:

What makes a language useful?

He quickly discarded a single metric. The language with the most speakers did not
necessarily offer greater access to knowledge; the most widespread one could have little
scientific relevance, and the economically dominant one did not necessarily maximize
linguistic transfer.

He divided the problem into seven variables:

Number of speakers: 20%
Geographical distribution: 15%
Economic influence: 20%
Scientific influence: 20%
Cultural influence: 10%
Available information: 10%
Transfer between linguistic systems: 5%

Then he established a scale:

Top 1 = 5 points
Top 2 = 4
Top 3 = 3
Top 4 = 2
Top 5 = 1

Kaito researched each category, recorded the five highest-ranking languages, and
calculated the weighted score of each one.

He was not looking for the most important language in a single dimension.

He was looking for the greatest cumulative usefulness.

When he finished, six languages stood above the rest.

He wrote them on a sheet:

Volnyr
Zhavorin
Orlovek
Azhevorsk
Velyransk
Zerimov

Kaito put down the pencil.

It had taken longer than expected, but he finally had a selection based on multiple
variables, weights, and relationships.

Then he heard footsteps.

Claire appeared with a plate.

—Kaito.

—Yes?

—Do you want chocolate cookies and milk?

Kaito looked at the table and then at his mother.

—Yes, Mommy.

Claire placed the plate on the table.

—They're still hot. Eat carefully.

—Thank you, Mommy.

Claire left.

Kaito took a cookie, bit into it, and looked at the sheet again.

The six languages were still there.
Now came the complicated part.

Kaito had to learn Volnyr, Zhavorin, Orlovek, Azhevorsk, Velyransk, and Zerimov to C1
within a year.

He looked at the six names.

He did not intend to learn them as six independent lists. If he wanted to reduce the
problem, he first had to discover how they were constructed.

He took a sheet and wrote:

Phonology → Grammar → Vocabulary → Order → Function → Relationship.

He began by listening to native speakers to identify phonological patterns, permitted
combinations, and their restrictions.

Then he studied grammar, prioritizing structures capable of producing the majority of
sentences instead of memorizing every possibility.

He did the same with vocabulary. He selected the most frequent words and related them
to their function within the sentence.

He did not want to memorize:

word → meaning.

He wanted to understand:

sound → function → structure → meaning.

But there were still six languages.

Kaito placed their structures side by side and began looking for shared patterns,
differences, and functional equivalences.

He classified:

What remains.
What changes.
What is replaced.
What can be reused.

If a structure fulfilled a certain function in Volnyr, he looked for how Zhavorin expressed
that same function. If there was no direct equivalent, he identified what mechanism
served a similar purpose.

He was not trying to make the languages identical.

He was trying to discover how each one solved the same problems.

Thus, instead of studying six isolated systems, he studied six related systems.

During the following months, he read texts and articles by natives, listened to
conversations, wrote, debated, and expressed ideas in all six languages.

Each error was classified:

What rule did I break? Why? What information was missing? What do I need to modify?

The error became information about the system.

Four months later, Kaito closed his book and walked over to Abaoa.

—What's wrong?

—I did it already.

Abaoa looked up.

—What did you do?

—I learned the languages.

—I gave you a year.

—I know.

—Four months have passed.

—Yes.

Abaoa observed him.

—At the level I asked for?

—Yes.

—Can you converse, write, explain complex ideas, and debate?

—Yes.

—Prove it.

Kaito began speaking in Volnyr. Then he switched to Zhavorin, Orlovek, Azhevorsk,
Velyransk, and finally Zerimov, maintaining the same line of reasoning for five minutes
and adapting the structures of each language.

When he finished, Abaoa remained silent.

—I noticed that you thought for a second before some words.

—Yes.

—Why?

—Because I don't know all the languages.

Abaoa raised an eyebrow.

—Then you didn't learn them.

Kaito shook his head.

—I did learn them. I didn't memorize every word. I learned the rules.

—And when you're missing a word?

—I can infer it. I identify the possible phonemes, the restrictions of the language, and the
function it should fulfill in the sentence. Then I reduce the possibilities until I find what it
should be.

Abaoa reviewed his answers.

Kaito's structural understanding and command had surpassed the expected C1 and were
approaching C2 in grammar. However, he still lacked part of the cultural intuition of a
native speaker.

He could reconstruct structures, infer words, and transfer patterns, but an expression
could be grammatically correct and still sound strange. The social, emotional, or cultural
meaning of certain constructions could not always be deduced through rules.

Kaito understood how languages worked.

He was still learning how people used them.

Abaoa closed the papers.

—Well done, Kaito.

—What's next?

Abaoa looked at him.

He had understood something important.

He was not simply teaching Kaito to learn languages.

He was teaching him to find the systems that produced whatever he wanted to learn.

The same idea kept appearing:

structure → components → relationships → result.

Kaito had learned six languages, but the most important lesson was another:

when he did not know an answer, he could try to reconstruct it.

Abaoa took a chocolate bar and threw it at him.

Kaito caught it.

He looked at it. Then he looked at his father.

—Don't try to use operant conditioning on me, Dad.

Abaoa smiled.

—Oh, really? Then you don't want it.

He extended his hand to take it back.

Kaito looked at the chocolate.

—...

Abaoa waited.

—You're trying to generate an association between completing a behavior and receiving a
reward.

—Correct.

—And if I accept, I reinforce the behavior.

—Also.

Abaoa began to withdraw his hand.

—Then I suppose I can put it away.

—...Give it to me.

Abaoa stopped.

—Weren't you avoiding conditioning?

Kaito extended his hand.

—I can know exactly what you're doing and still want the chocolate.

—Heh. All right.

He handed him the bar.

Kaito began opening it.

—But it didn't work.

—Didn't it?

—No. I just wanted chocolate.

Abaoa looked at him.

—Sure.

Kaito narrowed his eyes.

—You don't believe me.

—Not particularly.

Kaito ignored the comment and ate the chocolate.

Three more years passed.

During that time, something began to change.

Claire stopped accompanying them in some activities. Abaoa began to be absent for
increasingly long periods. Conversations became shorter and his explanations more vague.

Until one afternoon, their parents asked them to sit with them.

Claire placed a cup of tea on the table.

—Kids... we're going to disappear.

Riko frowned.

—Disappear?

—Yes.

Kaito looked at both of them.

—Where are you going?

Claire exchanged a look with Abaoa.

—We can't tell you.

—Why?

—Because knowing too much can also put you in danger —Abaoa replied.

Kaito was not satisfied.

—That doesn't answer the question.

—I know.

Abaoa took out several letters and handed them to Saiko.

—I want you to keep them. Use them when you don't know what to do.

Saiko looked at them.

—What for?

Abaoa held his gaze.

—When the time comes, protect your siblings. At all costs.

Saiko understood that it was not a simple request.

—Okay.

Claire smiled slightly.

—It's not your fault. We'll come back when we can.

Kaito continued observing them.

There was something that didn't fit.

Not only because of what they were saying, but because of what they were avoiding
saying.

Then he saw it.

A small crack ran across Abaoa's arm.

The skin began to fragment and small pieces fell to the floor.

Kaito opened his eyes.

—Dad...

Abaoa lowered his gaze.

—It's nothing.

—Your arm is breaking.

—I know.

Another fragment fell.

Kaito understood that it had already begun.

Abaoa placed a letter on the table.

—When we disappear, agents will come looking for you. Make sure at least one of them
sees this.

Saiko nodded.

—Understood.

Claire picked up her cup again.

Her hands were trembling slightly.

—Before it happens... why don't we talk for a while?

For the next few minutes, they talked about anything.

What they had done that day. Who had won at cards. What they had eaten.

Saiko and Riko even began arguing over a game.

Claire and Abaoa laughed.

For a few moments, everything seemed normal.

Until another fragment fell to the floor.

Kaito remained silent.

He watched.

There was less and less of his parents left.

Then Claire added:

—Someday, a white-haired lady will find you.

—Who? —Kaito asked.

—We don't know.

Abaoa intervened.

—When you find her, stay with her.

The three nodded.

—Understood.

Claire's body began to fragment as well.

First her arms. Then her shoulders and finally her face.

Riko understood what was happening.

—Mom...

Claire extended her hand.

—Take care of each other. No matter what happens.

Abaoa looked at the three.

—Swear it.

Saiko pressed the letters against his chest.

—I swear.

—Me too —said Riko.

Kaito remained motionless.

He did not know how to react to a farewell he still did not understand.

But he understood the promise.

—I swear.

Claire smiled.

The last fragments began to detach.

Saiko began to cry. Riko took refuge against him.

Kaito did not cry.

He only watched.

As if as long as he could still see his parents, they had not yet disappeared.

Abaoa looked at him one last time.

—Be strong.

Claire added:

—And remember that we will always be watching over you.

The last fragment fell.

His parents disappeared.

The cup of tea remained on the table, still giving off some steam.

No one spoke.

Kaito continued looking at the place where they had been.

For the first time, he found himself facing something he could not reduce to variables.

There was no structure to analyze.

No rule to discover.

No answer to reconstruct.

Only an absence.

And Kaito did not know what to do with it.

A few minutes later, the agents arrived.

Saiko was still crying. Riko remained hugging Kaito, while he continued looking at the
door.

—Calm down. They said they'll come back.

He pointed to the place where his parents had been.

—And if they said it, then they'll come back... right?

Neither answered.

Kaito waited a few seconds.

—Right?

The agents exchanged a look.

The silence worried him more than the disappearance.

One of them began searching the room until he stopped in front of a letter.

There was a name on the envelope:

Governor Kim Geodor.

The agent opened it.

It was not a farewell.

It was a threat.

Dear Governor Kim Geodor:

Claire and I have disappeared. The three children have been left alone.

I want you to adopt them.

I know you have hidden your relationship with the Bio Mafia for years and that you are
one of its main financiers.

I have gathered evidence.

You have two weeks.

If you do not protect them, all the information will be made public.

Take care of them, Kim.

Even though you can no longer see me, believe me.

I can still see you.

The agent finished reading and slowly raised his gaze.

Kaito was watching him.

—What does it say?

The man closed the letter.

—Nothing you need to know.

Kaito did not insist.

But he had seen his expression.

Fear.

Someone had managed to frighten an adult even after disappearing.

That meant Abaoa had left something prepared.

Later, the three were transferred to a special room.

Riko fell asleep near Kaito. Saiko remained by the door.

—What is the Bio Mafia? —Kaito asked.

—I don't know.

—But Dad used it to blackmail the governor.

Saiko remained silent.

Kaito continued:

—Then Dad knew this was going to happen.

—This isn't the time to investigate.

Saiko stood up.

—We need to rest.

—And you?

—I'm going to find out what's going on.

Riko opened her eyes and extended a hand.

—Stay.

Saiko stopped.

Then he returned to them.

Kaito watched him.

He still did not understand everything, but he was beginning to recognize the pattern.

His parents had not simply disappeared.

They had prepared a chain of decisions.

And they were inside it.

During the following months, Governor Kim tried to adopt the three.

Once.

Twice.

Then he lost count.

Every attempt encountered new opposition.

The authorities considered the children a threat. They had detected an energy since their
birth that did not correspond to what they considered natural.

They did not need to wait for them to prove they were dangerous.

For them, simply existing was enough.

The proposal began circulating among the rulers:

immediate execution.

Kim opposed it.

Kaito understood that he was not doing it solely for them. If he adopted them, he would
fulfill Abaoa's condition and protect his own secret.

Kim did not pretend very well.

Kaito knew it.

Even so, he decided to believe that perhaps he wanted them to survive.

Not because he cared about them.

Simply because, for Kaito, that was better than nothing.

Eventually, Kim lost the battle.

But the rulers did not get what they wanted either.

The children would not be adopted.

Nor would they be executed.

They would be sent to a place that officially did not exist.

A secret orphanage.
The orphanage was exclusively intended for children considered extraordinary.

Geniuses.

There were no reports, photographs, or announcements.

To the world, the three had simply ceased to exist.

One morning, a car arrived.

The agents made them get in. Riko held Saiko's hand while he looked at the house one last
time.

Kaito remained looking out the window.

He did not cry.

He was still trying to solve a question:

Why had his parents prepared all of that if they really intended to come back?

The answer was not in the car, nor in the agents, nor even in Kim.

It was somewhere along the path Abaoa had built before disappearing.

And Kaito still did not know how to find it.

The car continued moving forward.

Behind them was their home.

Ahead, the orphanage.

For the first time, the three siblings were completely alone.

From the outside, the building looked normal.

Too normal.

There were no imposing walls or signs revealing its true function. It could have gone
unnoticed among any other building in the city.

But inside there were six floors, 200 cameras, and 90 guards.

The windows could not be opened from inside and all the lighting was artificial.

Some guards were strict.

Others even seemed kind.

The place did not need to look like a prison.

It only needed to function like one.

The siblings were led to room 43.

A caretaker opened the door.

—You can leave your things wherever you want. Take a moment to get settled.

Saiko, Kaito, and Riko entered.

They observed the walls, ceiling, and corners before arranging their belongings.

When they finished, the caretaker returned.

—Good. Now let's introduce you.

She led them to a common area, where several children were reading, playing, or simply
ignoring their presence.

—Say hello.

Saiko stepped forward.

—Hello. I'm Saiko Volkov.

He paused.

—If you think about hurting my siblings, I'll break your legs.

Silence.

The caretaker froze.

Kaito looked at him.

—You didn't need to say it like that.

—Yes, I did.

Riko let out a small laugh.

Then it was Kaito's turn.

—Hello. I'm Kaito Volkov.

Riko raised her hand.

—Hi. I'm Riko.

Nothing more.

The others barely reacted.

Or so it seemed.

Kaito had observed something.

When Saiko mentioned that he would break the legs of anyone who hurt them, two
children stopped paying attention to their games.

When Riko introduced herself, another slightly raised his head.

And when Kaito said his name, a caretaker showed a brief pupil dilation.

It lasted barely an instant.

Kaito said nothing.

He simply remembered it.

The following days passed with a strange normality.

The siblings began to get to know the place.

There were children with extraordinary abilities: exceptional memory, accelerated
learning, mathematical abilities, mastery of languages, strategy, music, and analysis.

But there was something they all had in common.

None of them seemed to be there by choice.

They had not simply been abandoned.

They had been separated.
Kaito began to understand that the difference mattered.

A normal orphanage sought to find them a family.

That place seemed to seek something else.

Saiko adapted the worst.

He argued, provoked, got into fights, and received punishments.

But there was something that never changed.

When another child had problems, Saiko intervened.

It did not matter if he ended up being punished afterward.

He never explained why.

He simply did it.

Riko took another path.

For weeks she observed in silence.

Who talked to whom. Who lied. Who obeyed. Who avoided whom. Who was alone.

Little by little, she began to reconstruct the relationships within the orphanage.

Kaito, on the other hand, discovered board games.

At first he simply played.

Then he began analyzing the rules, probabilities, patterns, and decisions of his opponents.

First he won some games.

Then most of them.

Finally, he began defeating even children who had been playing for years.

For Kaito, games had ceased to be games.

They were systems.

And Abaoa had taught him to look for what was beneath them.

That was why every defeat became information:

defeat → variable → modification → new test.

Until victory came.

The children began talking about him.

The caretakers also began observing him.

Kaito did not just learn quickly.

He learned how to learn.

Then it happened.

One day, while Kaito was playing a game, the others began looking toward the entrance.

Saiko stopped arguing.

Riko interrupted her observation.

Even some of the caretakers fell silent.

Kaito slowly raised his gaze.

Someone was entering.

At first glance, he did not seem particularly special.

But everyone's reaction said otherwise.

Kaito placed a piece on the board.

For the first time since he had arrived at the orphanage, he stopped studying the game.

He began studying the player.

Until he arrived.
—You must be Kaito.

Kaito raised his gaze.

In front of him was a boy who did not seem particularly impressed by the board.

—Hum? Yes. And you are...?

—Kamil.

He pointed at the pieces.

—I challenge you to a bet.

—What are we betting?

Kamil smiled.

—The winner takes whatever they want from the loser.

Kaito observed the board.

—That doesn't seem fair.

—Are you afraid?

Kaito picked up a piece.

—No.

The game began.

Kaito was accustomed to winning. He observed patterns, anticipated moves, and reduced
his opponents' possibilities.

But Kamil played differently.

He sacrificed pieces, changed the pace, and made moves that seemed bad until several
turns later.

Kaito began to frown.

When he understood the plan, it was already too late.

—Checkmate.

Kaito looked at the board.

He had lost.

Kamil smiled.

—I thought you'd be better.

Kaito remained silent for a few seconds.

He was not upset.

He was analyzing the game.

—I need to practice more.

Kamil blinked.

He had probably expected an excuse.

He did not receive one.
—Can I teach you?

Kaito narrowed his eyes.

—Really?

—Yes.

—Will you risk being humiliated?

Kamil burst out laughing.

—That won't happen.

The following games also ended in defeat.

But Kaito began to detect patterns.

Then he began to anticipate them.

Finally, he began forcing Kamil to modify his strategies.

The games became longer.

And so did the conversations.

At first they only talked about chess. Then they began playing even without a board.

Until they no longer needed one.

—King to g3.

—Queen to g5. Check.

—King to f2.

—Queen to d2. Check.

The position continued developing solely in their minds.

Kaito visualized every piece, every square, and every possible move without having
anything in front of him.

Kamil made his next move.

Kaito closed his eyes for an instant.

He mentally reconstructed the position.

—Queen to g2.

Silence.

Kamil stopped speaking.

He was trying to reconstruct the board in the same way.

Finally, Kaito smiled.

—Checkmate.

Kamil opened his eyes.

He had lost.

By very little.

—Wow... not bad.

Kaito raised an eyebrow.

—Is that all?

—You realized it in time.

Kaito looked toward the nonexistent board.

—I can barely see it.

—But you saw it.

Kaito smiled.

—Heh.

Kamil laughed.

—Then let's play again.

And they both continued.

Over time, the bets stopped mattering.

Kaito and Kamil no longer played to win something. Chess became their way of
communicating.

Kaito analyzed positions for hours; Kamil tried to create situations he could not anticipate.
When one discovered something, the other searched for an answer.

The competition remained, but it stopped separating them.

Kaito had learned that games were systems that could be understood. Kamil taught him
that they could also become a way of understanding another person.

Without realizing it, they stopped being rivals.

They had become friends.

Ninety people meant ninety different models.

The same anomaly could produce ninety different responses.

Therefore, Kaito and Riko had to continually distinguish between:

what was happening,

what each guard believed was happening,

what each guard wanted to achieve,

and the action he would probably take as a consequence.

That process required simultaneously understanding behavior, emotions, motivations,
priorities, and interpretation of the situation.

They could not afford to reduce the guards to mechanical patterns.

They had to treat them as agents capable of thinking and adapting.

They did not construct a physical map they could consult.

They constructed the map inside their own heads.

They did not construct a list of ninety guards.

They memorized their individual patterns and the psychological models necessary to
anticipate their decisions.

They did not write down twenty-five escape routes.

They learned the conditions that allowed them to generate new routes.

After finishing their chores and completing the remaining time of that session, they met.

Each verbally reconstructed their own model and compared their conclusions with the
other's.

If a position matched, it was confirmed.

If a route differed, they mentally reanalyzed the observations that had led to that
conclusion.

If one had interpreted a guard's behavior differently, they examined the variables that
could explain the discrepancy.

Finally, they arrived at a common representation.

The entire building.

The surveillance systems.

The positions and patterns of the cameras.

The sensors.

The routes.

The temporal changes.

The ninety guards.

Their individual behaviors.

Their relevant psychological characteristics.

Their possible responses.

And the conditions that could cause any of those variables to change.

All stored without a single sheet of paper.

When they finally began moving, they did not blindly trust their predictions.

They tested them against reality.

Every guard change required comparing the observed behavior with the model stored in
their heads.

If it matched, they continued.

If it did not match, they mentally modified the route.

One prediction failed.

Riko stopped.

Kaito looked at her.

She pointed in another direction.

There was no time to argue.

Kaito quickly reconstructed the relevant variables.

Guard.

Camera.

Time window.

Alternative route.

Risk.

He chose.

It worked.

Later, another deviation occurred.

This time Kaito reacted first.

Then another appeared.

Riko corrected.

Another one.

Kaito changed the trajectory again.

The guards continued behaving in ways that did not always match their predictions.

And they continued adapting.

The escape stopped depending on a perfect prediction.

It depended on their ability to quickly detect when a prediction had failed and reconstruct
the next decision using the available information.

They finally reached the outer area.

Riko had previously memorized how the supply vehicles operated, their schedules, and
the opportunities they could use.

They waited.

A window compatible with the model appeared.

They hid among the usual movement of the staff and managed to leave the building.

When they were outside, Kaito looked back.

The orphanage was still there.

Six floors.

Two hundred cameras.

One hundred forty-four microphones.

Seventy-two sensors.

Thirty-six temperature detectors.

Ninety guards.

And everything they had discovered still existed only in their memories.

Riko took him by the arm.

—We have to go.

Kaito nodded.

But before leaving, he went back for something.

Saiko's body.

He couldn't leave her there.

The two continued walking, not knowing exactly where to go.

Until they heard a voice.

—Are you boys okay?

Both stopped.

Kaito raised his gaze.

A white-haired woman stood before them.

She did not look like an agent.

Nor did she seem surprised to find them.

Kaito positioned himself in front of Riko.

—Who are you?

The woman looked at them.

—I suppose you don't know me yet.

She paused.

—I'm an envoy of your father.

Kaito did not respond.

Neither did Riko.

The woman looked toward the path they had just traveled.

—It's time to go home.

Silence.

Kaito lightly squeezed Riko's hand.

For four years, he had thought that his parents had disappeared.

Now someone had just told them that there was still a place they could call home.

And, for the first time since that night, Kaito did not know what conclusion to draw.

End of the orphanage arc.
