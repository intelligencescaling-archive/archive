FANFIC OF THE SUB: THE MIST ACCORDING TO, PT. 2

CHAPTER 2: THE VAGUENESS OF EARLY DAYS

If I am allowed of my recollections and my agency to my own personal intimations of my private muddled memories, a question of my own adequacy to begin this, to the states of the in-public tones that ran through my indifferent companion's world, I would have to begin with the statement of her father: Dr. Verstappen; he like her was of complete contempt, an illusory disposition negative to the state of all things: he was without any reason; for many reasons I am to liken he to her and she not to he but to the veil he was to her throughout those early days.

Verstappen was what we call a Renaissance Man, one without living in the Renaissance: thus he was deprived of any seeming sense to most us, stuck backwards; Night said it best to I, once—"We live in the past times because we know of what is to be of them: it is not easy to live out of memory; it is not easy to play chess out of the comforts of a book. Whatever freedom is granted to us by the present we know not to do with, for an infinite kind of freedom, of the natures contradicting, is a difficult thing to weather, and to make sense of."

He was perhaps always in the past because he feared of the present as many of us do: and so did Night. Whatever vessel they exerted to sail and traverse through an unknowable set of distances, they could become kings of little maritime kingdoms until they collapsed under whatever external or internal pressure exercised force upon them.

I remember he never removed his bowler hat before me or Doggo: certainly not Doggo, whom he despised without any hesitation or logic.

Needless to say Night and her father, Dr. Verstappen, were not of good conditions towards each other: he wanted her to be him, and she wanted nothing to do with him until he wanted her to be like her mother. He was a good man with a respectable old head of any Kingdom Subredian, but with it he had been beheaded with the changes of time; no longer of he and his breeding were of welcome companion to this eerie change that befit us and shifted us back and forth from ourselves more fervently than before. He knew little of chess but fancied himself to it; in ways, he fancied it because his child was tolerant to him in that field of expertise: the two, I believe, attempted to live through each other when they could not see to each other's eyes; the more they had, the more they wanted, and the less they were able to understand of themselves and their own proximities, they departed usual family and became colleagues of two generations, always quarreling, always together nonetheless, as the blood denied them of an equal indifference to their kinship.

He made do of everything that he suspected he lacked in expertise, and in not suspecting alone but also subconsciously perceiving of his failures, by a combination of indescribable severity, energy, and pendatry: chess, no exception, was a board game he took far too seriously.

I do confess't, I feel as if I distantly agree with the past that I came upon to see the two staring off, sitting at distance by a window; I saw two people who, although bypassed by their consciousness, and thoughts, and vessels across that endless sea, were unfortunately doomed to a forever-extreme preference for dreaming, living outside of time, and doing what they could to make time and space whatever dream they envisioned for themselves.

I end my first speech of Dr. Verstappen and must now recall Night's mother: I say I write of her with a good heart of fondness, as in gratitude to the happy childhood (as best as happiness allows itself to extend across Night's troubles), she excelled in the placating of the tumultuous youth: Ice. I have truly never a'upon-ed again a more un-pleasant and warm person such as that woman: Ashes took after her more than Verstappen, which he will deny.

The two children took after their parents in appearance, true inheritors of hair and eyes and noses.

I must clarify: then in those days we had little understanding: it was always Night who had inwardly wrestled with something till' it drove her mad and would send her in a cooped-self imprisonment until she figured whatever ailment had multiplied to stay in her thoughts. That, again, is the wiseness that possessed her: and alongside it came the indifference, the ease of in-expression, and the fervency of being a kind of living privation to Verstappen, who in conjunction to her acted in similar dress.

Furthermore it is a site of remembrance that I recount this; I had gone for berries and Night, alone in her "meadow", had apparently wandered off until I was a distant after-figure; then I did not understand, now I still do not. When I returned from my minor expedition for those berries, she treated me far less of T.G and more now as a person she had once known but had been un-acquainted. Evidently I was offended by this sudden increase of her usual distance. Still, my companion Night had a subconscious conflict with whatever I was, and whatever she was; whatever we were, to begin with. I comfort myself now believing that she had a fractured logic of reality, and yet reality was always running to her, and she always making reason of it, over anyone else.

These were odd days; no talk nor others returned her to any place that had had a T.G, nor a Faded. I was once again renamed, now to Vergilius, formal and polite, one who must wear a bowler hat and speak to her in tones that I, even back then, found reminiscent of her kin-patriarch.

And her demeanor had shifted again: now bequeathing herself action: great violence perhaps had dwelled but now she was in effect possible of a perpetration of it: this was the first of her bouts with the entering and leaving of the "Magic Show,", another place that grew alongside her "meadow." She Played King without ruling with words no more, but sat up in the grass and tree ossuary of her own room-confine of thoughts, and upon it was sutured the vague resemblance to a freeing vagrancy that denied always left her in more locked recollection. I would dare to say that, as she was, she was among the rare who traversed all things with the spirit; and since they were of the spirit's unknown nature, they were forever imprisoned by their desire for clarity. A race among us, condemned to themselves, found no moment of opportunity in this world.

Her childhood I say, even when surrounded by a family supposedly well strung, was a lonely endeavor, spent idle and active, but never bounding. I was a lucky anomaly that she could find solace in; not as I, but as some vessel to her vessel. Doggo was inconsequential; he most likely was to her youth eyes a ransacking ship that occasionally invaded her part of the sea, her territory.

The whole-sameness of her day to day thoughts radiated outwards melancholia when she looked happy, and when in form sad was truly happy.

Though a passing man who exhibited business that exempted him from a familial affection deep, Verstappen procured a limited assortment of tutors: Section Sabarus Revoltoré stayed the longest; and to my companion Night he stayed the shortest. He never boarded her ship and she never let him; never let anyone, at all. She pointed out to me, (now and still Vergilius), that a bowler hat would fit Section: do hear me describe him; he was not without height nor a lack of it, and he was handsome without a gentility, only pedantry.

Nevertheless, for as much exertion she had spent on learning and absorbing arts and habits, she accumulated only nothing of interest to her and confided in me that she would return me to T.G when I had disapproved of her painting spree (I will not detail it, but she had upon seeing a painting bought by Ice that she would venture to that measure of skill. I do not recall seeing her painting for many years after I had disapproved of her attempts).

Growing older made her grow into an even-length sound; no roughness at the start, no rough endings, only a line that rose and fell; she transmuted herself through different means, and that is why unto us we are un-privy to the emotions of her youth. She was given to declarations as she was given talent to things that excited her little to null.

I do not believe we fought often; I was then scared of violence. I was no agent of my own vices, and perhaps as I was of witness to Night's own tribulations to discover her budding authority to the madness to the way things were, I stuck by and watched. She appreciated my company; I the same to hers. Albeit, with this closeness there inhabited a polite distance she held and maintained. She was incapable of closing a distance; and opening a new distance she found difficult.

One day, indoors, her "meadow" still following, she said to me, pushing a pawn forward on a board—"The recognitions of patterns in chess is necessary for the adequate balance in all situations. I can't remember yesterday, but I recognize the patterns. If I can see my patterns I am comforted. In adolescence, as I am now, the soul either wanders or it roots itself. The recognitions...the recognitions. If I pass this pawn of yours I am going to checkmate you in the following sequences. Perhaps I will, perhaps I won't if you play better. I recognize it, but you may not. I want you to see through me so I may as well be an honest ruler."

She said to me after a silence—"I have had no interest in this game: I excel in it. In this game, with my pieces, I rule over a dominion ever fragile. A magister, a king. It is all in a game: a game with glass pieces. The beauty of the game does not come from new boards nor pieces, but from seeing it all with new eyes. Huh? mother is calling. I have to go to bed." She detoured and stood up, knocking the board over.

I did not pick the pieces nor the board up. I admit I was surely confused at what she had said. That, my reader, is why Night was of that race condemned, because unlike you or I, she did not search for time or reality, it had perhaps fallen apart already: the days stretched on for forever, until they did not, and the ways of searching in inconsequential fashion appareled none: perhaps it was not one day, but one-hundred years, spent in the slimmest company, spent in solitude, spent in her own board, her own vessel, intolerant to the troubles, until the troubles became intolerant to that resistance.

I must say; the bowler hat stayed. Not on my head; but whenever she went to anywhere in that sleeping valley town, she would in-company vaguely assign a bowler hat an especial role that of her disquiet apathy gave her a company I perhaps was unable to fulfill, such as Verstappen.

She ditched the one's who wore the bowler hat, and instead had taken the item alone for consolation: with chess, she took it as an item and abandoned whatever else attempted recovery of ground with her whilst in chess; she always sought her own joy, seeking and seeking, from youth onwards: if she ever found it? That is for later.

I had been thoroughly re-assumed in my position as T.G once Section was confirmed to be a tutor who was in residence permanent in her life: my old name had been raised from the dead, or her own sense of dead, and so had my identity alongside it.

I rejected T.G's identity: I kept the name, kept Vergilius as I. She said nothing of it until she told me, over an escapade on a hill—"...You have amputated your limbs and kept your heart: I have amputated your heart and kept your limbs. What's to stop me from saying that I am you now?"

I had no answer to that eerie query, and now even as I write, I am sometimes unable to recognize myself with a bowler hat on a rainy day.

But according to her displeasures, there were the instances in which an involvement of fleeing was certainly on her mind, and I am afraid to note them, for I no nothing of them, only that she had the habits of vanishing and returning.

Night, for all her qualities, may as well have been born as a girl without qualities to my knowledge.

She found that a treachery of time, a treachery of consciousness, so severe it indebted her to fanciful depictions, was an inescapable aspect of her character. Had she cared for Ashes as siblings should one day, she forgot she did the next day.

Alas, the more I try to remember, the more I travel through the avenues and meadows we ventured through, all I find is that although memory stays to us, it lies and deceives involuntarily: whatever truth once lived in anything may pass, and as I realize that truth, memory, and our views, are as fleeting as time itself, failing us and renewing their honesty, until we know next to nothing of the past except a distant after-math.
