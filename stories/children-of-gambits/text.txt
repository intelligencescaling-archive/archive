The city skyline glittered above, a distant constellation of ambition and wealth. But down on the streets, a different story unfolded. Here, in the gridlocked canyons of glass and steel, a river of faces flowed past, each one a testament to a different struggle. The sleek, black car was an island of silence in the river of noise, its window rolled down to let in the thick, city air.

I peered out, my gaze analytical, dissecting the world outside. I watched the stream of pedestrians, noting the scarcity of crisp suits and purposeful strides.

The number of people who move through the streets with suits and purpose has been dwindling for a while now.

My attention shifted to a construction site as the car crept forward. Muscled men, their skin tanned and clothes dusted with concrete, swarmed the steel skeleton of a new tower.

And the number of those engaged in hard labour has increased just as drastically.

I rested my jaw on my knuckles, a habit of deep thought.

Education is the most prized possession in the world today. It's the key that unlocks the gilded doors. And yet, so few manage to turn the lock. It's strange, isn't it? Everyone is given a chance at it, but most fail. A relative of mine, a boy who was practically canonized as a genius by our family, failed so spectacularly it was almost a work of art.

A flicker of something, not quite amusement, but a detached curiosity, passed through me.

It intrigued me. To what end?

The car fell silent again, the only sound the distant blare of a horn. I closed my eyes, and the memory surfaced, as crisp and clear as the day it happened.

---

The vast, dark-wood dining table was a sea of silence. At its head, blurred figures loomed, the architects of the Klein name, their gazes sharp and critical, fixed on the teenager who sat with his head bowed.

Shame radiated from my cousin in palpable waves, thick enough to choke the room.

At the far end of the table, I watched. I didn't feel the shame; I only recorded it. To me, my cousin wasn't a brother or a failure. He was a fascinating, collapsing structure.

Suddenly, a figure detached from the shadows. My mother. She moved with a silent, predatory grace, her perfume arriving seconds before she did, a cloying, floral cloud that felt like a physical weight.

She scooped me up, her touch soft but her grip unyielding, and carried me out into the cool air of the courtyard.

She whispered into my ear. At the time, the words were just sounds, melodic, sorrowful noises meant to soothe. I hadn't understood the vocabulary then, but I had understood the frequency.

Looking back, I thought as the car bumped over a pothole in the present, I realize what she was doing. She wasn't comforting a child; she was rehearsing. She used guilt the way a painter uses color, layering the blues and greys of disappointment until the canvas of my mind was exactly the shade she wanted.

In the memory, I had looked at the gathering storm clouds instead of her face. I had offered her a look of confusion, a mask I had learned to wear before I could even tie my own shoes. It was my first successful experiment.

A moment later, the teenager from the dining room, my cousin, walked past me. His face was a thundercloud of dark emotion, his eyes fixed on some distant point. He didn't see me. He walked with a heavy, defeated gait toward his art space on the other side of the manor, his world shattered.

---

I opened my eyes, the memory dissolving like morning mist. I shifted in my seat, crossing my arms.

Ever since then, I've been curious. If my cousin, with all his supposed genius, could fail so utterly, how would I fare against such a system? Was his good nature his downfall? Perhaps. It'll be a valuable experiment. A good experience for me in this new school.

I stared out the window, the buildings giving way to more manicured lawns and imposing gates.

Failing your era of education is a death sentence in today's society. It's a slow, social death.

"We're here, Mr. Merlio."

The voice pulled me from my thoughts. What had felt like an eternity in the traffic jam had finally ended. I found myself in a vast parking lot, dotted with other sleek, expensive vehicles. "Mr. Merlio…?"

Wells raised a questioning eyebrow.

I clarified, my voice softer than usual. "Your retirement, Sir Wells. I can look after myself now."

The old man's eyes widened, first in shock, then in a wave of warm surprise. A genuine, crinkling smile spread across his face. "Not yet. You're still a young lad." He chuckled. "But thank you for the thought."

I didn't object. I held my stoic pose, but a small, almost imperceptible smile touched my lips. "Thank you."

I paused, observing from the periphery.

"Remember what you're here for," the father continued, tucking the tie with a final, decisive yank. "No unnecessary relationships. No distractions. Focus on the prize."

The boy hardened his face, his expression becoming a perfect mask of determination.

"Yes, sir."

As they turned, the father caught my gaze. His eyes narrowed, a clear dismissal. The son followed his father's lead, his glare just as cold. Caught in the act of observation, I quickly dropped my eyes to my own polished shoes. They walked past, a unit of shared ambition.

A son forged in his father's image. Will he break when the pressure becomes too great? Or will he become something new?

"Number 206!" a woman's voice called from the reception counter.

I stepped forward, placing my numbered slip on the cool surface.

"Administrative Block 5, Room 15."

I nodded and turned to leave but stopped short before a large, ornate mirror on the wall. I stood before it, my reflection staring back. I reached up and gave my already-perfect tie a slight, final adjustment. I examined myself, the crisp lines of my suit, the controlled composure on my face. I nodded in satisfaction.

Then my eyes flickered to the other students milling about. Their outfits were more relaxed, comfortable. A sweater here, a pair of casual trousers there.

Did I overdress?

The thought flickered, unwelcome. I pushed it away with a slight shrug. It was a calculated choice. I would be remembered. I turned and walked toward my next destination.

I sat perfectly still in a chair facing a wide desk. Behind it, a man sat ramrod straight, his chest puffed out, his expression as blank and unyielding as the white walls. On his desk, a small plaque declared him the "Screening Officer."

"Yes."

He pressed. "Is everyone equ—"

"Yes."

My voice cut through his, clear and unwavering. My face remained a mask, devoid of any emotion, any hint of doubt.

The screening officer stared, his eyes narrowing as if trying to peer behind my calm facade. After an age, he leaned back, picked up his stamp, and began methodically pounding it onto the documents, the birth certificate, the middle school results, the application form. Each thump was a final, irrevocable judgment.

When he was done, he slid the stack of papers back across the desk.

"Welcome to the Children of Gambit's Academy."

I gathered the documents, stood, and walked toward the door.

*Is everyone equal?* The question echoed in my mind as my hand touched the cold metal of the handle.

I knew the answer I had given. I knew the answer the school expected. But the truth?

The truth was far more complicated.

And I intended to discover exactly how complicated it could be.

—

I moved with quiet efficiency as I dressed. My deep blue blazer hung over one shoulder, the grey inner shirt already tucked in perfectly. I glanced at my watch: 6:40 AM. My eyes swept the small space, barely furnished, with only a simple table and a single chair. The kitchen section was minimalist to the point of austerity: just the necessary utensils, a few pots, basic cookware. The living area held a portable student bed, and the air conditioner hummed its constant, monotonous drone.

Quite a blank space, I thought, adjusting my collar. I'll have to add a few items to make it feel like home.

I slung my handbag across my shoulder and stepped out.

---

The corridor stretched long and impersonal, lined with identical doors. I punched my password into the keypad beside my door, waited for the confirming beep, and made my way toward the elevator. The student housing complex consisted of three towering seven-story buildings, each housing different facilities. Some wings were tailored to specific positions within the school's rigid hierarchy, a constant reminder that even rest was stratified.

I pressed the button for the ground floor and waited. As the elevator doors began their slow slide closed, a hand shot through the gap. The sensors triggered, and the doors jerked back open with a soft chime.

My eyebrows rose slightly as a girl stepped into view.

She was striking in an austere way. Shoulder-length jet-black hair, ironed straight with military precision. Her blazer was immaculate, the brilliant white of her inner shirt almost glowing against her very pale skin. She carried a cold gaze that swept over me briefly, a sharp contrast to my own warmer light brown complexion.

"Good morning," she offered, her voice neutral.

"Morning."

The elevator descended in comfortable silence, the floors ticking by steadily. Just as we approached the ground level, she spoke without looking at me.

"Aren't you going to ask?"

I remained silent as we stepped out together, walking beside each other toward the reception counter. I could feel her gaze furrowing into the side of my face, but I kept my expression stoic.

Finally, I replied, "Ask?"

Her lips twitched. "...?"

"Now you're the one keeping me silent."

She turned her attention to the receptionist at the counter. My eyes drifted to the bag she carried, the class label matched my own. I also noticed a food flask and several thick books from niche academic authors, their spines unfamiliar.

We reached the counter and placed our student IDs down. My voice carried a note of bored curiosity. "Why are you in the boys' hostel?"

"It's none of your business," she replied coldly.

What...?

I stiffened slightly as the receptionist took my ID and scribbled in her notepad. I kept my tone even. "None of my business, yeah? Then why ask me why I didn't ask?"

A soft chuckle escaped her. "Hehehe."

Quite an eccentric character, I noted.

The receptionist finished, and we headed outside. The sun hit us from a new angle, warming the cool morning air. I tucked my hands in my pockets and stood at the edge of the sidewalk, waiting for the campus bus. The girl followed suit, humming a melodious tune under her breath.

I didn't mind. In fact, I found myself subtly bobbing my head to the rhythm. After a moment, I said, "You came to give your brother food, didn't you?"

She stiffened, turning to look at me slowly. "And what told you that?"

"So I'm right, then." I loosened my tie in a fluid motion, a habit when I was thinking aloud. "It can't be a boyfriend. The school's placement of classes is rigorous, plus, information about hostels and class assignments isn't given out until the first day. Also..." I glanced at her. "It's very early."

She raised her eyebrows in mock admiration. "A bit close. I'm here early because he doesn't want others getting the wrong idea about a girl visiting him. And if it's me..." She shrugged. "They won't stop teasing him."

I shrugged nonchalantly. "Eh, that makes sense."

The bus pulled up with a hydraulic sigh, and we both stepped on.

---

The ride was quiet but far from silent. I observed, as I always did. Students sat in clusters or alone, each revealing visible tells. Some slumped with tired expressions, already defeated by a day that hadn't truly begun. Others sat with proud postures, chests puffed out, chins raised, first-years staking their claim.

It was a normal visual spectrum in any place with people of different backgrounds, but here, the contrasts were sharp enough to cut.

Near the front, a student with exaggeratedly long hair and sharp, angular features sat utterly still. His face was devoid of any emotion, but he radiated a dominant air of confidence and calm, as if the bus and everyone on it existed merely as his audience.

He's definitely the 'your highness' type, I classified silently.

I looked to the other side. A girl with chestnut brown hair and a lean figure animatedly communicated with those around her. Her section of the bus was the only one booming with laughter and giggles, a pocket of warmth in the otherwise chilly morning.

I rested my chin on my palm thoughtfully. Beside me, Gertrude, I'd decided to think of her that way for now, muttered, "What a try-hard."

I glanced at her with a questioning gaze, but before I could respond, the bus arrived at its destination: the lecture sector.

We descended last, having sat at the back. I stood still for a moment as my feet touched the ground, gathering myself.

"You know..." I still didn't look at her. "What's your name, anyway?"

A pause. "... Gertrude Hildegard."

"So, Hildegard?"

"I'd prefer you refer to me as Gertrude."

"I see..."

Gertrude and I made our way toward our respective classes, our paths diverging only slightly.

---

I stopped before a door marked Class B. I pulled it open and stepped inside. Gertrude followed, walking past me without acknowledgment and taking a seat in the third row, where her name was plastered on the back of the chair.

Following suit, I checked each empty desk until I found my own. Also in the third row, but positioned against the wall, where a glass window offered a view of the corridor beyond. I sat, still and observant, my eyes sweeping the room.

Okay. There's 'your highness.' The long-haired boy sat near the window, already projecting that same imperious calm. The social butterfly girl... The chestnut-haired girl from the bus was there, still radiating energy.

I looked behind me. A girl dressed in prep fashion sat with a sweater layered over her long sleeves, chatting with a small clique of friends who seemed to orbit her like planets around a sun.

So there are two social architects? I muttered quietly to myself.

As if on cue, one of the orbiting girls said something that made the leader shoot her a sharp, warning look. The girl immediately fell silent.

I quickly reevaluated. Not just any social butterfly. A mean one.

I turned back to the front just as the lean, friendly girl from the bus, Mia, apparently, was pushing her friend toward the mean girls' clique. Her friend looked hesitant, almost terrified.

"Just go talk to them," Mia urged, nudging her friend forward. "They won't bite."

"I don't know!" her friend whispered desperately. "What should I say? Whatever I say, I'll look uncool!"

Suddenly, Mia's eyes beamed with brilliance. She had an idea.

"Oh, I know!" She stood up and called for the class's attention, her voice bright and carrying. "Good morning, everyone! Since we're still waiting for homeroom to start, why don't we introduce ourselves?"

Her friend hid her face in embarrassment.

I observed with interest. So she's creating an opportunity for everyone to converse and get to know each other. This gives her friend an excuse to talk to the mean girls without it being obvious.

"I'll go first," Mia continued, undeterred. "My name is David Mia. You can call me Mia or Mimi." She beamed.

We will not be calling you that, I thought dryly.

As soon as she finished, the class erupted into motion. Students eagerly took the opportunity to expand their social circles, voices overlapping in a symphony of nervous introductions and forced laughter.

I sighed inwardly. Should I be socializing? It's necessary, I know. But just from the brief interaction I've had today with Gertrude, I'm already a bit exhausted.

I watched as Mia's friend, Pamela, I'd learn, mustered her courage and walked toward the mean girls' clique. She stopped at their desk, her posture awkward.

"Um, yo, what's up!" Pamela raised her hand for what looked like a high-five. "I'm Pamela Williams."

The mean girls stared at her. One of them smirked, a slow, cruel curl of the lips. They exchanged a glance, then turned back to their conversation, completely ignoring Pamela as if she'd never spoken.

"Sheesh," someone muttered from across the room.

A few giggles erupted. Pamela's face crumpled. She bowed her head and retreated as Mia rushed to console her.

I watched the exchange and shook my head slightly. Turns out she did, indeed, bite.

An amused look crept onto my face, not cruel, simply observational. Fascinating. The social order establishes itself before the first lesson even begins.

The door swung open with a sharp click.

A man walked in, dressed in a formal coat, his gaze sharp as a blade as it swept across the room. The chatter died instantly.

"Good morning, everyone. Settle down."

His presence commanded immediate attention. He walked to the front of the class, placed a leather folder on the teacher's desk, and turned to face us.

"My name is Mr. Ashworth. I'll be your homeroom teacher for this year." His eyes scanned the room, lingering for a fraction of a second on certain students, I noticed it lingered on 'your highness,' on the mean girls' leader, and briefly, on me. "Before we begin, I have two announcements."

The room was utterly silent.

"First, welcome to Children of Gambit's Academy. You've survived the entrance screenings. That means nothing. What matters is what you do now." He paused, letting the weight of his words settle. "Second, there will be a placement assessment in three weeks. Your performance will determine your class stratification for the remainder of the semester. Study accordingly."

A ripple of unease passed through the room. I kept my expression neutral, but internally, I was already calculating.

'Three weeks. A placement assessment?' I thought to myself.

I glanced toward Gertrude, who sat perfectly still, her pale face revealing nothing. Then toward 'your highness,' who seemed almost bored by the announcement. Then toward the mean girls, who exchanged confident smirks.

I turned my gaze to the window, watching students hurry past in the corridor beyond. As I fell into a deeper level of thought
The classroom had fallen so quiet that one could hear the soft whisper of their own breathing. I glanced around and noted the skeptical expressions on my classmates' faces, a mixture of suspicion, curiosity, and guarded interest. Mr. Ashworth stood at the front, marker in hand, commanding attention without effort.

"First, I shall explain to you what this is." He wrote on the clean whiteboard in bold, decisive strokes: CREDITS.

My eyebrow rose slightly. Beside me, a boy leaned forward in his chair, curiosity evident in his posture.

"The credit system is very simple. Put simply, it is the school's own form of currency. One credit point equals one euro." Mr. Ashworth paused, letting that sink in. "With your student card, you can purchase items. If it has a price, it can be bought."

The effect was immediate. Students who had been slumped in their chairs straightened. Eyes that had been glazed over sharpened with interest. I spun my pen slowly between my fingers, an idle motion that helped me think.

"Right now, you each currently have 10,000 credits in your accounts. Furthermore, credits will be deposited into your student accounts at the end of every month."

My pen stopped mid-spin. From my peripheral vision, I noticed the boy beside me jotting down figures in a small notebook. I filed that observation away and returned my attention to the front.

Mr. Ashworth gestured to a stack of manuals on the desk. "Pass these to the back." Mia, at the front, took one and passed the stack along. As the manuals reached my row, Mr. Ashworth added, his tone shifting slightly.

"By graduation, your accumulated credits can be carried forward to your next college. The credit system is recognized and used worldwide." He paused, and his expression darkened almost imperceptibly. "However, you cannot transfer credits to a personal bank account. They operate only within the confines of the education system."

His eyes swept the room, sharp as flint. "In other words, credits cannot be used outside of schooling. Some universities even outright refuse to accept credits from other institutions. So be careful."

I squinted slightly, my mind turning over the words. His phrasing is carefully chosen. A bit vague upon closer inspection. There's something he's not saying directly.

"Any questions?" Mr. Ashworth asked.

Silence stretched for several seconds. Then the boy beside me raised his hand.

I took the opportunity to study him properly. I haven't actually taken a good look at this guy yet. But upon closer inspection... He had dark brown hair, neatly combed and well-kept. Flint straightened further. "It seems as if we're being paid to perform better as students. That's a harmful type of motivation—extrinsic. It undermines genuine curiosity and the love of learning for its own sake."

Mr. Ashworth chuckled slowly, a dry, knowing sound. "A rather basic question. However, that's not the case. If you look clearly at things, you'll find the answer to your question."

I furrowed my brows at the teacher's response. He didn't really answer. He deflected.

"Okay, then. Class dismissed." Mr. Ashworth walked toward the door, then paused with his hand on the handle. "And have a wonderful day." His expression was utterly robotic as he said it, a performative pleasantry with no warmth behind it. The door clicked shut behind him.

Silence reigned for a moment longer. Then, almost imperceptibly, murmurs began to ripple through the room.

Some groups were already leaving. The mean girls' clique departed first, their leader casting a dismissive glance back at the room. A few other students followed. Those who remained were scattered, isolated, or deep in conversation with the one or two people they'd already latched onto.

I shook my head with a soft sigh. Somehow, we're already at a disadvantage as a class. Not a single formal introduction from one to another. No class representative. No cohesion. Islands forming before we've even mapped the archipelago.

I stood, tucking my sketchpad away, and walked toward the door. As my hand closed around the handle, I felt a light touch on my shoulder.

I turned. Gertrude stood there, her pale face expressionless but her eyes holding a question.

"Mind if I come with?" she asked.

Examining her for some seconds before I shrugged nonchalantly. "Sure. Why not?"

"Not particularly..."

After a long moment, I muttered, "...Not yet."

I nodded slowly. "Which is bad, because islands can't form a large mass of land. Even when you pull them together, it's still just a cluster of separate islands."

"You mean you want to see how feasible the credit system is?"

"Touché."

The bus arrived with a gentle hiss of brakes, and we stepped aboard together.

---

The Commerce Hub sector was a world unto itself. As the bus rolled to a stop, Gertrude and I stepped off onto a plaza that could have been transplanted from a small city. Towering buildings rose on all sides, their ground floors lined with shops, cafes, and services. Students flowed through the space in a constant current.

"The school is absurdly large," I murmured. "Good thing it has a transportation system."

The campus, I'd learned from the orientation materials, spanned 800,000 square meters. Four sectors: Education, Commerce Hub, Events, and Residential. Each had its own purpose, its own ecosystem. And at the heart of the Commerce Hub stood the largest structure I had yet seen.

The Oval Plaza Mall.

I read the name silently, committing it to memory. Gertrude walked past me without waiting, and after a moment, I tucked my hands in my pockets and followed.

---

Inside, the mall was overwhelming. Light streamed through a massive glass ceiling, illuminating multiple levels of stores, restaurants, and walkways. Escalators crisscrossed the open atrium. The hum of conversation, the beep of register scanners, the distant murmur of music from a store, it all blended into an urban symphony.

I let my gaze wander, momentarily dropping my guard. It feels like a city in here. How many stores are there, anyway? I caught myself staring and quickly reasserted my usual mechanical expression.

Beside me, Gertrude walked like a child in a dream world, her head swiveling, her eyes wide.

"You want to go eat first?" she offered.

I opened my mouth to reply, but Gertrude raised a hand abruptly.

"No! Essentials first!" She snapped her fingers and pivoted, marching in the opposite direction.

I rolled my eyes slowly and followed.

We found a shop that sold home amenities, everything from bedding to kitchenware to toiletries. Gertrude grabbed a basket and dove into the aisles with purpose. I watched her for a moment, noting how naturally she moved through the space, picking up items, examining them, placing some in her basket and others back on shelves.

I looked down at my own empty hands, then at the rows of products stretching before me.

"I'm not sure how to shop," I muttered.

After observing Gertrude a while longer, I grabbed a basket of my own and chose an aisle at random.

So this is shopping, huh?

At first, I simply reached for anything my hands could grasp, a pack of sponges, a bottle of shampoo, a set of utensils, a small lamp. But after a few items, I stopped and looked down at my basket.

That's not right. I should probably get only what I need. I began replacing items, methodically selecting toothpaste, a toothbrush, a small towel, a notebook. I was reaching for a pack of pens when I felt eyes on me.

"You cook?" Gertrude's voice came from directly behind me.

I turned. She stood there, basket in hand, expression blank.

"So, no?"

"Yes."

"Yes, what?"

I deflected. "Are you done with your shopping?"

I raised an eyebrow. I picked up the pack of spoons I'd been considering and checked the price tag. My eyes widened slightly. "Yeah... I wasn't even paying attention to the prices."

"Be more careful. Let's find another store."

We both began returning items to their proper places, weaving through the aisles. As I reached for a shelf to replace a box, I noticed another section I'd missed before. I walked over and examined the products.

Stacked neatly were home goods, each bearing a distinctive label: the school's logo, printed prominently on the packaging. And beneath the logo, in bold red text: FREE.

I picked up a duster. The box was pristine, sealed, with the school's crest embossed on the corner. What's this?

I scanned the shelf. Some items were marked free. Others were fifty percent off. Others still were discounted by varying percentages, but all bore the school's branding.

Gertrude appeared beside me. "What are you doing?"

"...Nothing. Just curious about the existence of these."

She took a product off the shelf and examined it. "Free?"

I nodded. "Not all, though. This one's fifty percent off. Some are just—"

"Free," she completed.

"Mhmm. And the school's logo is on each package."

Gertrude rubbed her chin thoughtfully, her brow furrowing. "Maybe the school partners with certain brands to offset inflated pricing?" She was thinking aloud now, working through it. "Shop owners could inflate prices and disrupt the school's economic balance. I watched her reason it out, saying nothing. I found myself impressed by the speed of her deductions.

After a moment, I carefully placed the products back on the shelf and arranged them neatly, as if they'd never been touched.

"The day's running out," I said. "How about that meal now?"

Gertrude looked up from her thoughts, meeting my monotonous expression with one of her own. "Sure. Why not."

---

Finally, Gertrude spoke. "You're not what I expected."

I looked up from my sandwich. "What did you expect?"

Outside the window, the sun continued its arc across the sky. Students came and went, laughing, arguing, studying, living. And somewhere in the depths of the mall, the credit system hummed along, tracking every purchase, every transaction, every choice.

I finished my sandwich and wiped my hands on a napkin. Islands, I thought. We're all islands. But maybe... maybe some islands can form archipelagos.

I glanced at Gertrude, who was staring out the window with an unreadable expression.

Maybe.

The day had turned gloomy, the sky a heavy canvas of grey that threatened to release its burden at any moment. "Offer people variety. Don't just give thorough explanations, give them reasons to listen. Throw in a joke. A relatable experience. A challenge." I turned to leave, then paused. "You'll need this. Not just as a tutor. But as the leader you plan to become."

I walked away, leaving her alone with her thoughts.

When she returned to the table and took her seat, a small smirk played on her lips.

I noticed the shift immediately, the change in her energy, her posture, her presence.

She caught my eye and pointed at her chest, then raised her voice.

"Who wants food? I'm exhausted and hungry from all this teaching."

Several students looked up, surprised by the sudden shift in tone.

I raised my hand lazily.

Gertrude's smirk widened. "I'll also pay for anyone's meal!"

Flint's head snapped toward her. So did several others.

"Pay for our meal?" someone echoed.

"Well—" Gertrude held up a finger. "What I *meant* to say is: I'll pay for the meal of anyone who can answer a question related to today's topic!"

Christa's hand shot up. "Quartiles always come in four, right?"

Suddenly, students who had migrated to Flint's table were reconsidering. Two tutors meant two chances at a free meal. The dynamic had shifted.

I sat back, watching with quiet satisfaction. My expression betrayed nothing, but internally, I approved.

*Good. She's learning.*

A small, private thought followed:

*I could have won that meal for myself, though.*

Later, as the session wound down and students filtered out of the café in ones and twos, I found myself alone at the table with Flint.

The other boy was packing his materials with methodical precision, each notebook aligned, each pen capped and returned to its proper place. He didn't look up when he spoke.

"I was called by Flora."

He walked out without looking back.

The café door swung shut behind him, the little bell above it chiming softly.

I sat alone at the table, my expression unchanged. Several minutes passed, or perhaps only one. Time felt elastic in the sudden quiet.

Finally, I stood. My movements were unhurried, deliberate. I gathered my things with the same methodical precision Flint had displayed.

As I walked toward the door, a single thought surfaced, flat, practical, almost mundane.

*I should get something to eat.*

I pushed open the door and stepped out into the afternoon sun, leaving the weight of Flint's words behind.

Or trying to.

"Good afternoon, Sir Ashworth."

His eyes narrowed, suspicion flickering behind them.

I could almost hear his thoughts. *What's wrong with this student? Already losing faith that he'll fail tomorrow's test? How... endearing.*

Aloud, he said, "Okay, then. What about the next upcoming test?"

I placed my hands in my pockets, a casual gesture that I knew conveyed something else entirely. "You never told us about contracts, did you, Mr. Ashworth?"

"You didn't."

He got me figured out, I imagined him thinking. A little slip. A crack in the facade. And I saw it.

But before he could dwell further, I continued.

"However. My terms are simple: I'll ace the test. And for every wrong answer, I'll pay 1,000 credits."

Ashworth's skepticism returned. I could see him calculating behind his eyes, running the numbers.

He's desperate. Instead of focusing on tomorrow's test, he's trying to leverage the time window, buy himself space to study for the next one. But if that's the case, he'll need to develop strong study habits quickly. His mock test score placed him eighth, which isn't a testament to academic strength so much as surrounding mediocrity.

He leaned back, and I watched the assessment continue across his features.

Either way, I benefit. If he somehow passes the next test, I have a capable student in my class, and the assessments only get harder. But if he fails... A small smile tugged at his lips. The school still benefits. Credits circulate back into the system. Students have 10,000 credits total and a total of fifty questions. If he fails close to five questions, his total credit will plummet significantly.

His eyes glittered with calculation.

Even if he studies, the tests increase in difficulty exponentially. He can't magically close that gap. He'll fail at least three questions. Minimum.

Ashworth chuckled lightly, the sound dry and knowing. "I see no issues."

I stared at him with an expression that seemed to peer past flesh and bone, into the very mechanisms of his thoughts. After a long moment, I replied, "Alright, then."

"My price?"

He leaned forward slowly. "And you think I'll place a price on this because...?"

He let the sentence hang, unfinished.

We regarded each other across the desk, reading between lines that hadn't been written. A lock. A standoff.

Finally, he spoke. "Give me 6,000 credits, and I'll think about it."

For the first time, something flickered in my eyes, a slight widening, quickly controlled.

"That's cruel, sir."

He nodded slowly.

Good, I thought. He's underestimated my academic capabilities. Either that, or the next test is harder than I anticipated. Not a problem for me.

I mirrored his nod and stood, walking toward the door. My hand hovered over the handle.

"And yes, Merlio."

I turned, my expression monotonous. "Hmm?"

"Don't disappoint me."

I stood there for several seconds, as if processing the weight of the words. Finally, I replied:

"Hopefully."

The door clicked shut behind me.

Alone in the office, I imagined Mr. Ashworth staring at the closed door for a long moment, then looking down at the contract, my signature fresh on the page.

He would file the document away and return to his work, but the encounter would linger at the edges of his mind.

Rain.

It fell outside the classroom windows in steady sheets, each drop tapping against the glass like a thousand tiny reminders of the weight pressing down on us. The sky was a uniform grey, depressing, suffocating, appropriate.

Beside me, Flint sat uncomfortably in his chair, shifting every few minutes as if he couldn't find a position that eased whatever was coiling inside him. The classroom was filled with heavy silence and tension, broken only by the scratch of pens and the occasional nervous cough.

I glanced around discreetly. Some students stared at their test papers with expressions of utter confusion, eyebrows furrowed, mouths slightly open, as if the questions were written in a language they'd never seen. Others had already given up, their pens resting on desks, their gazes fixed on nothing.

A drop of sweat rolled down the side of my head.

I felt Mr. Ashworth's gaze on me, daring, probing, watching my every side glance. I waved my hand slightly, a small acknowledgment, and returned my attention to the paper.

Tess Oberlin. One of Mia's circle. Pamela's close friend. She was whispering something to herself, encouragement, probably. Or despair.

"Mr. Merlio!"

Mr. Ashworth's voice cut through the quiet like a blade. I looked up.

"Sorry, sir. It won't happen again."

He held my gaze for a moment longer than necessary, then returned to monitoring the room.

I looked down at my test sheet.

Fifty questions. From a single glance, you could tell this wasn't just an assessment, it was a filtration system.

Twenty-five questions were straightforward: simultaneous equations, definitions of economic terms, lists of importance. The kind of questions that separated those who paid attention from those who didn't.

The other twenty-five were something else entirely. Complex questions disguised as puzzles, woven together from multiple subjects. They didn't test memorization, they tested whether you could think. Whether you could see connections where others saw only confusion.

Attempting those questions meant you possessed at least a general but deep understanding of multiple disciplines. Not mastery, but synthesis. The ability to weave knowledge together.

This further solidified my theory about the school's approach. They weren't testing what we knew. They were testing how we thought.

I worked through the questions methodically. By the time I reached question forty, I heard the shuffle of papers from the front.

Hanman Durant was standing, his completed test in hand. Mr. Ashworth glanced over it briefly, nodded, and placed it on his desk. Hanman left without a backward glance, his footsteps echoing in the silent corridor.

I finished my remaining questions but didn't move.

Instead, I sat still, pen in hand, staring at my paper as if deep in thought. As if I had no idea what else to write.

The reason was simple: there was a time limit. One hour and thirty minutes. And I wanted to avoid any questions about why I'd finished so early.

So I waited.

Twenty-five minutes left on the clock.

Fifteen minutes.

"Fifteen minutes remaining!" Mr. Ashworth's voice cut through the room like a starting pistol.

Panic rippled through the class. Students who had been frozen in indecision suddenly scrambled. The rule was clear: once he left the classroom, no more papers would be accepted.

Everyone rushed to place their sheets on the stack at his desk.

I slipped into the current of bodies and added mine to the pile.

Stepping back into the hallway, I let out a breath I hadn't realized I'd been holding.

*What an experience, I thought.

The hallway stretched endlessly in both directions, or at least, that's how it must have felt for some of my classmates. I stood by the vending machine, a can of popping grapes in my hand, watching the flow of students like a river of varying emotions.

Dorian walked past me. His expression stopped me cold, soul-crushing, defeated, hollow. The kind of look that said more than words ever could.

He hesitated.

I watched him closely.

It didn't go well.

My mind pointed out the obvious, and I let it.

We stood there together, two figures in the flow of students passing by. Some wore expressions of shared disdain toward the test's difficulty. Others clutched something else, false hope, perhaps. Or was it the opium of faith? That dangerous belief that your abilities would be enough in an environment designed to prove they weren't.

Either way, it soured the mood of what had once been vibrant hallways.

I made my way toward her.

I sighed.

Seconds passed. Then a minute. She didn't speak.

Finally, I sat down next to her.

"Gertrude?"

Instead of replying, she rubbed her temples. The gesture was heavy, weighted with something deeper than test anxiety.

Her stress was visible, but it wasn't the test. It was something heavier. Something relational.

I pieced it together silently. If the test had been difficult for her, and I suspected it hadn't been, that wasn't the source. The source was the people she'd taught. The students who had looked to her for guidance.

If they failed, she would become someone to blame.

It sounded unrealistic on the surface. But yes, something like that could happen. Would happen. In an environment this competitive, blame flowed downhill, and tutors were convenient targets.

I watched her profile as she stared at the field, the evening light beginning to fade.

What I'm concerned with, I thought, is whether she understands that there are three possible outcomes arranged before her. Three paths. Three futures.

Gertrude, which are you going to choose?

The thought hammered in my head as the illuminance of the field's bright lights flickered to life, pushing back the evening darkness.

Tomorrow, our class's fate would be decided.

The rain had stopped, but the clouds remained.

Too much effort.

"So you're friends with Gertrude?"

"..."

"..."

We both spoke simultaneously: "You go first."

"..."

I went quiet before I spoke again. "I think so. We haven't declared ourselves as friends, though. Probably because we were acquainted before the first meeting in class."

Caspian nodded in understanding. "Not friends yet, gotcha. Many would think the same 'cause you guys hang around a lot."

"Yeah, I get that. And you aren't the only one who's asked about that."

Caspian dug his hands into his pockets. "You want some ice cream?"

I waved my hands. "I don't think I can accept tha—"

"Relax, it's on me," he said. "Oh yeah, we just walked past some vanilla-flavored cookies. I'm a sucker for those."

He rubbed his head. "You know what? How about you go buy the ice cream ahead, and I'll go back? I'll pay you back."

I nodded. "Sure."

Since credit couldn't be withdrawn, I would have to purchase it with my student card.

We went our separate ways temporarily. My goal was to buy ice cream. And I had to admit, I wasn't a fan of sweets. I preferred something mundane on my taste buds. I was a boring man.

After purchasing two cups of ice cream, I made my way back. But I decided to cut my path short and went another way.

As I walked down the stairs, I noticed this pathway was desolate. I wondered if I might get mugged, but I had no worries. About 89% of the people on this campus were students.

"Who the hell does that bitch think she is?"

I froze. The soles of my shoes made a soft click against the pavement.

Just ahead was a girl. The darkness made it hard to make out who she was, and her back was facing me as she punched the wall.

"What a group of fucking prep queens, thinking they're above everyone else, huh?"

"Interesting," I said to myself from my hidden spot.

"Everyone in that classroom thinks they're some kind of hotshot. But none more annoying than that slut Emilia, Gertrude, and Christa!"

I dropped the cups of ice cream on the floor and peeked to see who it was. Upon closer observation, I heard another digitalized voice.

"I understand. Is there anything else you want to share, dear? I could have them expelled."

My eyes narrowed at the word: *expulsion.*

Then the girl turned. Her shoulder-length hair and thin frame. There was no doubt.

It was Mia.

Suddenly, my phone started vibrating. She snapped her attention to my direction with pinpoint accuracy. I cut the incoming call and tapped on the recording app.

"Who the hell is there?" she demanded. "Come out, you dipshit!"

"No need to raise your voice, Mia." I stepped forward, my monotonous voice and expression a stark contrast to hers.

Her face was a mess of makeup, her big playful eyes now filled with deep resentment directed at me. Her hair was disheveled, and the knuckles on her fist were bleeding profusely.

"It's just me—"

"C'mere." Her voice was playful again.

I was amazed by how quickly she could change her high-pitched tone. I walked down the stairs toward her.

Standing in front of me, she brought her hand forward. "Give it here."

"Why should I?"

"Give me the damn phone, Merlio." Her voice was calm now.

"Tell me, who's on the line, Mia?"

"You're not in the position to ask questions."

I gave her the phone. She swiped it from my grasp and told me to take a step back. After that, she threw my phone back to me. I caught it without a sweat, my gaze still fixed on her.

She arranged herself, dusted her blazer, and wiped off her makeup. Then, without warning, she leaned in very close.

"You heard that, didn't you?"

"If I didn't, then why ask for my phone?"

"That's not what I said. But the voice on my own phone?"

"..."

She leaned closer, now on her tiptoes, her breath brushing against my ear.

"Be careful. Unless I might have your little friend expelled."

She took a step back. "I don't mind you telling others what you heard about me. However, I'll do anything in my power to drag the ones I dislike down with me."

Her friendly voice was back on now, as if a switch had been flipped. It was like watching a magic trick, how she did it was unsettling.

"Please... don't do anything rash," I asked, desperation lacing my tone.

"Hmmm... Be a good boy, and I won't."

I stood and watched her turn and walk to the opposite part of the alleyway. Her figure disappeared as she made a turn to the right.

I looked up at the wall she had been punching earlier. A small dent was fractured into it, blood splattered around it.

I looked down at the phone call from earlier and saw Caspian's name on the missed calls list.

A sudden gust of wind blew.

"It's come to this, huh?"

---

I picked up the fallen cups of ice cream, both now melted, ruined,and tossed them into a nearby bin.

Mia's words echoed in my head. "I'll do anything in my power to drag the ones I dislike down with me."

The bubbly, enthusiastic girl who had proposed the study group. The social butterfly who had seemed so harmless.

A mask, I realized. She's been wearing a mask this entire time.

And now I had seen what lay beneath.

I pulled out my phone and stared at the recording app. I hadn't pressed stop in time, or had I? The screen showed a blank recording. She had deleted it before throwing the phone back.

Clever.

She hadn't just taken my phone to delete evidence. She had taken it to see if I had recorded anything. And she had found nothing, because I hadn't started recording until after she had already seen me.

Or had I?

I checked the files. There it was. A recording, but only of the conversation after she had taken my phone. The part where she threatened me. The part where her mask slipped further.

I deleted it.

Not because I was afraid of her. But because a recording of her threatening me wouldn't help anyone. It would only escalate things.

And Mia, for all her venom, had just shown me exactly who she was. Or a part of herself?

And even more of a mystery. Who's the one behind the phone?

So many questions.

Forewarned is forearmed.

I pocketed my phone and walked back toward the main path, where Caspian was probably waiting with his vanilla cookies, completely unaware of what had just transpired.
