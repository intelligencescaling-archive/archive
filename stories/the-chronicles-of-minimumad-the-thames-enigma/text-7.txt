Chronicles of MinimumAd: The Thames Enigma

# Chapter 8: The Roofs of London

The morning fog had thinned by the time *MinimumAd* and *Sieben* stepped out from the dim shadow of Trueluck’s apartment. The street was waking, horses clopping past with yawning drivers, milk carts rattling, and the sharp calls of a newsboy echoing like a church bell in the cold London air.

“*Evening Standard!* Big fire at the docks! Warehouse gone up like a bloody lantern! Fire at Smith Inc—!”

Minimum, by habit more than intent, plucked a copy from the boy’s stack and flipped it open. His eyes caught the bold headline—and then he laughed, a quiet, dry chuckle.

Sieben raised an eyebrow. “What’s so funny?”

Minimum handed over the paper, still smiling to himself.

**“WAREHOUSE DESTROYED.”**

The article was curt: *Late last night, a fire broke out in the dockside warehouse of Smith Incorporated. Authorities report no casualties and claim that no valuable goods were stored within. The cause remains undetermined, but early suspicion hints at arson. Smith Inc. has reported substantial financial losses.*

Sieben frowned. “So someone torched it?”

“They always torch the stage once the performance is over,” Minimum muttered. He stepped forward, coat flaring behind him as they continued their walk.

Sieben turned the page. “But why do you think this *Xamot* isn’t Trueluck’s friend? Couldn’t the card have belonged to him?”

Minimum gave a sideways glance. “That room had already been searched. Someone had ransacked it—but *they* didn’t find the card. It wasn’t for Trueluck. It was left for *me*.”

Sieben blinked. “You mean—”

“A message,” Minimum said, his voice quiet. “A subtle one. This game is being played by someone sharp. Someone who takes risks. Xamot isn’t the one I’m after… but he’s standing near enough to the flames to know who lit the match.”

The lobby of Free Huckleberry *& Co.* was marble and muted voices, polished shoes and cautious nods. Minimum walked in like he owned the place, Sieben trailing a few steps behind with mild irritation at the extravagance of it all.

They approached the front desk. The receptionist, a middle-aged man with the tired eyes of someone too often lied to, straightened at the sight of them.

“We’re here to see Mr. Xamot,” Minimum said, already scanning the people beyond the glass.

“I’m afraid Mr. Xamot isn’t available today,” the man replied politely. “He returned from a business trip just yesterday and requested the day off.”

Minimum smiled, far too pleasantly. “Of course. Just one thing, if you’d be so kind. His residential address?”

The receptionist hesitated, but a flash of something in Minimum’s look—calm, yet unmistakably sharp—led him to check the book anyway.

“Thirteen, Dover Street.”

Minimum tipped his hat. “Much obliged.”

The cab ride to Dover Street was brief and uneventful, except for Sieben reading the address aloud like it was a spell about to summon trouble.

The building was tall, narrow, and sandwiched tightly between two forgettable grey structures. They climbed the stairs, footsteps echoing up the wooden steps, and stopped at a plain brown door.

Minimum knocked.

A thud came from inside—rushed, panicked movement.

Then silence.

Minimum stepped back and crouched by the lock. “He’s either bolting the door or climbing out the window. Let’s—”

*CRACK.*

The door exploded inward as Sieben’s boot struck it hard.

Minimum blinked. “...And we could do it *that* way.”

Sieben was already charging inside, scanning the room with the same intensity he reserved for lectures and chessboards. Minimum entered more slowly, eyes sweeping the floor.

“Window,” he said, pointing.

The curtains fluttered in the breeze.

He stepped up to the sill—and there, just vanishing behind the edge of the rooftop across the alley, a figure in a black coat dashed like a shadow fleeing dawn.

Minimum climbed up in one fluid movement, leaping onto the rooftop with the practiced grace of a man who’d done this far too many times.

The chase was on.

Boots thudded over clay tiles and chimneys. The man ahead—lean, fast, desperate—vaulted over a gap to the next rooftop. Minimum followed without pause, eyes locked on the prize.

Beneath them, London blurred—an endless sea of smoke, spires, and cobbled veins of street. But up here, it was all wind and adrenaline.

And from a nearby rooftop, sitting like a queen at court, *Dark* watched.

Her legs were crossed, her smirk curled, and her gloved hand twirled a silver ring between her fingers.

“Oh, Minimum,” she whispered, amused. “Still playing catch with ghosts.”

She stood and began walking along the slanted rooftop’s edge, keeping pace like a waltzing spectator in a violent ballet.
