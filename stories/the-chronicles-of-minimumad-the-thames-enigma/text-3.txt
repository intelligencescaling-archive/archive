Chronicles of MinimumAd: The Thames Enigma

# Chapter 3: A Gentleman's Visit

The fire had long since gone out, leaving behind only the ghostly crackle of cooling embers. SiebenGuts sat cross-legged on the faded rug, a fresh cup of tea now in hand, steam curling up into the shadows of the dimly lit apartment. Outside, the rain had returned, drumming lightly against the panes with the persistence of a ticking clock. Across from him, MinimumAd sat hunched over a thick manila file labeled TRUELUCK, I.

For a while, they sat in comfortable silence—Minimum’s eyes flicking through reports and forensic photographs, Sieben occasionally jotting a note or two on the margins of a notepad that wasn't his.

Eventually, Sieben broke the quiet. “Minimum, why *me*? Why bring me to a crime scene when you could’ve easily gone alone or called one of your many... eccentric acquaintances?”

Minimum didn’t look up from the file. “I’ve already stated the reason.”

“You said it was for the thrill.”

“Yes,” Minimum said flatly, flipping a page with surgical precision. “And also because I needed someone who *doesn't think like a detective.* Someone who sees *wounds*, not *weapons.* Who sees *intention* in incisions.”

Sieben tilted his head. “You think I can see something you can’t?”

“No,” Minimum said finally looking up, eyes sharp as glass. “I think you’ll see something I’ll *miss*.”

Sieben blinked, caught between insult and flattery. Minimum was already back to scanning the document. He paused at a particular section and narrowed his eyes. “Hmm. Look at this, Sieben.”

Sieben scooted closer, peering over his shoulder.

“TrueLuck had a consistent pattern of court attendance and consultancy gigs for shady firms. Here—three months ago, he was a legal consultant for ‘Smith Inc.’… yet that firm doesn’t *exist* on any public registry. Not Companies House, not international databases. Ghost company.”

Sieben frowned. “Front for something?”

“Possibly. Now,” Minimum closed the file, standing up so suddenly the tea in Sieben’s cup rippled. “Excuse me.”

Without another word, he vanished into his room, the old door creaking shut behind him. Muffled rummaging could be heard, followed by the strange sound of metal clinking and a low hum of satisfaction.

When the door opened again, Sieben nearly dropped his cup.

Minimum stood there dressed in faded overalls, a heavy utility belt slung around his hips, a wrench in one hand and a red toolbox in the other. His hair was slicked back, and a smudge of grease adorned one cheek. He looked completely out of place—and entirely *convincing.*

“…You’re a plumber now?” Sieben said slowly.

Minimum gave a half-smile, walking toward the door. “Smith Inc. has an old warehouse address listed under a decommissioned boiler repair invoice. I doubt anyone answers to lawyers at the front door.”

Sieben raised an eyebrow. “And you’re expecting someone to let in a boiler technician?”

Minimum shrugged on a heavy waterproof coat. “It’s either that or breaking and entering. This way, if I’m caught, I get tea and polite questioning instead of handcuffs.”

He opened the door, then paused. “If I’m not back in three hours, call Ifteaz. Tell her to check the old Thames Water files for the Shoreditch grid. And Sieben—” he looked over his shoulder, suddenly serious. “Keep the file open. Look for patterns in the autopsy notes. Cut angles, entry points. Something’s off about the body—too clean. As if someone knew *exactly* where to slice.”

And with that, the detective-turned-plumber vanished into the fog.

Sieben sat alone for a moment, the rain outside intensifying. He turned to the file, brow furrowed, flipping back to the photo of the severed hand.

“Too clean…” he whispered. “As if by a surgeon.”

His own pulse quickened. A strange, dreadful familiarity gnawed at the edges of his mind.

The rain showed no signs of ceasing. A gray curtain hung over London, thick and unrelenting, blurring gas lamps and dampening the distant clatter of hooves. Inside the apartment, Dr. SiebenGuts hunched over the kitchen table, the file on TrueLuck now splayed wide across it like a post-mortem puzzle. The autopsy diagrams, sketched with eerie precision, showed lacerations that were *surgical*, deliberate—nothing like the messy improvisation of a street thug.

He had begun to sketch something on the back of a ledger sheet—an overlay of the cut angles and muscle fiber tears—when a soft *knock* pulled him from his reverie.

Three precise raps.

Sieben blinked, adjusting to reality, and walked over to the door, tea still in one hand, pencil tucked behind his ear. He opened it to find the landlady, Creation Cawthon, standing in the hallway, a shawl clutched tightly about her shoulders.

“Evenin’, Doctor,” she said with her usual clipped kindness. “Forgive the disturbance. This gentleman here says he has business with Mr. Ad.”

Behind her stood a man whose presence immediately unsettled Sieben. The visitor was in his late thirties, impeccably dressed in a dark frock coat, the collar of his white shirt neatly pinned beneath a silk cravat. His hair was combed back with almost mathematical precision, and his umbrella—tipped in ivory—was utterly untouched by the rain. He stood like a man who *knew* he didn’t need an introduction.

Sieben blinked. The stranger smiled faintly, yet something about the way his eyes flicked over Sieben made his skin crawl. There was intelligence in those eyes—not the excitable, curious kind MinimumAd had—but a cold, architectural sort. Like he wasn’t looking *at* Sieben, but *through* him.

“Mr. Ad is not home at the moment,” Sieben said, stepping slightly into the doorway.

The man’s expression didn’t shift much, but there was a subtle downturn in his mouth—an expertly measured disappointment. “Ah. A pity,” he said softly, his voice smooth as silk but with a resonance that hinted at tightly controlled power. “I was hoping for a word. Professional interest.”

“I can tell him you came by,” Sieben offered, still wary.

The man gave a short, thoughtful nod. “That would be most kind.”

With a slow, practiced motion, he pulled a card from an inner pocket and handed it to Sieben.

**Far Transition**
**Department of Physics, University of Oxford**

Sieben took the card with a frown. “Physics?” he said aloud before he could stop himself. “And you’re here for…?”

Far Transition allowed the faintest glimmer of amusement to pass across his features. “Mr. Ad and I… share certain curiosities. He and I have intersected in *other* contexts. Academic. Philosophical. Perhaps even *theatrical*, depending on how you frame things.”

The phrasing unsettled Sieben more than he cared to admit.

“Well,” Far Transition said, offering a slight bow. “I’ll be around. Thank you for your time, Doctor Guts.”

Sieben blinked again. “How do you—?”

But the man was already turning, walking down the stairs with the grace of someone descending an opera stage. He left no trace of his presence—no echo, no scent, not even the expected tap of his umbrella. Just the echo of his words and the clean, cream-colored card in Sieben’s hand.

As the door clicked shut, Sieben stared at the card. *Far Transition*. An odd name—deliberate, perhaps. A pseudonym? A message?

The name gnawed at the edges of his thoughts. And something about the way the man looked at him… he had seen that before. In the eyes of surgical instructors who enjoyed making students sweat.

Sieben returned to the table and sat down, feeling suddenly watched even though he was alone. He flipped the card over. Blank.

He looked at the sketches again—the surgical cuts. Too precise. Too… familiar.

“Minimum,” he muttered aloud, “what have you gotten us into?”

Outside, thunder cracked faintly over the Thames.
