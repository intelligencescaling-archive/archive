# Chapter 9: A Room of Shadows

The apartment was quiet again, but this time, with a different kind of stillness. Not the calm before a storm, but the eerie void after something irreversible.

Minimum and Sieben returned to their flat just before midnight. The lamps flickered faintly, casting long shadows over piles of opened folders and photographs that blanketed their cramped study table. Outside, the fog had thickened. London’s gaslights flickered like uncertain thoughts in a tired mind.

Sieben dropped his coat on a chair and immediately sat down, pulling over stacks of documents—case files, newsprint clippings, a map of London with places marked in red ink. The room filled with the soft rustle of turning pages.

Minimum placed the letter from Xamot under a magnifying glass and switched on the small desk light. The words "Thames Bridge Station" glared back at him. He scanned for fingerprints, checking the creases, the fold lines, the smudges on the edge of the envelope. Nothing.

No oils. No trace.

He sniffed lightly—no scent of cologne or smoke. No clue.

He checked the writing—mechanically typed, uniform ink. The backspace marks were faint. Still nothing.

“Huh,” Minimum muttered, sitting back.

Sieben looked up. “What?”

“No prints. No distinguishing handwriting. Whoever sent this knew what they were doing.”

Sieben raised an eyebrow. “Could’ve used gloves. Or wiped it.”

“No,” Minimum said. “This wasn’t wiped. It was *cleaned*. From the start. Like it came from a lab.”

He sighed. “It’s too perfect.”

Sieben threw a pen on the table and leaned back in his chair. “Then we’re chasing ghosts.”

“No,” Minimum said quietly. “We’re not chasing ghosts. We’re chasing people who pretend to be.”

Across town, in the academic hush of Oxford, a very different evening played out.

FarTransition opened the door to his townhouse, eyes weary from the post office walk. But before he could step in fully—

***SWOOSH!***

A metal bucket swung from the top of the doorframe, nearly colliding with his forehead. He jerked back and caught it in time, grumbling.

“Bloody idiot,” he muttered.

From the top of the stairs came laughter.

“Too slow, old man!”

FarTransition looked up to see DeletinRedditSoon descending the staircase with the energy of a child and the smirk of a menace. “One day I’m going to fill that bucket with paint.”

FarTransition frowned. “And one day I’ll replace your shampoo with nitric acid.”

“That’s the spirit,” Deletin beamed.

In the drawing room, EnvironmentNo6525 sat by the fireplace, sipping tea, flipping through a botanical journal as if the house weren’t hosting semi-organized chaos.

“Did he enter as planned?” FarTransition asked, hanging up his coat.

Environment nodded. “Yes, and more. He’s gone through everything we predicted. Xamot, the warehouse, even the roof chase. He’s… elegant, in a clumsy sort of way.”

Deletin flopped onto a couch, tossing a walnut in the air and catching it. “He’s a dunce. A clever dunce, but still a dunce.”

“He’s better than you were at his age,” came a voice from the shadows.

Dark appeared near the back wall, uncoiling from silence like a whisper taking form. Beside her was a woman with a coat darker than dusk and a stare that gave doors second thoughts before creaking open.

Night Dozor.

“Finally decided to show up, you lazy bum,” Environment greeted her.

“I’m not lazy,” Night replied with the patience of someone who had argued this before and would argue it again.

“Oh, you most definitely are,” Deletin grinned.

Night walked past him with minimal eye contact and maximum indifference.

FarTransition glanced at Night. “Xamot?”

“Done. As per your instructions,” Night said. “Slipped between ribs, through the heart. No noise. High-caliber subsonic. Minimal residue.”

“Did Minimum speak to him?”

“He did,” Night nodded. “Got the letter. Read it outside the apartment. Perfect pacing.”

“He’s moving faster than I thought,” Environment mused.

Dark’s lips curled into a smile. “The doctor’s fun. I like watching him work.”

“You say that about everyone you tail,” Deletin quipped.

“No,” Dark’s tone was as sharp as it was soft. “Most fizzle out on page two. Minimum’s still interesting by chapter six.”

The group laughed, in their own subdued way. These weren’t just shadow-dwellers or game-masters. They were brothers—siblings by blood, madness, or ideology. A strange family, laced together by precision and patience, playing a game older than themselves.

Deletin, never one to waste a moment—or a newspaper—grabbed a crayon and started sketching strange spirals in the margins. “When do we tell him?”

“We don’t,” FarTransition said simply. “Not until the final piece is on the board.”

“And Dark?” Environment asked.

“I’ll keep tailing him,” she said. “See if he notices.”

“He won’t,” Night muttered. “Not yet.”

“Everyone gets noticed eventually,” Dark replied, her eyes on the window, watching raindrops gather like thoughts about to fall.

FarTransition walked to the table, unrolled a map of London, and placed a small red marker on Thames Bridge Station.

“We've set the fire,” he said. “Now we wait for him to burn through the lies.”

Back at the apartment, Minimum stood staring at the map on the wall. Lines crisscrossed in red from Trueluck’s home, to the warehouse, to Dover Street.

“You think he was telling the truth?” Sieben asked, not looking up.

“Yes,” Minimum replied. “He was scared. But not of us. Of someone else. Someone bigger.”

“Who?”

“That’s what I intend to find out.”

He looked back at the letter again.

Not a letter anymore. A lure. A script. And he was meant to follow.
