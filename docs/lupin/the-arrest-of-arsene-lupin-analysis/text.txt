Analysis on Lupin's actions during "The arrest of Arsene Lupin”

OVERVIEW:
Before analyzing the individual moves, I'll give an overview of the full operational structure of what Lupin has designed. Lupin is attempting to transport himself from Europe to America while carrying stolen goods, evading a known and skilled opponent (Ganimard), aboard a closed vessel where he cannot escape if identified. The structural problem is severe: on a ship, there is nowhere to run. The constraints are maximal. And yet Lupin turns this closed environment, which should be his greatest vulnerability,  into a controlled theater that he directs from beginning to end.
The operation has the following layers, ordered from the most visible to the most concealed:

Visible Layer (what everyone sees):
A mysterious telegram announces that Arsène Lupin is aboard. Suspicion falls on a passenger named Rozaine. Thefts occur. Rozaine is arrested, then released. Rozaine is assaulted. Terror spreads. At arrival, a man named d'Andrézy is arrested by Ganimard and identified as Lupin.

Operational Layer:
(what actually happens): Lupin, traveling as d'Andrézy, commits the thefts, engineers suspicion toward Rozaine, robs Rozaine, hides the evidence inside a camera, and transfers the camera to Miss Nelly just before his arrest. He is apprehended without material evidence.

Strategic Layer (the pre-voyage design):
Before boarding, Lupin planted a false intelligence clue directing the wireless telegram to point at an "R" name, knowing this would generate exactly the investigative dynamics he needed.

The Pre-Voyage Operation/The False narrative from Havre:
Ganimard's words at the arrest reveal (that happened at the end of the story) was "Yes, another of your tricks; a false scent that deceived them at Havre." This line tells us that the wireless telegraph message (the one at the beginning of the story that arrives dramatically in the storm, announcing that Lupin is aboard under an "R" name) was designed and planted by Lupin himself. He crafted the information before boarding. He ensured that the authorities at Havre would transmit exactly that message. So thinking about this operationally this means that Lupin, before setting foot on the La Provence, had already scripted the first three chapters of the voyage. He knew:

That the telegram would be sent:
He either arranged the tip himself, through an intermediary, or engineered a situation at Havre that would cause the intelligence services to form exactly this conclusion. The message points to a blonde man, traveling alone, with a wound on his right forearm, whose name begins with R. Now I do believe that Rozaine (the man who will get used as the decoy) was the name that was supposed to be named in the original message but got messed up due to the unforeseen lightning strike messing up the signal (the evidence being Lupin's self investigation manipulation/tactic later) but as it was cut off he had to slightly adapt to put things back on track. Every detail of this is specific enough to be credible but structured to be wrong about the most important thing: the name.

That the telegram would leak:
 Lupin understands institutions/social environments. A dramatic piece of intelligence about a famous criminal, delivered mid-ocean via the new technology of wireless telegraphy. He knew this would not stay contained within the captain's office. He knew it would spread to every passenger within hours. He needed it to spread.

That the R name would create a decoy dynamic. On a ship full of first-class passengers, the statistical likelihood of there being at least one or two passengers whose names begin with R and who happen to be blonde is not negligible. Lupin either knew or inferred that Rozaine (the man who Lupin will later use as his decoy) would be on board, or he designed the clue broadly enough that someone fitting the profile would inevitably exist. Either way, the R name creates a ready-made lightning rod for suspicion.

That his own chosen alias (d'Andrézy) begins with a different letter entirely:
 This is the core of the false scent. Everyone searching for Lupin will search among R-named passengers. The letter itself becomes a cognitive filter that eliminates the correct suspect from consideration before anyone starts looking. This is great narrative engineering as Lupin is not just reacting to circumstances,  he has authored the environment in which the entire ship will operate for five days.

The Havre false narrative tactic also serves to act as shielding for the wound on his right forearm during the 5 day voyage. It is a real and undeniable  piece of physical evidence that is impossible to fabricate or conceal if examined. Ganimard at the end of the story uses it to confirm the arrest. Lupin could not remove the wound. So his response to this irremovable evidence is not to hide it better but to reframe what the wound means. By including the wound in the false clue, he associates the wound with the false identity (R-name, blonde, traveling alone) rather than with his actual identity. If someone on board had thought to look at his right arm, the wound would still be there but by that point, the wound has been pre-coded as a clue pointing to "the Lupin who is hiding under an R name," not to the charming Parisian gentleman d'Andréz. This is great in what we call Falsification.Which is  taking genuine evidence about yourself and pre-associating it with a false lead so that the evidence confirms the wrong conclusion.

The d'Andrézy Persona:
Bernard d'Andrézy died in Macedonia three years before the voyage. Lupin acquired his papers and assumed his identity. The d'Andrézy identity is carefully calibrated for this specific social environment if you consider what it provides:

Social class and register:
d'Andrézy is a Parisian gentleman, comfortable in first-class transatlantic travel, educated, witty, socially fluent. This is not an identity that Lupin has to try hard to embody, since it is one that fits his natural social register. The less stretch required in an identity, the less chance of slippage. Lupin selects a cover that costs him the minimum in continuous performance energy.

Romantic plausibility: A charming, attentive, witty Parisian gentleman pursuing a beautiful heiress is one of the most culturally legible archetypes in French society. It immediately explains his presence in her company, his attention, his chivalry. The archetype is so familiar that nobody questions it. People read "Parisian gallant courts beautiful American heiress on ocean liner" as a complete and sufficient story, leaving no suspicions.

Investigative credibility: When Lupin decides to lead the investigation into Arsène Lupin's identity, d'Andrézy is the perfect vehicle. A curious, intelligent, slightly show-offy Parisian gentleman who wants to impress a beautiful woman by solving a mystery is consistent with the persona, draws no suspicion, and provides perfect cover for the investigator to be the perpetrator.

The dead man's advantage: A living person whose identity you've stolen is a permanent risk as they might appear, might file reports, might be seen elsewhere. A dead man three years in the ground has no such risk. The papers are valid. The name is real. The history is real. The only gap is the body, and Ganimard is the only one who knows this particular dead man's status. Without Ganimard present and until the very last moment of the voyage, nobody knows Ganimard is present; the identity is essentially airtight.

Lupin is essentially operating on a three layer identity structure. With the surface layer being appearance, manner, clothing, the behavioral layer (Parisian social habits, romantic attentiveness, intellectual playfulness), and the contextual layer (first-class passenger, legitimate traveler, known social personality on the ship by day two due to Lupin's great Social Integration and engineering. All three layers are operating simultaneously and are mutually reinforcing.

Using the Telegram as a weapon:
The telegram said "Arsène Lupin is on your vessel, first cabin, blonde hair, wounded right fore-arm, traveling alone under the name of R———".

Now I'm gonna break this down word by word as every element serves a double purpose as it's authentic enough to be credible, and misleading enough to protect Lupin.

First cabin:
Lupin is in first class. This specific accurate detail makes the entire telegram feel reliable and well-sourced. If the first detail is verifiably accurate, observers extend credibility to the rest.

Blonde hair: Now whether Lupin actually has blonde hair or not doesn't matter as it only needs to be stated as True and the authority Harve would provide the credibility.

Wound right fore-arm: it's true that Lupin has this wound and it's irremovable, so it gets integrated rather than concealed. The wound appears in the telegram as a detail that will be searched for in the R-named passengers and if/when they find that Rozaine has no visible wound it will partly exonerate him, creating confusion rather than clarity.

Traveling alone: True of Lupin as d'Andrézy. Somewhat misleading in its application to R names — Rozaine is also traveling alone, which makes him fit the profile, but the "alone" detail is correct in describing Lupin's actual situation.

Under the name of R———: Is false the only false element. Everything else in the telegram is technically true. The name beginning with R is the one planted falsehood, but it is supported by four true details  which means anyone who verifies the true details (blonde, first cabin, traveling alone) will have their confidence in the R detail strengthened by the verification of the others.

Lupin shows great Information control as The structure of the message exploits a known feature of human credibility assessment: people evaluate the reliability of a source by checking its verifiable claims. If the verifiable claims check out, they extend credibility to the unverifiable ones. Lupin has made four verifiable claims that are all true, and one non-verifiable claim that is false. The true claims do the work of establishing the false one.

The message is also structured to create  investigative funneling: for example it begins broad (first cabin 100 passengers) and narrows through each characteristic (blonde, perhaps 30 passengers) (traveling alone, perhaps 12) until the R name reduces it to a very small number. This funnel structure makes investigators feel they are converging on the answer through logic, when in fact the funnel has been designed to converge on a predetermined false conclusion.

The Self-Investigation manipulation/tactic:
When Miss Nelly complains about the situation and asks d'Andrézy whether he knows anything, his response is to propose that they investigate themselves. He takes out the passenger list and begins systematically eliminating names. Lupin is now leading the investigation into Arsène Lupin's identity and it's a tactic that accomplishes five things:

Presumption of Innocence through Action:
The investigator role carries a powerful presumption of innocence. A guilty person doesn't lead the investigation into their own guilt. This is a cognitive heuristic so deeply embedded in human social psychology. By taking the lead in the investigation, Lupin places himself in a cognitive category of person helping to find Lupin is, by definition, mutually exclusive with the category of being Lupin. The move becomes its own alibi.

Control of the Investigation's Direction:
If Lupin doesn't lead the investigation, someone else will. And someone else might look in the wrong directions or in Lupin's case the right directions. By seizing the investigation, Lupin ensures that its logic is his logic, its conclusions are his conclusions, and its endpoint is the endpoint he has designed.

Demonstration of Analytical Capability as Social Performance:
The method Lupin uses when he addresses Miss Nelly are phrases like, "do you find the problem complicated?" "what key do we hold?" "Let me apply the process of elimination" is a performance of intellectual superiority that is socially compelling, especially to Miss Nelly. He is simultaneously "solving" a puzzling problem and winning romantic points by solving it in an impressive way. Which has both miss Nelly and the people around them charmed and reassured simultaneously.

The Process of Elimination as Misdirection/framing:
The logic Lupin applies is correct, which makes it devastating:
•Only 13 passengers with names beginning with R in first class
•Of those, only 4 traveling alone
•Of those, only Rozaine is blonde and doesn't immediately provoke alternative explanations
The logic is sound. The conclusion is wrong. The error is not in the reasoning but in the premise. The assumption that the "R" name in the telegram is genuine. Lupin has constructed a logical argument with a hidden false premise, and because the logic from that premise to the conclusion is perfectly valid, the false premise is never examined. This is the technique of modus ponens with a poisoned major premise: if you accept P, then Q follows necessarily; Lupin made sure P would be accepted.

The Social Engineering of Suspicion:
By performing the investigation publicly, in front of a group, and arriving at a shared conclusion, Lupin creates something more durable than individual suspicion. He creates social consensus. Once the group has collectively worked through the logic and arrived at Rozaine, that conclusion becomes self-reinforcing through social dynamics: people who might individually have doubted will suppress those doubts because the group has spoken, because doubting the group conclusion requires the social cost of contradicting everyone, and because the logic was performed in public and seemed airtight.

Then there's the moment with Rozaine hearing the reasoning and coming under suspicion. When Nelly turns to him and he replies with a dark irony that "considering my name, my position as a solitary traveler and the color of my hair, I have already reached the same conclusion, and now think that I should be arrested"  Rozaine's response is the response of an innocent man who has seen the logic arrayed against him and knows it is unfair. His "strange appearance," his pallor, his thin lips that he expresses after coming under suspicion are not signs of guilt. They are signs of someone who recognizes they have been implicated/framed by circumstance with no obvious defense against the logic.

The Wound Misdirection:
So a bit after Rozaine was accused of being Lupin he was asked to show his arm to see if the wound was there or not. Which leads to this, "He had shown us his left arm, and I was on the point of calling his attention to the fact, when another incident diverted our attention." He pulls up his sleeve and shows his arm. But he shows his left arm. The telegram specified a wound on the right forearm. Lupin (who is the one narrating) notices this discrepancy immediately.

So what should d'Andrézy (who is Lupin), the concerned investigator helping to identify Lupin, do at this moment? He should point out the error. He should say, "Monsieur Rozaine, the telegram specified the right forearm, would you mind showing us your right arm?" This is exactly what a genuine investigator would do but Lupin does not do this. He is "diverted" by the jewel theft. But who staged the jewel theft? "Lupin" did. But if that's the case, who just watched Rozaine show the wrong arm? Lupin. The timing of the theft , at precisely the moment when Rozaine's innocence was about to be established or his guilt confirmed, is not coincidence. It's strategic timing Lupin committed the theft (or ensured it was discovered) at the exact moment when doing so would prevent a closer examination of the wound question. Let's consider the options at the moment:

If Rozaine's right arm is unwounded: Pointing it out exonerates Rozaine. But exonerating Rozaine through this specific method would then redirect scrutiny back to the passenger list (including d'Andrézy). Lupin cannot allow this.

If Rozaine's right arm has a wound: This would indicate Rozaine genuinely is Lupin, which contradicts Lupin's own knowledge of the situation. This can't be the case.

The actual situation: Rozaine is innocent. His right arm has no wound. If Lupin had prompted the arm check, Rozaine would have been exonerated at this early stage, the investigation would have restarted as Rozaine was the only one to fit all the requirements. With him eliminated as a suspect it might drive further investigation into excluding the blonde hair trait, and with  d'Andrézy traveling alone, and being intellectually prominent might then come under renewed scrutiny.
So the theft is triggered at this precise moment to interrupt the arm examination. The distraction serves a triple function: it produces loot (Lady Jerland's jewels), it reinflames suspicion against Rozaine (everyone immediately concludes it must be Lupin's work), and it prevents the one moment of scrutiny that might have shifted attention away from Rozaine and potentially toward Lupin.

The Jewel Theft's Method, Selection, and Timing:
The theft of Lady Jerland's  method and timing are all intentional.

The timing: As analyzed above, it occurs at the exact moment when Rozaine is being examined most closely and might be exonerated. Its discovery immediately reinflames his guilt.

The method: Lupin takes only the stones, leaving the mountings on the table. This choice is essential as the stones are:

•Small and easily concealed
•Portable in a way that mountings are not
Separable from their identifying contexts (a specific pendant or bracelet is recognizable; loose diamonds and pearls are not, without the specific knowledge of which stones came from which piece)
•Capable of being hidden in an object the size of a camera
•The mountings, left behind, serve as evidence of the theft without being the evidence that convicts. The people can see a theft has occurred, but the stolen items are now effectively invisible because they're unmoored from their identifying context.

The stateroom entry:
The passage notes that the thief was "obliged to force open the door of the stateroom, search for the jewel-case, which was hidden at the bottom of a hat-box, open it, select his booty and remove it from the mountings." This was done "in broad daylight, in a stateroom opening on a much frequented corridor." This is the audacity: doing something that appears impossibly risky in the most publicly accessible location, at the most public hour. The very openness of the corridor provides cover as a person walking in a frequented corridor is not suspicious; they belong there. The same corridor that seems to make the theft impossible actually makes it easier.

The destination of the stolen goods:
The stones ultimately end up inside the camera. This means Lupin must have had the camera with him, or obtained access to it, between the theft and the search. The camera's presence throughout the voyage is used regularly to photograph Miss Nelly, which means it has been completely normalized as an innocent personal item. No one would think to search it precisely because it has been so visible and so associated with an innocent activity (courtship photography) throughout the journey.

The Rozaine Decoy exploitation:
Rozaine functions as Lupin's primary strategic asset on the voyage, and his exploitation goes through three distinct phases, each building on the last.

Passive/Starting Exploitation:
Rozaine exists. He is blonde, travels alone, has an R name. The  pre-planted R name clue (as I said before I believe that the full name would have been Rozaine but due to the storm lighting cutting the message off Lupin had to adapt by using the self investigation manipulation) which means that Rozaine naturally fits the profile perfectly leading to Lupin beginning the exploitation.

Active Reinforcement:
Once Rozaine has been identified as the suspect, Lupin must ensure the suspicion sticks despite the absence of hard evidence. The jewel theft accomplishes this as  it occurs in a way that reinforces everyone's conviction that Rozaine is guilty (timing, opportunity, the sheer style of it as they think he's lupin). When Rozaine is released (he was imprisoned on the boat for a bit by the ship crew) for lack of evidence (no evidence that he stole lady Jerland's jewels and all of his identification is valid), the passenger community's response is, "of course Arsène Lupin will furnish you with as many documents as you desire". This isn't just everyone being paranoid. It is the social engineering of the investigation Lupin ran in Phase One. He built the conviction so thoroughly that evidence against it is reinterpreted as further evidence for it.

Active Assault and Exoneration: This is the most interesting phase and the one that reveals Lupin's strategic thinking. By later attacking Rozaine, stealing his 20,000 francs, and leaving a calling card, Lupin accomplishes something that seems paradoxical: he exonerates his own decoy. Why would he do this?
Because a decoy that is too thoroughly fixed as the suspect becomes a liability. If Rozaine is arrested and thoroughly investigated, a rigorous examination might reveal that he is genuinely innocent AND that the initial investigation that made him a suspectwas led by d'Andrézy (who is Lupin's persona) which might redirect attention. By staging the attack on Rozaine, Lupin achieves several things simultaneously:

•He proves (or strongly implies) that Rozaine is not Lupin, since "Lupin" has just attacked him.
•He steals Rozaine's 20,000 francs (practical).
•He creates the calling card moment ("Arsène Lupin accepts with pleasure the ten thousand francs offered by Mon. Rozaine") that is theatrical and serves as the signature of Lupin, and confirms the legend.
•He spreads terror by demonstrating that Lupin is capable of violently assaulting people in addition to theft.
•He destabilizes the investigation by removing the only suspect, creating the condition of "Arsène Lupin is now anyone and everyone" that generates maximum confusion in the final days of the voyage.
•He stops the investigation of Rozaine before it gets thorough enough to potentially implicate d'Andréz.

The assault on Rozaine is simultaneously: a robbery, a theatrical calling card moment, an exoneration of his own decoy, a terror operation, and an investigation-disruption tactic. Five functions in one action.

The card itself, "Arsène Lupin accepts with pleasure the ten thousand francs offered by Mon. Rozaine". Deserves a moment's attention. The handwriting is confirmed to be Lupin's and different from Rozaine's, which helps exonerate Rozaine. But there is some wit to the wording: Rozaine offered 10,000 francs for Lupin's capture (this was a bit after Lupin's self investigation tactic. Rozaine did this to get people to help him look for the real Arsene Lupin so that he can clear his name). Lupin takes 20,000 and thanks him for the 10,000. The joke is that Lupin is "accepting" a reward offered for himself by stealing from the person who offered the real reward. Not really important but I thought I'd mention it as I found it a bit funny.

The Captain's Watch/control maintenance:
The watch theft and its recovery in the second officer's collar box accomplishes specific operational objectives:
It demonstrates reach and power: By stealing from the captain ( the highest authority on the vessel) Lupin demonstrates that no one's personal space is secure, no status is protection. While it shows Lupin's bravado it is also a calculated display that maximizes the terror and confusion that keeps the investigation fragmented.

It implicates the second officer:
Temporarily. The watch is found in the second officer's possession. This creates suspicion within the ship's authority structure as officers begin investigating each other and the captain is left unsure whom to trust among his own crew. A divided and mutually suspicious command structure is a weakened investigative structure.

It raises the stakes of the atmosphere:
After the watch incident, the passage notes that passengers "supposed him capable of assuming the most unexpected disguises; of being, by turns, the highly respectable Major Rawson or the noble Marquis de Raverdan." The watch theft expands the psychological radius of suspicion to include even the most respectable passengers, which makes the investigation more obscured and less effective.

The Camera's tactical usage:
The camera is the single most important physical object in the operation, and its role is established with care over the entire voyage.
By the time the camera becomes relevant as a hiding place, it has been thoroughly normalized through multiple uses as a romantic prop throughout the voyage. d'Andrézy (who is Lupin) mentions photographing Miss Nelly "in various poses." The camera appears in social contexts:  deck chairs, group gatherings, and is associated entirely with the romantic subplot. It has been handled publicly, seen by many people, and associated consistently with the activity of portrait photography.

The passage itself contains the confirmation of the importance of the camera before it's revealed what's actual use is when d'Andrézy/Lupin is talking with Miss Nelly about the investigation, he says:

"But he should examine the lining of our hats and vests and everything we carry with us. Then, exhibiting my Kodak, a 9x12 with which I had been photographing her in various poses, I added: 'In an apparatus no larger than that, a person could hide all of Lady Jerland's jewels. He could pretend to take pictures and no one would suspect the game.'"

This is Lupin telling Miss Nelly exactly what he has done, while framing it as a hypothetical about what a thief might do. This is very bold of Lupin as he is revealing his method to the person who will ultimately hold the evidence (at the end of the stories chapter chapter), framing it as clever speculation, watching her react and assessing whether she is likely to make the connection or not. This moment serves multiple strategic functions:

It plants a seed into Nelly's mind:
When Lupin gives her the camera later, she either immediately understands what is happening (if she made the connection during this conversation) or she has the information to quickly understand when the arrest happens. Either way, he has ensured that when she is holding the camera and watching his arrest, she knows what the camera contains. He is passing her the crucial information she needs to make her choice, disguised as witty speculation.

It serves as a test:
By floating this specific possibility and watching her response, Lupin is assessing Nelly's perceptual acuity. A woman sharp enough to make the connection immediately, and who responds with alarm or dawning realization, is being evaluated. A woman who takes it as clever speculation but doesn't connect it to reality is being evaluated differently. Nelly's response (she takes it as an interesting conversation) tells Lupin something about the gap between her intelligence (sharp) and her willingness to believe her charming companion is actually a criminal (low). He can work with this.

It provides him plausible deniability even in the moment of the arrest:
When Ganimard arrests him and he hands the camera to Nelly, he has already publicly established (in conversation and in front of witnesses) that he considered the possibility of hiding things in a camera. If the camera is later found to contain jewels, he cannot be accused of secretly hiding things with no witnesses. But the conversation was framed as hypothetical, so it doesn't constitute a confession.
The physical transfer of the camera also requires a bit of  analysis. d'Andrézy (who is Lupin) says: "I gave her the camera, but too late for her to use it. Rozaine was already passing the detective."
The pretext is photography as he wants her to capture Ganimard and Rozaine in the same frame. This pretext is perfect because:
•It is entirely natural given his established habit of photographing things
•It explains why he gives her the camera in the specific moment when Ganimard is visible
•It frames the transfer as a romantic/social gesture rather than a criminal handoff
•It requires no verbal communication about what the camera contains as she already knows, or will figure it out By the time Ganimard stops him ten steps later, the evidence is already in civilian hands that Ganimard has no immediate authority to search.

Romantic Courtship manipulation:
Miss Nelly is not just a romantic interest. She is the operational holding point of this voyage strategy as she's one of the single most important assets Lupin develops.
Lupin's feelings for Nelly appear to be real. His final words at the end of the chapter is, "What a pity that I am not an honest man!" are not the words of someone who used her purely instrumentally and feels nothing. They are the words of someone who recognizes, in the moment of arrest, that the relationship he has been building for strategic reasons has become something he genuinely values, and that his life and its necessities have made honest reciprocation impossible.

The reason I mention this is because the most effective cover is the one you actually believe in. The most effective relationship is the one that is actually real. Lupin's genuine attraction to Nelly is what makes her trust in him plausible, his manner persuasive, and ultimately what makes her choose to drop the camera rather than hand it over. If Lupin had been purely calculating and she had sensed it, she would not have made the choice she made.
Although the feelings were real the courtship still had strategic goals:

Establishing constant proximity:
A man romantically pursuing a woman has an obvious, socially accepted reason to be constantly near her. No one thinks it strange that d'Andrézy is always in Nelly's company. This proximity means he is always present, always part of the social group at the center of the investigation (because Nelly is naturally at the social center), and always able to monitor and influence how events are interpreted.

Building a trusted relationship: The camera transfer only works if Nelly trusts him enough to hold something he gives her without examining it too closely or immediately handing it to authorities. That trust is built through the entire voyage's worth of attentive courtship. He is betting five days of romantic investment against the possibility that she will protect him when it matters.

Creating a narrative in which he is a sympathetic figure:
When Ganimard arrests him, the other passengers observe a scene in which a charming, attentive, apparently honest man is suddenly seized by police. The emotional register of the arrest (from the perspective of everyone who has watched the romance develop) is not "criminal caught" but "something wrong is happening to someone we've come to know." This matters for crowd psychology in the moment and for whether anyone intervenes or helps the authorities against him.

The evidence handoff mechanism:
The romantic relationship is a strong social framework in which a natural, unquestioned handoff of an object doesn't raise suspicion.

Anticipating Ganimard:
Lupin cannot know for certain that Ganimard will be in New York. He knows (or suspects) this by inference: "the silence was not reassuring" regarding wireless dispatches from America, and when he identifies Ganimard at the gangway, he immediately explains to Nelly, "I can understand now why we did not receive any news from this side of the Atlantic. Ganimard was here! and he always keeps his business secret."

This is retroactive explanation, but it reveals that Lupin had modeled the possibility of Ganimard's presence and prepared accordingly. Consider the reasoning chain:
•If French authorities know Lupin is aboard La Provence and they have any serious intent to arrest him, they will coordinate with American authorities.
•If they are coordinating with American authorities, the natural deployment point is the arrival port. If Ganimard (Lupin's specific nemesis, the most capable detective pursuing him)  is handling this, he will keep his presence secret to prevent Lupin from jumping ship, bribing crew, or otherwise escaping before arrival.
•Therefore: the absence of wireless communication from America is consistent with Ganimard being in New York, maintaining operational secrecy.

This is not certainty, but it is a probability assessment that warrants defensive preparation. And Lupin has prepared a contingency for it: the evidence is in the camera, which is in Nelly's hands, before he gets within ten steps of Ganimard. He doesn't know for certain he will be arrested. But he has designed the operation so that the arrest scenario is already addressed if it occurs.

This is contingency planning at work: not planning for a specific single future, but creating structures that work regardless of which of several possible futures materializes. If there is no Ganimard, Lupin walks off the ship with the camera and everything is fine. If there is a Ganimard, the camera is already out of reach. The structure works in both cases.

The sequence at the gangway is also worth looking at. Lupin observes Ganimard from the deck. He identifies him. He explains to Nelly who Ganimard is. He gives her the camera. He follows her toward the gangway. Ganimard stops him. This sequence is not reactive, it is the execution of a prepared contingency. The camera transfer, framed as the desire to photograph Ganimard and Rozaine together, happens at the last possible moment when it can appear natural. If Lupin had given her the camera earlier ( up on the deck for example) it might have seemed unmotivated. He waits until Ganimard's presence provides the perfect naturalistic pretext.

The ten steps he takes with Nelly toward the gangway before Ganimard stops him represent the minimum necessary distance between the camera handoff and the arrest. Every second of that walk is Lupin ensuring the camera is clearly in Nelly's possession and not subject to any kind of "he just handed you that" scrutiny.

The finale gamble of trusting Miss Nelly:
When Lupin looks at Nelly after the arrest and they exchange a glance, he is doing several things at once. He is communicating without words (any words would be damning since it'd expose the camera that has the evidence), he is reading her face for what she intends to do, and he is placing his legal freedom entirely in the hands of a woman he has known for five days and who has just discovered he is a criminal.
This gamble has specific odds that Lupin has been calculating during the voyage.

Factors in his favor:
•She has shown genuine warmth toward him throughout the voyage
•The camera drop is ambiguous — she can plausibly claim clumsiness
•There is no direct cost to her in not handing it over; she is not breaking a law by dropping a camera
•His courtship has cultivated genuine emotional sympathy
•He told her explicitly (in the hypothetical about the camera) what the situation is, which means she will feel she made an informed choice, not that she was unwittingly used
•He does not ask her anything, plead with her, or put any pressure on her, which is itself a form of respect that she likely senses

Factors against him:
•She has just discovered he is a thief who has been deceiving her for five days
•Lady Jerland, her close friend, is one of his victims
•Handing over the evidence would feel like justice for her friend
•The correct, law-abiding thing to do is clear
