Key Characters:
* The Protagonist: The Protagonist is the main character of the movie. As I said in the introduction, he’s unfortunately not known by any other name. I’ll be abbreviating his name as TP for the rest of this doc.

* Neil: Neil is The Protagonist’s companion that joins him early into the movie, he’s played by Robert Patterson. Initially brought in as a helping hand to get to Priya, he reveals that he knows more than he lets on throughout the course of the film.

* Andrei Sator: A Russian arms dealer and The main antagonist of the film, he acts as a “broker” for the future into the present. His goal is to assemble The Algorithm and seal it underground, “triggering and avoiding” armageddon for the future which will have the consequence of destroying the present

* Priya: An arms dealer and a high ranking member of Tenet. She initially acts as TP’s boss, giving him orders and telling him where to go as well as explaining what The Algorithm is

* Tenet: A “beyond secret” organization that uses ignorance as its ammunition. Not a single member of it knows anything about the organisation's nature nor anything significant about any of its members. Tenet was founded in order to combat Posterity, a future group of scientists that wish to reverse the flow of time using The Algorithm.

* Posterity: They’re never given a name within the film, nor are they even seen on screen, but I will be referring to them as “Posterity” for convenience's sake. Posterity is a group of scientists “generations from now” that are responsible for the creation of The Algorithm and act as the overarching villain of the film. They’re the ones that contacted Sator from the future in order to get him to manipulate events in the films present.

Part 0: The Situation

The contextual adversity of The Protagonist’s situation is at the core of what makes him scale so high in my books. It’s important to fully understand the situation he found himself in and the opponents he had to overcome in order to fully comprehend the scale of his plans.

Posterity:
As previously stated, Posterity are the future group of scientists that are essentially trying to destroy the past in order to save themselves. One of the scientists that was a part of Posterity rebelled, sending all 9 pieces of The Algorithm back into the past, trying to hide it forever. The Algorithm is a literal algorithm rendered into physical form so it could not be copied or replicated. Posterity is now trying to send it back to the future so they can use it to invert the entire world. They’re doing this because we in the past are the direct cause of climate change, which has impacted them so much that “they had no choice but to turn back”. In order to better understand how destroying the past could possibly benefit them, check out this video by Welby CoffeeSpill (the GOAT):https://youtu.be/Ff-1mSjHaPE?si=bK0l6ziO_4wvHi1e

Given the fact that “their oceans rose and their rivers ran dry” meaning the effects of climate change got so bad that they’re close to extinction, as well as the fact that they have/had the capability to invent Inversion technology, I estimate their timeframe to be at least 150 years ahead of the present in Tenet (which is set in May-June 2019). They could easily be 300 or 1000 years into the future, but it’s unknowable to how far along they are since nobody in the present can verify. This is all just to say that at bare minimum, Posterity has at LEAST a century and a half head start on everyone in Tenet.

It should be noted that Posterity is implied to be in desperate times from their point of view. I also estimate that they’re mere decades away from the complete end of the world and have run out of options. The fact that they’re even trying to destroy their own past, violating the Grandfather Paradox yet thinking they can get away with it just goes to show how dire things have become for them. This is to say, for the sake of fairness, that their position may not have been the most advantageous, they had struggles and adversity of their own. But don’t let that distract you from how ungodly powerful of a position they were really in.

Everything and everyone in Tenet, every event and everything that has not yet come to pass for the present, is all ancient history from the viewpoint of Posterity. Having access to inversion technology, old internet logs, newspapers as well as the hindsight that comes from simply being in the future, they are more than prepared to succeed in their goal of retrieving The Algorithm.

They’re already aware of where each section of The Algorithm is, all they need is someone in the past to go fetch it for them and bury it in a capsule so they can dig it up in the future, easy as that.

So they pick their best candidate:

Andrei Sator:

Andrei Sator was born into the Soviet Union and was a teenager by the time it collapsed. He was born in Stalsk-12, a secret city that was never put on any maps and was unknown to the world. One day an underground nuclear warhead exploded and scattered its pieces across the city, they needed people to dig up the plutonium and that’s where Sator came in. He dug up a capsule sent by Posterity that contained dozens of gold bars and a contract for an LLC with his name already written on it. He then spent the rest of his life (teenager to middle aged) hunting down and securing all pieces of the algorithm, financed and guided by Posterity. By the time the movie starts, Sator has all but one piece of The Algorithm already in his hands, all he needs is the Plutonium-241, the last piece.

“No Friends At Dusk”:

The events of the film as shown to the audience are all from the perspective of The Protagonist of the past, going through the situation for the first time.

After being through it all himself (but crucially, not knowing all of the details), The Protagonist realizes the true gravity of the situation he’s in and the challenges he has to overcome.

Place yourself in The Protagonist’s shoes for a second, you are against:

* A group of scientists that are centuries ahead of you, they invented the very technologies that you yourself previously used against them. Meaning that they are far more experienced and knowledgeable about Inversion and Time Travel than you could ever be.
* They have virtually all available information about the past in their hands. Internet history, phone records, newspapers, anything at all that happened in the past is information they can easily access, acquire and use against you.
* They’re already well aware of the potential consequences of trying to change the past, but are willing to risk it all anyways. Just because things worked out in the end for yourself in the past, doesn’t mean that they always will forever.

In summary, if you slip up once, it’s all over. Leave any trace behind, and I mean ANYTHING that could ever possibly tip off the future to your existence and the existence of your goals and plans to stop them, it’s all over. Make a phone call, send an email, anything. It’s all ancient history from their point of view, anything you could ever do has already been done for them. All they would need to do is find where your plan began and simply stop it from happening via any method possible.

So what do you do? What can you even begin to do without it all crumbling away right in front of you?

Part 1: The Kiev Opera Siege

Despite being the opening scene of this movie, this is easily one of the most complicated scenes to analyze in the whole film. This is because we are watching events already manipulated by the future. It also doesn’t help that this entire scene only took like 5 minutes and it explained nothing.

Present Perspective:
The Protagonist is introduced as being a part of an undercover CIA team who is already waiting outside the Opera House, waiting for the oncoming Terrorist Attack to happen (obviously implying they knew the terrorist attack would happen before it already did). TP and his team are seen with two men at the driver and passengers seat, the driver of which says “Wake up the Americans.” in Ukrainian. After the attack happens, we see that they have a collection of false badges to put on their sleeves, waiting to see which Ukrainian special forces show up so they can impersonate them and slip in undetected. It all goes to plan and TP and his team are able to blend in with the Ukrainians.

TP and his team are there to extract a person only known as “Well-Dressed Man’ in the script (WDM). WDM was sitting in a room with a Russian Colonel General and was there to sell what everyone believed to be Plutonium-241 to him when in actuality, it was a piece of The Algorithm. Before TP breaks into the room where WDM is, he spots the (actual) Ukrainian Special Forces gassing the audience of the Opera unconscious, which confuses him. TP successfully infiltrates the room, knocks the Russian General unconscious and gives WDM the CIA code phrase: “We live in a twilight world.” to which WDM responds with the appropriate response phrase of “And there’s no friends at dusk.”

After verifying the WDM, TP explains that the Opera Siege was a setup in order to kill WDM under the cover of a terrorist attack. WDM is confused, saying to TP: “But I established contact.” TP then explains that his mission is either to rescue him or kill him, and he tells WDM to make up his mind quickly. This is likely a bluff as I’ll explain later that it makes no sense for the TP to have actually gotten the orders to kill WDM here.
[Deception: Bluffing]

TP asks where “the package” is (what they believe to be Plutonium) and WDM informs him that it’s at coat check. He successfully convinces WDM to come with him to their team's rally point but encounters heavy gunfire from the Terrorists that pin him down. On his way out of the audience room, TP spots the Ukrainian forces planting bombs in the audience seats. TP is naturally stumped by this, a Ukrainian Agent notices his confusion and realizes that he’s an imposter and starts questioning who he is. Before he’s gunned down, another member of TP’s team (verifying himself by saying “No friends at dusk, huh?”) shoots the agent and takes over TP’s job of extracting WDM so TP can get to coat check to secure the Plutonium.

TP finds the Plutonium but remains confused at its encapsulation, later stating that he’s never seen a Plutonium container that looks like it. After he gets to his team's rally point with WDM already there, he demands that WDM and a member of his team (referred to in the script as “Target”) to swap clothes, swap exit routes and take the 241 with them as he no longer trusts that their escape route is fully safe due to his reasoning that the Ukrainians clearly have their own hidden agenda combined with the fact that their way in and out are also Ukrainian. Considering the top secret nature of their mission, he also reasoned that the “Ukrainians” that brought them there wouldn’t actually know what WDM looked like and thus would be fooled by the fake.

Remember how I said that there’s not one, clear cut explanation for Tenet and that almost anything works, the only difference being levels of complexity? This scene takes it to the extreme. I’ll try to formulate events based on the available facts we know and were shown in the film.

In 2008, either Tenet or Tenet-friendly forces lifted the Plutonium-241 out of a Russian missile site. This is the same 241 that was given to the CIA for it to have “surfaced” at the Opera Siege that WDM was trying to sell to the Russian General.

This was all a part of a plan to get in contact with Sator, who’s averse to dealing with the CIA directly (as shown in the same scene as the screenshot above). Because he’s “tapped into the intelligence services”, Sator learns that the WDM is actually CIA so he himself won’t show up to the Opera House although he will remain nearby, in case he needs to perform a temporal pincer. He makes a plan based on the knowledge that the 241 will be at the Opera House on the 14th. From the shadows, he finances and arms a group of Russian Terrorists to break into the Kiev Opera House and hold the audience Hostage. They don’t know who they’re doing this for, but their mission is to secure the floor and the VIP boxes, taking the WDM with them to get the 241 and leave with it. The Russian General knows this plan which is why he motions to WDM to tell him to stay seated when they arrived at the Opera.

Sator then hedges his bet and deliberately leaks this plan to the CIA so that they formulate a plan of their own to rescue WDM, who employ local Ukrainian sources (Sator's men in disguise) to get the CIA team in and out (Sator made sure to tell his men what WDM looked like just in case.) If the CIA fails to extract WDM then he gets the 241 from the Russian General, if the CIA succeeds then he gets it from the extraction team. This is where The Protagonist comes into play at the beginning of the movie and is also why he didn’t receive orders to kill WDM, it would make no sense for him to do that.

Meaning that FP saw through Priya’s desire to “tie up loose-ends” by herself based on what little he knew of her in the past (and what little he knows about her now in the future given that he recruited her). He even mistakenly let her believe that tying up loose ends was her role in Tenet when it wasn’t, probably just for this exact moment and another one that will come much later.

Debunks:
I’ll be using this section to quell any debunks that might befall upon this doc or The Protagonist. I’ll update this section with any and all debunks that come my way, just to save me from having to rehash old conversations multiple times.

Debunk 1: “Tenet is a deterministic film”
You’ll have noticed that I assumed that every character in Tenet had agency over their own actions and that changing the past was indeed possible for this entire document.

This debunk is the most reasonable and concise out of all of them. It’s the belief of the vast majority of people that have analyzed Tenet that the film portrays fate/destiny in a beautiful/accurate way. The characters simply go through the motions, acting out a prewritten path. The future simply recreated whatever they saw in the past. Even people such as Welby Coffeespill, who’s probably the person who understands the most about the events of the film than anyone else, has come to this conclusion. Naturally, this conclusion makes this whole doc kind of worthless or at least it lowers the credibility of several interpretations, as everything that was planned was simply always fated to happen.

However, this premise is false. Nolan very clearly made Tenet with the concept of the characters free will and agency in mind and there are multiple points of evidence for this. I’ll start with the literal introduction to Inversion.

Part 1: The Literal Introduction of Inversion
During the Lab sequence when Inversion and its properties are first introduced, the scene plays out as follows: The scientist gets The Protagonist to hold his hand over an inverted bullet. The Protagonist hovers his hand over the bullet, and nothing happens. Then the scientist lady says “You had to have dropped it.”

Suddenly when The Protagonist wills the bullet into his hand, it happens. That’s when the scientist lady explains “From your perspective, the bullet flew into your hand. From the bullets perspective, you dropped it. Either way we run the tape, you made it happen.”
That last line is the most important part, nothing happened when The Protagonist held his hand over the bullet, it’s only when he willed it to happen that it actually moved. If Nolan really wanted to make the world of Tenet completely deterministic, then a character's will and agency would be irrelevant
It becomes even more clear when TP asks “What about free will?” The scientist lady responds with “The bullet wouldn’t have moved if you didn’t put your hand there.”

Therein lies the “reality” that Neil described. The world they live in is neither fully deterministic nor fully free either. More on that in the following part.

Part 2: “What’s happened, happened.”
That exact line from Neil is brought up several times within the film and is also used by people who believe the “Tenet is fully deterministic” interpretation as exact confirmation that their interpretation is true. But this could not be further from the truth and we really don’t need to go much further than when that quote is fully explained at the end of the film:

TP: “But can we change things if we do it differently?”
Neil: “What’s happened, happened. Which is an expression of faith in the mechanics of the world, not an excuse to do nothing.”
TP: “Fate?”
Neil: “Call it what you want.”
TP: “What do you call it?”
Neil: “Reality.”

That “not an excuse to do nothing” line compounds with another piece of evidence in favour of agency that some people tend to overlook. During the scene where Neil explains The Algorithm to TP, TP asks the question about Posterity “Doesn’t us being here now mean that it never happened, that we stopped them?” and Neil responded to that crucially with “Optimistically I’d say that’s right”.

If Tenet were truly deterministic, the answer to that question is a simple “Yes.” But if the answer to that is just “Yes.”, then why did Neil say that it’s not an excuse to do nothing? If even existing in the present which Posterity was aiming to destroy means that they never succeeded, then that is the biggest excuse ever to do literally nothing. As clearly no matter what they do, it would be in line with the actions/fate that lead to Posterity not succeeding in their plans in the first place.

Ok, whatever. Posterity was mistaken in their goal anyways because changing the past is outright impossible in Tenet as confirmed by Priya (“If that universe can exist, we don’t live in it.”)...right?

Part 3: “That’s unknowable.”
First of all, I wouldn’t really take Priya’s word on anything. If you’ve been paying attention to this doc then you’d know her entire job is to practically lie to TP about everything to keep him on track on the path FP set out for him. TP is a person who’s shown numerous times to be curious about changing events that happened in the past and literally went to Priya in that scene to request her to do exactly that. It’s simply far more convenient for her to tell TP that it’s impossible rather than tell him the truth which could jeopardize the mission. Anyways…
There has never been a single time in the entire film where they expressed exact confirmation that changing the past is impossible. In fact, it’s the exact opposite. Several times throughout the film the assumption that the past CAN change is brought up and taken seriously as the literal motivation of the antagonists and thus the entire plot of the film rests upon the fact that Posterity changing the past is a real threat. I’m sure the group of scientists that invented Inversion technology in the film would know that better than anyone else.
Anyways, the FIRST time the “What’s happened, happened” line is brought up is even more telling than the last time. During the Tallinn sequence, TP goes to Neil and asks “Sator threatened to shoot Kat in the past, if he goes back and actually shoots her, what happens to her here?”
Once again, if Tenet was TRULY deterministic, then the answer to this question from Neil would be “Nah don’t worry about it fam, that’s impossible.” but it wasn’t. His actual response was
“That’s unknowable. If you’re there to make a change you’re not here to observe its effect.”
Unknowable, not impossible.
Later on in this exchange, Neil realizes that TP takes the idea of Sator shooting Kat in the past seriously and decides to go back and make sure it doesn’t happen. Neil tries to stop him and says “If you go back, you might give him exactly what he wants.” TP ignores him and continues the plan anyways.
When that sequence ends and TP inverting himself gives Sator what he wanted, Neil is the one to say: “I tried to warn you.”

This exchange is literally NEIL HIMSELF trying to stop TP from going through events that have already happened, thereby it’s him trying to change the past.
I can go on and on with examples of Neil among others personally confirming that Tenet isn’t entirely deterministic but I’ve had enough of this doc at this point. I encourage anyone to go back through the film and note how many times the question of “Can we change things if we do it differently?” or “What about free will?” is asked by The Protagonist and note how many times the answer to those questions are “No.” and “It’s not real.” (Spoiler Alert: They never answer those questions so simply.)

Conclusion:
Tenet is neither fully deterministic nor fully free. Determinism HAS to exist in some capacity in order for Inversion to even be possible. What could explain this dichotomy? My personal interpretation is this: “The past doesn’t change unless you make it change.”
This makes the most amount of sense to me because the past simply remains the past. Whatever’s happened, happened, and will continue to keep happening just as it always did until someone comes along and deliberately changes it. But for people still experiencing that “past”, any changes made to it are unknowable because for them, it’s already happened.
