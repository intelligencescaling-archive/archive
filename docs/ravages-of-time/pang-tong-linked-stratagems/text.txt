Context: After taking control of the young emperor Liu Bian, Cao Cao manipulated the imperial authority to oppress the court. He held dominant power over crucial policies within the feudal apparatus. At this point, Cao Cao relocated the capital to Xuchang, which bordered Jingzhou.

The Importance of Jingzhou:

“During the Eastern Han dynasty, China was divided into thirteen provinces, among which Jingzhou lay in the south. It encompassed regions corresponding to modern-day Hubei, Hunan, Guizhou, and Jiangxi, with its administrative seat located at Nanyang (Wan City – the place where Emperor Guangwu of Han rose to power).
Situated at the confluence of the Yangtze River, Jingzhou bordered Yangzhou to the east, Yizhou to the west, Jiaozhou to the south, and Yuzhou to the north. It was the second-largest province after Yizhou. Strategically, Jingzhou occupied a position of paramount military importance, especially in its northern commanderies such as Nanyang, Nan Commandery, and Jiangxia.
Beyond its geopolitical significance, Jingzhou also held great economic value. Enriched by the alluvial deposits of the Yangtze River and its tributaries, the land was fertile and highly suitable for agriculture, livestock raising, and water resources. Its central location and plains terrain further made Jingzhou a bustling hub of both production and trade.
In every era, Jingzhou remained a prosperous land. Even in the late Eastern Han, when the imperial court declined, warlords carved up territories, and warfare was unending, Jingzhou continued to maintain relatively stable development compared with other regions.
For this very reason, Jingzhou also became a densely populated region, capable of both recruiting soldiers for warfare and mobilizing labor for production”.
In order to seize Jingzhou, Cao Cao first had to capture Nanyang – the territory of Zhang Xiu. Zhang Xiu’s strategist, Jia Xu, who had formerly maintained ties with Cao Cao, advised him to surrender, for if Cao Cao were to launch a campaign, the fall of Nanyang would only be a matter of time. Zhang Xiu accepted this counsel and submitted. Meanwhile, Cao Cao used the pretext of conquest to conceal his true intentions of drilling troops and indulging his own lust—specifically, his desire for Zhang Ji’s widow, who was also Zhang Xiu’s aunt. Because of this vice, Cao Cao nearly lost his life in Nanyang.
Returning to Pang Tong’s plan, it consisted of one stage of preparation followed by the implementation of the larger strategic deployment.
Preparatory Stage: Strategic Layout.
Very long after receiving the title “Young Phoenix” from Master Water Mirror, Pang Tong still had no intention of leaving the Water Mirror Academy to find a lord for himself. Instead, he remained at the academy to teach strategy to advisors from many different warlords. Each day, he would lecture the same lesson over and over, to the frustration of his students.

Purpose 1: To engrave into their minds a key philosophy of warfare, and at the same time to lay down the path for his own future.
Purpose 2: To train and filter out those advisors suitable for his Linked Stratagems.
Purpose 3: To improve the thinking of a group of strategists whose perspectives on warfare were stagnating – this served as an educational role (Pang Tong at this stage was acting like a lecturer).
Purpose 4: By remaining hidden within the academy, Pang Tong could make use of the Water Mirror Academy’s intelligence-gathering capabilities to keep track of events and choose the right timing to execute his plan, as well as to select the most suitable lord for himself.

The final step: capturing Jia Xu, who was stationed on the outskirts of Nanyang, to prevent him from sabotaging the entire stratagem.

Main Phase: Strategy Execution.

Cao Cao’s main army advanced toward Nanyang, outwardly under the banner of conquest, yet in truth Zhang Xiu had already submitted the day before. Upon entering Lushui, Cao Cao was cordially received by Zhang Xiu. At that very moment, however, a report arrived: a separate force was advancing toward Xiahou Dun, who was encamped outside the city. In order to pacify the turmoil, Cao Cao declared that under such circumstances, even if Zhang Xiu were foolish, he would not dare to act so rashly. Consequently, Cao Cao withdrew his army to Luoyang to prevent tensions from further escalation.

Meanwhile, among the assassins lurking in the shadows, discord erupted. They argued fiercely over who had fired the arrow. In reality, they did not belong to the same faction—each had been secretly hired by Pang Tong from different organizations, and thus there existed virtually no trust among them.

All of this had long been foreseen by Jia Xu.

Third step: Once Cao Cao’s main force entered the city, Jia Xu ordered that detachment to mislead the couriers, distorting the intelligence before it reached Zhang Xiu. In doing so, he provided the leverage that reinforced Cao Ang’s plan, thereby enabling them to reap the greatest possible advantage.

Cao Ang’s Stratagem

Step One: After Cao Cao’s army entered the city of Lushui, Cao Ang arranged for an assassin to make a feigned attempt on his father’s life. He then coordinated with Jia Xu to falsely accuse Zhang Xiu of orchestrating the attack.

Step Two: According to the prearranged scenario, Cao Cao would withdraw his forces to Luoyang, deliberately refraining from further investigation. This would plunge Zhang Xiu into a state of confusion, compelling him to frantically prove his loyalty. The operation thus served two purposes: training Cao Cao’s troops and enabling him to identify and eliminate dissenters within Zhang Xiu’s ranks.

Outcome: Lushui fell into chaos. Zhang Xiu, in fear and desperation, brought his generals and family into Cao Cao’s camp as hostages.

At this very moment, Pang Tong’s plan was set into motion.

Knowing that Cao Cao’s cavalry had been dispatched outside the city, Pang Tong and Zhao Yun devised a plan to infiltrate Cao Cao’s camp under the cover of night, disguised as a supply convoy. However, the challenge remained daunting: even with the cavalry absent, the disparity in strength was still overwhelming. Moreover, Cao Cao’s troops had recently undergone rigorous training specifically designed to counter night raids and sudden incursions. To make matters worse, Zhang Xiu—who served as Cao Cao’s rear guard—was now being manipulated under Cao Ang’s stratagem. Should open conflict break out, Zhang Xiu would undoubtedly launch a relentless pursuit to prove his loyalty, plunging the situation into even greater peril. It was at this moment that Zhao Yun conceived of a possibility: within Cao Cao’s forces, there might exist infiltrators secretly aligned with Pang Tong.

As Zhao Yun had anticipated, the infiltrator within Cao Cao’s forces was Hu Xani — the Yuan general who had been sent by Zhang Xiu into Cao Cao’s camp as a hostage.

Step One: Pang Tong ordered the allied armies to hover menacingly around the outskirts of the camp. In turn, Hu Xani would deliver a report to Cao Ang, claiming that an enemy force was preparing to strike at the encampment.

Using the pretext of lacking manpower, Hu Xani skillfully deceived Xiahou Dun and Cao Ang, forcing them to release the generals who had previously been detained during the purge (a scheme originally devised by Cao Ang to identify those dissatisfied with Cao Cao under Zhang Xiu’s command). To prevent any unforeseen incidents, Xiahou Dun personally accompanied Hu Xani, directly overseeing his every move.

From a subtle remark made by Cao Ang, it became clear: Hu Xani was the very man who had taken the initiative to capture Zhang Xiu’s aunt and present her to Cao Cao. Earlier, Cao Cao had received a report claiming that Zhang Xiu had “offered up his aunt and a general” as hostages. In truth, Zhang Xiu had only surrendered Hu Xani and was entirely unaware of Cao Cao’s peculiar desires. It was Hu Xani who orchestrated the abduction of Zhang Xiu’s aunt, feigned being coerced, and continued infiltrating Cao Cao’s ranks in order to execute Pang Tong’s scheme.

That scheme involved relaying information to Zhang Xiu, exploiting his resentment to incite him into mustering his forces and launching an assault on Cao Cao’s main encampment — precisely at the moment when Xiahou Dun’s core troops had been lured away, leaving them unable to provide timely reinforcements.

What is remarkable is that Pang Tong had already accounted for the possibility of this strategic arrangement collapsing. Without the “synchronization” of intelligence between Zhang Xiu’s camp and Cao Cao’s, the entire plan would have been impossible to carry out. Pang Tong clearly understood that Cao Cao would keep secret both his own improper intentions toward Zhang Xiu’s aunt and the claim that Zhang Xiu had “offered up his aunt and a general” in order to win favor.

If such information were to leak, the honor of both men would be utterly ruined. In particular, should Zhang Xiu learn of Cao Cao’s designs on his aunt, he would fight recklessly to the death. In that case, even if Cao Cao succeeded in seizing Nanyang, both his reputation and his army would suffer grievous losses. Not to mention the risk of a third power launching an opportunistic strike amidst the turmoil — scenarios which Cao Cao absolutely wished to avoid.

For this very reason, Pang Tong had no fear that Hu Xani’s deception would ever be exposed — for he was supremely confident that Cao Cao would go to any lengths to conceal this shameful secret.

His Method.

Proactively Eliminating Cao Cao:

Playing on Common Political Perceptions:

Pang Tong expertly played on the common belief that few would dare to directly confront Cao Cao. The powerful forces who could were either preoccupied or hesitant:
Lü Bu: Once failed to trap Cao Cao at Puyang and was not ready for another direct clash.

Other Liu Clan Branches: All were influenced by Cao Cao's dominance, but no faction was willing to sacrifice its own interests to oppose him.

Liu Bei: As the publicly recognized Imperial Uncle, he carried the aspirations of the people of Xuzhou, a place that had suffered from Guo Jia’s massacre. Although he no longer governed Xuzhou, most of his soldiers were from there, and their hatred for Cao Cao ran exceptionally deep.

The Smoke and Mirrors Tactic:
One of Pang Tong's key objectives was to firmly convince his peers that Liu Bei was his master. The entire campaign was framed as "assisting the Han and purging traitors," with the cooperation of Liu Bian and Liu Chong further cementing this theory.
