You’ll really want to watch the video to get the full context—having some background on Three Kingdoms history definitely helps.  Three Kingdoms Summary - OverSimplified
The story begins in the dying days of the Han dynasty. The warlord Dong Zhuo seizes control of the young, helpless Emperor and throws China into a bloody, tangled civil war between dozens of ambitious warlords.
The Ravages of Time centers on Sima Yi, a young prodigy from the merchant Sima clan, and his most lethal asset: Liaoyuan Huo, the brilliant leader of the Crippled Legion—a group of disabled but extraordinarily dangerous assassins who carry out the clan’s bidding. Though exceptionally intelligent, Sima Yi is notoriously lazy and mainly concerned with how the Sima clan can profit from the chaos. To that end, he repeatedly sends Huo across China on assassination missions.
Liaoyuan Huo is loyal, but increasingly restless. During his missions, he encounters a man renowned for his integrity: Liu Bei. Huo gradually grows to admire him. Before long, both Huo and Sima Yi are drawn into nearly every major conflict depicted in Romance of the Three Kingdoms.

The Eight Geniuses
These eight extraordinary advisors are students of the renowned scholar Sima Hui, known as “Water-Mirror.” A legendary teacher of the previous generation, he trained many of the era’s greatest strategists. The Eight Geniuses possess abilities far beyond those of ordinary men, making them highly sought after by the various warlords. Whichever faction one of them chooses—even a minor one—immediately becomes a serious contender in the struggle to reunify China.
Once a Genius selects the lord he wishes to serve, he removes the identical mask and uniform used to conceal his identity at the academy and departs to begin his work.

Background
Guo Jia is one of these Eight Geniuses—the elite students of Water-Mirror Academy. Although Chan Mou doesn’t reveal much about the academy itself, we can infer its curriculum from the feats of the Geniuses and the distinguished teachers who appear throughout the story. Subjects include:
* Geography

* Astronomy

* Climatology

* Psychology

* Mathematics

* Poetry and correspondence

* Ritual and ceremony

* Ethics

* History

* Politics

* Philosophy

* Military strategy

* Religion

* Technical arts

* Chess

* Painting

All eight Geniuses master every discipline. Some, like Pang Tong and Zhuge Liang, even go on to teach other students and mid-level strategists. Over time, however, each student discovers their own truth—shaping their ideals and strengths. Though they study under the same master and share a sibling-like bond, they ultimately pursue very different paths.
This context is important to understand just how formidable Guo Jia is. Even without resorting to his “Dark Strategy,” he would still be a terrifyingly capable strategist.
Among the Eight, Guo Jia embraces a unique philosophy. He believes in using ruthless force to gain power quickly, then governing with benevolence once unity is achieved. In his view:
“To save the world, you must first push it into the abyss, so that future generations truly value peace.”
His ideal stems from a desire to end the war as quickly as possible. Mercy, he argues, would only prolong the conflict and increase the death toll. Zhang Fei once summarized Guo Jia’s intent as “Kill one, threaten ten”—but a more accurate description is:
“Kill one, save ten.”

Part 1: Xuzhou Slaughter arc
The next topic I want to discuss is Cao Cao’s campaign against Xuzhou, one of the most brutal conflicts in Chinese history due to the staggering number of civilians killed. First, Cao Cao needed a reason to attack Xuzhou, and one of the Eight Geniuses, Guo Jia, suggested that Cao Cao use his father's death as a reason, accusing Tao Qian, the governor of Xuzhou, of killing his father and stealing his wealth.

Under Guo Jia’s direction, Cao Cao’s forces launched a devastating assault on Xuzhou:
   * Sishui, one of Tao Qian’s cities, was obliterated. Over 10,000 civilians were killed.

   * Guo Jia ordered all farmland in Xuzhou burned—not only to cripple the region, but also to bait Lü Bu.

   * He then blocked the flow of the Si River, cutting off water and food supplies.

The brutality escalated. Guo Jia’s forces massacred the population around the city of “30,000” (the name of the city, not a number), killing another 10,000 civilians. At Sishui alone, casualties reached 190,000. By this point, Cao Cao’s faction had seized ten strongholds and was advancing toward Pengcheng.
The soldiers themselves were terrified and begged Guo Jia to end the slaughter. Guo Jia paused, looked up at the sky, and eventually ordered a halt—not out of mercy, but because he had determined that a three-month dry season was about to begin. He erected a ceasefire and established a long encampment.
To the army, this decision seemed incomprehensible. Delaying the war would:
      1. Destroy the momentum of Cao Cao’s victories

      2. Squander their psychological advantage over the shattered Xuzhou defenders

      3. Give Tao Qian’s forces time to reorganize and prepare

But Guo Jia saw further than anyone else. He intended to crush Xuzhou strategically, psychologically, politically, and environmentally—all at once.
During the truce, Guo Jia spoke privately with Jia Xu. Xun Yu, horrified by the massacre, had isolated himself in his room. Jia Xu—himself a student of the “Dark Strategy”—understood Guo Jia’s intent immediately.
Guo Jia’s worldview was stark: “There are no good people in this world.”
 Allowing Cao Cao to massacre civilians was not just military strategy—it was a test of the surrounding factions. If everyone remained silent in the face of such slaughter, then their moral claims were empty. Guo Jia’s philosophy remained consistent:
“To save the world, you must first kill the world.”
 Blood and suffering would remind future generations of the horrors of war, making lasting peace possible.

3 months later...
Three months later, Mi Zhu fled to Pingyuan to seek Liu Bei’s help. At this moment we understand the meaning of Guo Jia’s plan: Right Time, Right Place, Right People.
Mi Zhu’s reaction also reveals something important: Liu Bei’s royal lineage had recently been made public. This was orchestrated by Zhao Yun, who had obtained an imperial decree after the assassination attempt on Dong Zhuo. Now recognized as an imperial relative, Liu Bei—valued for his benevolence—was a natural rallying point for the desperate people of Xuzhou.
Mi Zhu reported the current situation:
Right Time
Xuzhou had entered the dry season exactly as Guo Jia had predicted. Combined with Cao Cao’s blockade of the Si River, the population was now suffering extreme shortages of drinking water.
How did Guo Jia predict this?
Because he had mastered:
         * Chinese monsoon patterns

         * Seasonal air currents

         * The effects of the Tropic of Cancer

         * Ancient meteorology

Ancient Chinese observers used several methods to forecast weather—rainbows, stars, wind, clouds, thunder, fog, color patterns. Guo Jia relied on wind, the only method capable of predicting long-term seasonal shifts. With this knowledge, he conquered Pengcheng and ten other cities without casualties.

One city still resisted: Tan County, commanded officially by Chen Deng but strategically by Zhao Yun, leader of the Handicapped Warriors under Sima Yi.
Although the Sima clan were allied with Cao Cao, they also had deep business ties with Xuzhou. This forced their agents—like Zhao Yun—to oppose Cao Cao from the shadows.

Zhao Yun devised a clever defensive plan:
            * Loosen the wall’s defenses to lure Cao Cao’s siege towers in

            * Kill an enemy general during the approach

            * Use catapults to destroy the siege towers and the dense cluster of soldiers around them

            * Exploit the unstable terrain around the city so that the only accessible entry point was the debris-filled front gate

At night, Guo Jia uses the snake formation for his army to break through the defenses
: It's a formation that literally simulates a snake; if you attack the tail, the head will bite you, if you attack the head, the tail will attack you, if you attack the middle, both the tail and the head will attack you. He uses it not for battle, but for high maneuverability, focusing almost entirely on getting close to the Tan County front gate walls as quickly as possible, using the darkness of the night as an advantage.
https://i.imgur.com/aLnN2i2.png
And it worked quite well, because by the time Zhao Yun realized Guo Jia's strategy of attacking at night, they were already out of range of the catapult and archer, very close to  the walls, and started another battle.

Then Guo Jia struck at the supply chain.
He sent a messenger ahead to spread false intelligence, tricking Xuzhou’s transport team into believing enemy soldiers were lying in ambush on the mountain path. Because it was too dark to identify friend from foe, the confused transport team shot arrows up the mountain and killed the very rescuers who had come to protect them.
Meanwhile, Guo Jia set the stage for the true killing blow.

On a nearby hill, Guo Jia explained to Yue Jin that he had predicted a monsoon—scheduled to arrive that very day.
A monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal. In summer, moist winds blow inland; in winter, dry winds blow outward. Observing subtle meteorological signs, Guo Jia had timed the siege to the monsoon’s arrival.
He then surrounded the other three gates of Tan County with fire. When the monsoon wind shifted, smoke engulfed the city. The defenders were suffocating. Their only escape route was the front gate, where Cao Cao’s forces were already attacking.
It was a perfect trap: every choice led to death.
And yes—this operation was also part of a much larger trap aimed at luring out Lü Bu.

The mountains behind Tan County are the key to the entire campaign. They serve as:
               * A supply route

               * A natural fortress

               * A deployment zone

               * A strategic observation point

Whoever controlled the mountain controlled the war.
Cao Cao’s invasion of Xuzhou was never purely about territory—it was a bait operation to lure Lü Bu into attacking Cao Cao’s main city. By leaving his capital intentionally underprotected, Cao Cao planned to ambush and eliminate Lü Bu.
To bait Lü Bu effectively, Guo Jia ensured that Cao Cao’s army appeared to carry very limited rations, even though Cao Cao had secretly harvested enormous supplies from Xuzhou before burning it. This misinformation played directly into Lü Bu’s habit of attacking only when he knew the enemy was low on provisions.
However, one obstacle remained: Sima Yi.
He could not allow Xuzhou—and especially Tan County—to be destroyed, yet he could not openly oppose Cao Cao, whose current position had been secured through the Sima clan’s support. Sima Yi had to restrain Cao Cao indirectly, without ever striking at him.

 So the most important question remains: Where are Cao Cao's rations?
Guo Jia first moved supplies along the western route, the most obvious and safest path. But this route was actually a decoy, and one of Lü Bu’s commanders—Zhang Liao—fell for it with hidden soldiers instead of supplies.

This proves Lü Bu had already learned of Cao Cao’s second, secret supply line, but Guo Jia’s trap prevented him from seizing it.
When the wind shifted, fire from the surrounding regions destroyed multiple routes—except for Makeng Valley.
Unfortunately for Guo Jia, he was outmaneuvered by Zhuge Liang, forcing him to flee. But in his defeat, Guo Jia noticed something extraordinary: Sima Yi and Zhuge Liang were cooperating, just as they had long ago when they solved a puzzle no other genius in China could solve.
Realizing this, Guo Jia saw a new opportunity:
if Sima Yi could think on the same wavelength as Zhuge Liang—the greatest mind of the era—Cao Cao’s faction might gain an unimaginable strategic advantage.

He also needs Sima Yi for his businessman status, which will be hugely crucial for what’s about to happen in his Guandu strategy.

Part 2: Yan Province Arc (Both Guo Jia and Cao Cao made this plan)
The Yan Province arc is a surprisingly tight and cleverly constructed storyline, but before diving in, there are two things to clarify.
First, the light-blue area on the map represents Yan Province, where most of the events unfold. There is also a major location repeatedly referred to as “West City.” Since its exact position is unknown, we’ll assume it corresponds to the red dot on the map and refer to it as Xicheng for clarity.
For reference:
                  * Lu Bu and his army are stationed in Puyang.

                  * Cao Cao is based in Xicheng with his forces.

The arc opens with the supposed decapitated head of Cao Cao delivered to Lu Bu—an act orchestrated by Xu Chu, one of Lu Bu’s spies inside Cao Cao’s camp. Neither Lu Bu nor his strategist Chen Gong is fooled. They never trusted Xu Chu enough to believe he truly assassinated Cao Cao.
Cao Cao’s fake death was a trap—a pretext to provoke Lu Bu, and perhaps even other lords, into attacking Xicheng. But Cao Cao knew such a simple trick wouldn’t be enough to move Lu Bu, so he played a deeper game.
Inside his own guard unit was a hidden cell of Lu Bu’s spies known as the Lu Jin Faction. Cao Cao had known all along. When the Lu Jin spies attempted to assassinate him, Dian Wei was already prepared, cutting them down and protecting Cao Cao.
Yet Cao Cao ordered Dian Wei to spread the rumor that the Lu Jin faction had successfully killed him. Even those who knew his first “death” was fake were told he was dead again. This plunged Xicheng into chaos, and chaos was the bait.
As planned, Lu Bu mobilized to attack Xicheng—or so it seemed.

Cao Cao wanted Lu Bu to march toward Xicheng so he could ambush him there. In reality, Lu Bu had already anticipated this. He moved his army out of Puyang, leaving only a defensive shell, and prepared a counter-trap.

Using the map, the red arrow is Cao Cao's army marching from Xicheng to Puyang, and the blue arrow is Lu Bu's army marching from Puyang to Cao Cao's trap.​
Cao Cao seized Puyang rapidly. Only the North Gate held out, but the entire city appeared to be under his control.
Yet something was wrong.
Most civilians were missing, including Mr. Tian, one of Cao Cao’s spies. As Cao Cao pushed deeper, his forces realized too late that:
Lu Bu’s entire army had doubled back and surrounded Puyang.
 Chen Gong then burned the entire city, sealing all gates with walls of fire and trapping Cao Cao inside. It was a perfect reversal of Cao Cao’s own tactic.

So the With Cao Cao trapped, Xun Yu—one of the Eight Geniuses—arrived at Xicheng. Immediately grasping Chen Gong’s strategy, he split Cao Cao’s forces:
                     * Half would march to rescue Cao Cao in Puyang.

                     * Half would attack Chenliu, a major city in Yan Province.

This was the classic strategy “Besiege Wei to rescue Zhao.”
 But Chen Gong adapted quickly—he sent half of Lu Bu’s army to intercept Xun Yu’s march toward Chenliu.

He reached Xun Yu before the city and, instead of engaging directly, raced ahead to the valley Xun Yu intended to cross and surrounded it. Xun Yu halted his forces in the valley’s forests before Chen Gong could attack.
Chen Gong knew attacking a hidden army would expose his troops to massed archery, so he instead ordered the forests burned, forcing Xun Yu out and cutting off the road to Chenliu.

Chen Gong believed he had decisively cornered Xun Yu.
He was wrong.
Just as Chen Gong seemed victorious, Sima Yi appeared.
He approached Chen Gong as Xun Yu’s envoy and offered him a chance to surrender.
Chen Gong laughed. Why surrender when he held every advantage?
Sima Yi simply handed him a flag—saying it was a “gift”—and left.
Moments later, arrows rained down on Chen Gong’s army from behind.
They were fired by the Runan Yellow Turban Army, supposedly Lu Bu’s valuable allies. But in truth, they had been loyal to Cao Cao all along, operating under the command of Xun Yu, the Second Genius.
The flag Sima Yi handed over carried the phrase “Heaven is dead,” a dark inversion of the Yellow Turbans’ original slogan:
“The Blue Sky is dead; the Yellow Sky shall rise.”
It was a quiet announcement that the Yellow Turban remnants had switched sides.
Chen Gong’s troops were slaughtered, and he suffered a devastating defeat.

​

Back in Puyang, Lu Bu personally confronted Cao Cao. The Sima clan’s Handicapped Warriors intervened, not only protecting Cao Cao but even wounding Lu Bu. Using the secret underground passageways—constructed by the Sima clan themselves—Cao Cao escaped the burning city.

The cities in ancient China had a lot of secret passageways under their walls, and the clan that built those passageways was the Sima clan, and not just passageways, the Sima clan was a very rich clan because they could build anything, even walls.
From the outside, it looked like Cao Cao was constantly being saved by his strategists.
But the truth is more twisted.
Everything—the Xuzhou massacres, the false deaths, the self-sabotage, and even Lu Bu’s trap—was part of Cao Cao (and Guo Jia)’s long-term strategy.
He needed:
                        * His weak-hearted elites purged through crisis,

                        * Disloyal noble families exposed,

                        * His remaining followers hardened through fire,

                        * And the perfect environment for Guo Jia’s doctrine:
“A tyrant in war, a benevolent ruler in peace.”

Cao Cao was not a reactive warlord—he was engineering an empire through controlled catastrophe.

 Guo Jia’s influence during this arc serves multiple long-term objectives:
1. Reinstate Sima Yi into Cao Cao’s circle
Guo Jia knew Sima Yi would one day be essential in countering Zhuge Liang, the greatest strategic threat in China. Even after Sima Yi inadvertently contributed to the defeat in the Xuzhou arc, Guo Jia ensured he regained Cao Cao’s trust.
2. Secure Xun Yu’s integration into Cao Cao’s faction
Although their philosophies clashed—Guo Jia favored ruthless expediency, Xun Yu benevolent order—Guo Jia needed Xun Yu as Cao Cao’s future “good face.”
                           * Guo Jia = Tyrant during war

                           * Xun Yu = Benevolent ruler during peace

This duality was the foundation of Cao Cao’s future legitimacy.
3. Deceive the powerful Yuan clan
Yuan Shao’s clan was the most powerful in China. Had Cao Cao appeared too successful, Yuan Shao would have attacked early.
Guo Jia ensured Cao Cao looked weakened, preventing premature conflict.
4. Intentionally provoke Zhuge Liang
Guo Jia slaughtered civilians to bait Zhuge Liang into a contest of wits.
Two outcomes were possible:
                              * Zhuge Liang loses → no more threat.

                              * Zhuge Liang wins → Guo Jia learns his weaknesses and strengths.

Either result benefited Cao Cao’s side.
5. Build internal cohesion before the inevitable war with Yuan Shao
The ordeal in Yan Province forged Cao Cao’s army into a unified, hardened force capable of surviving the coming storm.

3- Wancheng Bloodbath arc
Guo Jia’s role is small in this arc.
Xun Yu and Jia Xu carry most of the strategic weight. Cao Cao’s reputation collapses, and the Sima clan becomes suspected of orchestrating internal damage because of Pang Tong’s schemes (one of the 8 geniuses) who managed to stay hidden.
This presents a serious long-term threat. To prevent the Sima clan from ever destabilizing Cao Cao again, Guo Jia makes a drastic decision: he orchestrates the destruction of the Sima clan itself. By wiping out Sima Yi’s family, he forces Sima Yi into a state of desperation and personal vendetta, ensuring that his only path forward is to seek Cao Cao’s favor. Guo Jia then orders Sima Yi to assassinate Pang Tong’s ally — the true culprit Guo Jia had already identified — using covert operatives. This gives Sima Yi the opportunity to “atone” and regain a place within Cao Cao’s ranks.
Of course, Guo Jia is fully aware of Sima Yi’s real intentions. But with Cao Cao’s reputation in ruins, Guo Jia cannot allow Cao Cao to remain the ultimate power figure just yet. He needs someone else to eventually step into the role of internal pillar — someone ruthless, brilliant, and capable of contending with the era’s most dangerous minds, especially Zhuge Liang. So Guo Jia deliberately allows Sima Yi to climb, shaping him into a pawn who will one day take control of Cao Cao’s faction, stabilize it from within, and face the greatest strategic adversaries of the age. Through this, Guo Jia not only preserves a future weapon for Cao Cao’s line, but also ensures that the clan’s crumbling prestige can one day be rebuilt.

4- Battle of Xiapi

Yuan Shao, Cao Cao’s greatest rival, rules from Yecheng in Ji Province and also controls Bing and Qing Provinces. His ambition pushes him further north toward You Province.

Cao Cao, by contrast, rules Yan and Yu Provinces and effectively controls the Sili District—the Emperor’s territory. The Emperor is a powerless child and essentially a political instrument, so Sili is functionally part of Cao Cao’s domain.

Meanwhile, Lü Bu has seized Xu Province from Liu Bei, making it his base of operations. This province is now Cao Cao’s primary military target.

Another potential target is Yuan Shu’s territory in Yang Province. Yuan Shu’s situation is dire: Lü Bu crushed him when he attempted to seize Xu Province, and Sun Ce stripped him of several key cities. Desperate, Yuan Shu turned to his brother Yuan Shao for help.

There are two problems for Cao Cao:
Cao Cao faces two major strategic dilemmas:
                                 1. He cannot allow Yuan Shao to aid Yuan Shu, as that would undermine his plan to annex Yuan Shu’s territory and expand Yuan Shao’s influence southward.

                                 2. He cannot attack Xu Province without leaving his main city in Yu Province exposed—a perfect opportunity for Yuan Shao to invade from the north.

To resolve both issues simultaneously, Cao Cao sends his top political adviser, Xun Yu, to negotiate with Yuan Shao.
Through the Emperor’s authority, Xun Yu offers Yuan Shao the one prize he has long coveted: You Province. If Yuan Shao moves his army north to occupy it, he will be too busy to interfere in the south.
This would grant Cao Cao:
                                    * Freedom to invade Xu Province and eliminate Lü Bu.

                                    * Assurance that Yuan Shao cannot supply Yuan Shu.

The plan works—almost too well. But then everything spirals out of control.

Lü Bu abducts Xun Yu to prevent Cao Cao and Yuan Shao from forming an alliance. If the two warlords united, Lü Bu would be crushed between them. So he kidnaps Cao Cao’s chief adviser to force suspicion and hostility between the two sides.
Cao Cao believes Yuan Shao murdered Xun Yu, and war between the two factions becomes imminent.
If war breaks out, Cao Cao must pull his troops from Nanyang (in Jing Province) back to defend the Yan–Ji border. This leaves Nanyang vulnerable—allowing Zhang Xiu to reclaim it and potentially strike at Cao Cao’s main city while he’s busy fighting Lü Bu.

Back in Cao Cao's camp, Sima Yi offered Liu Chong's head to Cao Cao, asking for Cao Cao's forgiveness for the clan's betrayal.

Cao Cao accepted, as Sima Yi's talents would be of use to him, and ordered him to help Guo Jia in the invasion of Xu Province.

In Nanyang, Jia Xu—now serving Cao Cao—manages to persuade Zhang Xiu to surrender. This eliminates the threat to Cao Cao’s rear and even allows the recovery of rations stuck in Nanyang, thanks to Sima Yi.

Cao Cao’s southern front moves toward Xiapi.
Xiahou Dun’s army is ambushed by Gao Shun, one of Lü Bu’s elite generals. Xiahou deploys archers on his left flank, but Lü Bu’s cavalry tears through them.

Xiahou moves the archers to the front of the left flank to arrow down Gao Shun's cavalry.
Xiahou Dun's entire left flank is wiped out, but he still manages to survive, and even kills the leader of the horse archers, as they have no arrows left.
With their leader killed and no arrows left, they retreat to Xiapi, and Xiahou Dun is saved by the remnants of his army.

Xiahou Yuan, not expecting that Xiahou Dun would lose to the Lu Bu's cavalry, also gets his army ambushed by Gao Shun.

Xiahou Yuan is forced to retreat.

Instead of pursuing Xiahou Yuan's army, Gao Shun chooses to attack Cao Cao's rear camp where the rations are stored, and it is also revealed that the forces of Mount Tai, previously allies of Cao Cao, have switched to the Lu Bu side and are blocking the main route for the rations,

The carts, instead of carrying rations, are full of flammable materials that block the entire valley road, making it impossible for Gao Shun to continue.This was Sima Yi’s trap. Anticipating Gao Shun’s moves, he had already moved the real rations to Yu Jin’s southern camp and prepared a fire barrier to halt any pursuit. Scouts divert Mount Tai’s forces, completing the deception.

In the Outer City, Sima Lang is revealed as a secret investor in Xu, and three former Dong Zhuo generals—now serving Lü Bu—have secretly been spying on him to avenge their fallen master.
They reveal that Xun Yu is alive and offer to help rescue him. They succeed, but burn the location afterward to frame Gao Shun’s failure to guard it.

Xun Yu, who Lü Bu had occasionally consulted for military advice, now provides Cao Cao with detailed intelligence.
Sima Yi, Xun Yu and Guo Jia find the rescue suspiciously convenient and orders surveillance of a certain man—possibly Wei Xu, not Chen Gong. Wei Xu is later revealed to be secretly working for Chen Gong.

Chen Gong orchestrated everything:
                                       * Planting false intelligence about Lü Bu’s archer numbers

                                       * Manipulating the rescue of Xun Yu

                                       * Setting up Gao Shun’s temporary “demotion”

                                       * Preparing a timed counterstrike

The trap works perfectly.
Yu Jin charges forward expecting few archers and is crushed by a wall of arrows. Retreating, he runs straight into Gao Shun’s ambush.
Han Hao and Xu Huang are defeated by Mount Tai’s forces. Cao Cao’s army suffers devastating losses in a single day.

Unable to match Lü Bu’s cavalry head-on, Cao Cao’s army digs pitfalls across the battlefield. Even then, supplies are lost, morale is low, and both Mount Tai and Zhang Liao march toward Xiao Pass—Cao Cao’s critical supply corridor.
Zhang Liao, blocked by Guo Jia’s pitfalls, heads south to join Mount Tai.
Xu Chu challenges and defeats all four Mount Tai generals, disorganizing their forces. Zhang Liao orders a withdrawal.

Only 1 day after Cao Cao began to attack the Xu Province with all his forces, many of his generals lost to the insanely strong cavalry of Gao Shun, and he even lost a camp of rations. After these brutal losses, both the Mount Tai and Zhang Liao armies march towards the Xiao Pass, which is Cao Cao's buffer zone and where he is now.
The Xiao Pass is probably the most important place for Cao Cao, as it's the place where he can transport his rations from the Yu Province, if Lu Bu takes it, we can say it's pretty much over for Cao Cao, as he is taking a lot of Ls, and losing his buffer zone would be RIP.
The Zhang Liao's army coming from the east is trapped by the Guo Jia's pitfalls

Zhang Liao realises that it's impossible to move his cavalry across such terrible terrain, so he moves most of his army to south to help the Mount Tai forces take the Xiao Pass, leaving the rest to help the victims of thepitfall.

Sima Yi meets Lü Bu in Xiapi, claiming to seek revenge on Cao Cao for destroying the Sima clan. Zhang Liao questions his loyalty, but Sima Yi speaks in metaphors about “raising a tiger only to invite disaster”—a reference to Lü Bu’s betrayal of Dong Zhuo.
Sima Yi explains Cao Cao’s dire situation: losses everywhere and the rainy season approaching. He predicts Cao Cao will soon retreat south.
Gao Shun appears with a map showing all of Guo Jia’s traps—given to him by Sima Yi and verified on the field. With this proof, Lü Bu trusts Sima Yi.
Sima Yi then outlines his plan:
                                          * He will return to Yan Province and make Cao Cao’s retreat difficult.

                                          * As Cao Cao pulls back, Xiao Pass will weaken.

                                          * Lü Bu can then seize it, cutting Cao Cao’s supply line and winning the war.

Lü Bu agrees and sends Yuan Shu’s troops to hold the southern front.

During the rainy season, Jia Xu abruptly announces that Cao Cao’s work in Xu Province is “done,” using deliberately misleading language to disguise their true intentions.

The pitfalls Guo Jia had ordered earlier were never meant to trap cavalry. They were arranged in a specific pattern near Xiapi. When the rains came, the pits would channel the swelling waters of the Si River directly into the city. But Chen Gong had placed Gao Shun near the river. So after Cao Cao’s numerous L, he immediately adapted and sent Sima Yi to “negotiate” with Lu Bu with the pretense of vengeance for the Sima clan’s slaughter. Lu Bu had no way to realize that Sima Yi was actually helping Guo Jia despite the atrocities, this is why the following trap will work.
Lü Bu ended up committing all his forces to Xiao Pass—clearing the riverbank so the engineers could dig and connect the Si River to the pitfalls.
When the floods hit, Xiapi becomes a water-locked prison, trapping all of Lü Bu’s supplies inside.

Chen Gong realizes the trap and urges Lü Bu to retreat. But when Chen Gong seeks support from Yuan Shu’s troops, he finds them pulling back to Shouchun—because Xun Yu convinced Yuan Shu to break the alliance.
Cao Cao and Yuan Shu even form a non-aggression pact. All escape routes close. Lü Bu becomes a Trapped Beast—isolated and doomed.

Back in Xiaopei, Sima Yi cleverly held Lu Bu's Wei Xu, which is now Lu Bu's brother-in-law, and a very trusted ally of Cheng Gong, as a hostage for Sima Yi's future plans.

This raises old but relevant questions. In any “meritocratic” or “aristocratic” system, who decides what counts as talent or excellence? Is there an absolute measure? Or is everything contingent on people agreeing on the criteria — criteria that someone must set, interpret, and enforce? Even if we ignore these theoretical matters and accept that “merit” includes prevailing in real power struggles — the idea behind “an antihero in chaos, a loyal minister in peace” — then rulership becomes an endless competition. Whoever is on top is simply the latest winner, echoing Xunzi’s rebuttal of Mencius’s claim that anyone could become a Yao or Shun.
Perhaps the very concept of “sagely rulership” is part of the problem: it dresses up domination in moral legitimacy. If ruling is just hegemony with extra steps, why fight for the throne at all? Why insist on unifying the realm under one banner, only to burden one’s children with the fallout?
What Guo Jia ultimately implies is that raw technical superiority is not enough to rule. Power sustained solely by ability teaches only that “the ruler is who happened to win,” and someone else can always replace him. What’s required is ideological sophistication — justified lineage, loyalty narratives, moral claims — tools that make authority feel legitimate rather than merely dominant.
From here, the conversation circles back to virtue. True virtue isn’t dependent on who sits on the throne. As Xunzi famously said, Heaven is constant and does not care who rules; individuals remain responsible for their own moral conduct whether they serve a sage king or a tyrant. The real issue isn’t virtue or ability per se, but the attempt to use them to justify domination — turning them into political alibis.
Legalist thinkers like Han Feizi would scoff at the moral debate entirely: people must be controlled to produce value, and control is best achieved through a central authority enforcing strict law.

6 — Post-Death Contingencies: Red Cliff
After the victory at Guandu, Guo Jia already understood he would not live to see the inevitable confrontation between Cao Cao’s northern armies and the southern coalition led by Zhou Yu — one of the Eight Geniuses and a master of naval warfare. He also predicted an abnormally large flood on the Han River that year.
Why the Flood Matters
The Han River’s banks were heavily fortified by Liu Biao. Normally, the river is far too shallow for large fleets, making a northern invasion by water practically suicidal. Even with Cao Cao’s superior manpower and resources, attacking through a shallow river guarded by Liu Biao’s ships would guarantee defeat.
However, the geniuses — Zhuge Liang, Jia Xu, Guo Jia, Zhou Yu, and others — all knew the Han River experiences an annual flood. Usually this rise in water level is still insufficient for warships. But in the specific year of the Battle of Red Cliff, the flood was predicted to be extraordinarily high, temporarily making the river navigable.
This foretold flood becomes the centerpiece of Guo Jia’s contingency planning.
The Problem After Guo Jia’s Death
Once Guo Jia was gone, Cao Cao’s faction would lack anyone capable of predicting weather patterns, tides, and climate shifts. Their enemies, however — Zhou Yu and Zhuge Liang — would still possess that advantage.
To compensate, Guo Jia secretly seized the talents of Zuo Ci, the only remaining weather-reader from the defeated Yuan clan. He hid Zuo Ci’s existence completely. Zhou Yu and Zhuge Liang would therefore falsely assume that Cao Cao’s side had no weather specialists, making them overconfident in using meteorological traps. Meanwhile, Jia Xu — whom Guo Jia intended as the leading strategist after his death — would be able to counter these traps using Zuo Ci’s concealed predictions.
Anticipating Zhao Yun's Possible Defection
Guo Jia also anticipated that Zhao Yun, the strongest warrior in Cao Cao’s faction, might one day turn against them due to manipulation by enemy strategists. To neutralize this risk, he adapted Yuan Fang’s famous counter-formation — the one that once defeated him — into a deadly anti-elite strategy capable of killing Zhao Yun or any other top-tier soldier.
This level of foresight was so extreme that even Zhuge Liang would have been neutralized had fate not intervened.

Accepting the Worst-Case Scenario: Cao Cao’s Loss
Guo Jia calculated that Cao Cao might lose at Red Cliff, for several reasons:
                                                                                                * Zhuge Liang would only enter a battle if he were absolutely certain of victory.

                                                                                                * Zhou Yu’s mastery of naval warfare, especially in home territory.

                                                                                                * The risk of Zhao Yun’s defection undermining Sima Yi’s position.

                                                                                                * Zuo Ci possibly seeking revenge for Yuan Fang’s death by sabotaging Cao Cao.

With these variables, Guo Jia built a multilayered posthumous scheme.

8 – Overall: (Guo Jia’s scaling)

Counterability: Guo Jia is just in his own tier in counterability, each and every action he takes has extremely high counterability, against such strong opponents; unlike Zhou Yu and Jia Xu who just have very adaptable tactics (they are still smart as hell), but what distinguishes Guo Jia with these characters is his ability to thinking holistically. He always thinks of the bigger picture, looking out for dangerous variables in all possible manner, preparing troop training programs for an incoming battle he foresees, Guan Du aftermath countering alone shows this. Being able to pull off multiple contingencies and get Zhuge Liang (who has the best foresight+risk calculation in the verse) to be cautious AFTER his own death alone tells us his great danger. Zhuge Liang states himself that he was only able to comfortably come out of hiding because Guo Jia had died from the plague, every time Zhuge Liang even makes a small action behind the scenes, there’s an increasingly greater risk of being seen through and foreseen by Guo Jia, resulting in having to confront very tanky counters. Meanwhile the rest of the geniuses were easy for Zhuge Liang to manipulate and orchestrate. Guo Jia was the real deal and heck of a threat.
