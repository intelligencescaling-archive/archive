To create both a conventional and a light bomb with a precise calculation of the kill zone to millimetres alone requires a combination of advanced knowledge from physics, chemistry, mathematics and engineering. In physics, this includes gas dynamics to model the shock wave, thermodynamics to calculate the energy of the explosion, detonation theory (Chapman-Jouguet, Sedov-Taylor) to describe the explosion front and its propagation in the medium, and continuum mechanics to assess the impact on solid objects. In mathematics - partial differential equations, numerical methods and CAD modelling (e.g. ANSYS, COMSOL). In engineering - knowledge of explosive design, initiation systems, material interactions and precise component synchronisation. In chemistry, a detailed understanding of explosives properties, sensitivity, composition, and thermochemical characteristics. A light bomb also requires knowledge of optics, photometry, and the bioeffects of light (e.g., blinding). For a highly accurate calculation of the kill zone, it requires consideration of environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, pressure, reflective surfaces) and the use of computer modelling and experimental data.
Abstract thinking is essential in the design and calculation of explosive devices because it allows us to operate with invisible, multi-layered concepts, to model processes that cannot be directly observed, and to keep complex relationships between variables in mind. For example, a shock wave does not propagate in a straight line, but depends on the shape of the charge, the properties of the medium, and the configuration of space - something that only someone with an advanced abstract mind can visualise without visual cues. It is also necessary to mentally model how changing one parameter - the composition of the explosive, the shape of the casing or the angle of reflection - will affect dozens of other characteristics such as pressure, velocity, momentum and light emission. Millimetre-level calculations require building ideal mathematical models and manipulating them without physical objects, combining physical laws and probabilistic scenarios. In addition, abstract thinking allows you to find non-standard engineering solutions, anticipate side effects and design errors before they actually occur. Without it, it is impossible to combine knowledge from different disciplines (physics, chemistry, biomechanics, mathematics, programming) into a single coherent model of a device. Therefore, abstract thinking is not just useful, but critically necessary at all stages - from idea to calculation and final modelling.

Visualisation is necessary because it allows us to visualise in our minds physical processes that cannot be observed directly, and thereby better understand, predict and accurately model the behaviour of a complex system - for example, an explosion. A shock wave is not visible to the eye, but its shape, speed of propagation, reflections off surfaces and interaction with objects must be clearly represented in the mind so that the consequences can be calculated to the nearest millimetre. Visualisation helps to retain multi-dimensional structures in the mind: the sphere of impact, the trajectories of fragments, the propagation of light and heat, the time delays between system components. This requires not just a static picture, but a dynamic internal cinema, where the engineer "sees" the explosion in real time, in slow motion, in different environments and with different parameters. This allows prediction of unexpected effects, such as shock wave focusing or light re-reflection. Visualisation is also critical for design - even before drawings, a person builds a device in their head, mentally assembles it, checks how components interact, where failures may occur, and how this will affect efficiency. Without the ability to visualise the inner workings, mass distribution, charge shape and directional effects, it is impossible to achieve high accuracy in calculations or in the design itself.
