Explaining subaru’s complexity in a way ppl actually get why he’s a top tier in fiction  requires someone to frame him as more than a surface level character. Subaru is a narrative experiment in what happens when you take the human condition and force it into a paradox. The paradox is this: he has infinite and limitless possibilities and chances but with zero freedom. Every reset looks like a choice but it’s just another prison where he alone carries the memory of death. This creates the central theme of rezero which is agency under absurdity, same thing where camus wrote about with sisyphus; the stone rolls back down every time. But what makes Subaru different is that he doesn’t push it just to merely escape and prevent his own predestined fate that leads him to his demise, but he pushes it to also keep other people alive. His suffering is not a bug in the story but instead the main point of it. Rezero asks if a human can retain identity, empathy and sanity when condemned to eternal recurrence with no acknowledgment and no validation, not even from god (this is much more shown and explained in arc 6). That's why his breakdowns are essential. Every time he breaks it’s the story testing if the peak of humanity itself can survive in the face of total absurdity because Subaru IS the embodiment of peak humanity. The answer Rezero gives is a yes, but barely. And only through genuine bonds and relationships could Subaru achieve such a thing, such as genuine aid and support from others, will you be able to strengthen yourself and become mentally motivated despite numerous burdens and suffering. That’s why Subaru's complexity is deeper than other people generally give it credit for. he’s one of the first characters written to embody the philosophy of narrative weight itself, he’s a human trapped as the load-bearing pillar of the entire world. he’s not just a protagonist for the sake of having to be a protagonist, he’s the glue that makes every other character’s existences and purpose even narratively possible. He's literally fiction proving that suffering, choice, love is enough to create meaning even when the universe strips everything else away. That's what makes him modern-philosophy-tier in terms of writing. Subaru IS rezero and rezero IS Subaru. The story doesn't properly function without Subaru because Rezero and Subaru are so intertwined and connected, providing more parallels and characteristics to its characters by creating other characters that serve as other versions of Subaru if their circumstances were different and they had gone into different paths, and vice versa.

Subarus 5 stages of grief:
**1. Denial **
Subaru tries to deny his weakness and his lack of personality. He does this by trying to convince himself that he is an Isekai MC hero. It should be noted that Subaru ideology on how personality is built is flawed. He believes that how he is as a man and his achievements makes him who he is. He acts as they do, trying to mimic their behaviors and traits. He is trying to build something so he can say that he truly is a person. A strong, reliable hero. After each moment of weakness he tries to console himself by feeding the delusion that he "will get strong". The thing is he never does. He treats this as a video game trying to make any excuses to his faults. He tries his best to push these delusions further anytime something bad happens. He still knows many of his failures are due to his weakness yet he still tries to hold on trying to shirk responsibility. He looks up to people like Julius, the finest of knights; Reinhard, a figure who is known among many to be the strongest; and Roswaal, the greatest magician. Subaru tries to be like them. He wants to believe that he will have the recognition that Reinhard has, the chivalry of Julius, and the generational talent that Roswaal has. Most of all he wants to fit the masculine image of a strong man who can defend his loved ones and be impenetrable. Rem stokes this delusion (sort of) by calling Subaru her hero. He believes her and tries to hold on to this statement. This is a problem because Subaru believes he must fit this hero figure. Subaru wants to meet these expectations of a hero, this would reaffirm that he is a valuable person with actual personality. He clings to any wins and statements that confirm his greatness.
When everything boils over during his fight with Emilia he tries to use his victory as justification for his actions. He is far into this delusion that he is a hero. That is when he is smacked out of his denial by Emilia's speech. He was being selfish, he was in the wrong. He wasn't an isekai MC who was always correct and as Emilia left him the facade of being an Isekai MC shattered. He was never a knight in shining armor but an insecure boy in a new world with no strength. This is when the story starts slowly breaking down Subaru’s character forcing him to confront this fear of being a nobody with no actual personality.
2. Anger
Subaru attempts to blame everyone and everything around him. This is shown when Reinhard tries smoothing things out. Subaru rejects this offer quickly and angrily. This highlights Subaru's fragile mental state. His strong grief has transformed into a quick and hot anger. He views Reinhard's act of kindness as patronizing. There are many other show cases of this anger. During one of his failed loops he attempts to get the help of Crusch. She notes that his anger towards the witch cult is blinding him from his main goal to save Emilia. The anger that he harbored clouded his judgement, making forget everything that was once important and only focus on revenge. This is also exemplified during his outbreak when he was detained by Pettleguese. He mocks Subaru for his fake madness. He is told that it was all due to his Sloth. This leaves Subaru with less and less excuses to be angry at the world for.  Subaru was angry toward the witchcult, but he was also angry with himself and his inability to help the people around him. These bouts of anger continue as he fails his negotiations with Crusch and Anastasia. During this time all Subaru feels is intense anger and despair over the situation. This anger is directed not only toward his own failures but the actions of other people. He questions everything such as the candidate’s “mean” behavior toward him. He questions why it is him who is being tortured by these loops. As he fails to get the help of both of the candidates he goes through the stage of bargaining.
3. Bargaining
During this stage Subaru tries to find a way out of this pit. Not only from the shitty situation but from himself. He now knows he isn't enough and it is clearly painted out. He tries desperately to fix the situation not only for the other people but himself. He goes to the last of the royal candidates in an attempt to salvage the rotting situation. He is willing to do anything to get something about the situation done. Subaru is trying to gain control over the situation. He is even willing to sacrifice his own pride just to reach this goal. This should be an obviously wrong choice but Subaru continues because that is what he could give up at the moment. Subaru’s desperation causes his willingness to give up anything at a moment's notice to reach what he views as a good outcome. Him almost getting Emilia dropped from the royal selection with his interactions of the other candidates. He almost licks Priscilla's foot just to get out from this pit he has dug. These sacrifices are in vain as it never directly leads to a positive outcome as it was all just failures in Subaru’s eyes. This makes Subaru fall into a state of depression.
4. Depression
This is the most apparent of Subaru’s stages of grief. He is completely mentally destroyed. Subaru nearly completely gives up without considering every possibility. His depression is also exemplified by his almost complete unwillingness to keep on going. It should be noted that the immediate trauma from deaths didn’t entirely cause this. Subaru’s mental state crumples due to the failures stacking up and the feelings of hopelessness. Subaru has been able to come back from very traumatic experiences such as when he dies in very gory ways in arc 5 but he was able to do much better because of his better mental state. The tipping point for Subaru is during his talk with Rem. At this point Subaru is 1000% sure that he will never be a strong hero who can challenge this adverse situation, but he never fully accepts this fact. He anguishes over it as he talks to Rem. Subaru’s self-hatred and loathing stems from this depression. In his talk with Rem he says how he has no personality (Due to his flawed perception of it). Subaru highlights all his failures during this dialogue which is the source of his grief. Rem counters this with what great things he has done. This leads to Subaru’s acceptance.
5. Acceptance
As Rem tells Subaru about the good things he starts to realize he did great things. His bias against himself was due to his self-hatred and low self-esteem. As Rem starts telling him what is good about himself, he starts seeing the good side. He looks at himself not from the flawed lenses which his ideology gave him but from a healthier stand point. He isn’t physically able to win but he can rally people together and help create a cohesive group. He accepted the fact that he can’t be a strong figure who can solve problems by his own strength. This acceptance isn’t absolute as he still has moments where he feels that he doesn’t do enough. Rem’s speech was very helpful but wasn’t a full cure to Subaru’s condition. A common misconception is that Subaru simply got better after that speech, which isn’t true. Subaru was simply able to be mentally well enough to put up his usual happy act. This time it isn’t a fake personality but simply hiding his fear and pain. Subaru initially had been faking everything entirely but now this is the actual Subaru. He isn’t some strong knight who can solve every problem with ease. He isn’t a bloodline talent, he is a strategist and someone who helps out in battle not with pure strength but with insights. He has relapses where he feels very similar self-hatred to his depressive state. It isn’t a linear path to self-acceptance. Subaru is literally starting from zero at this moment. His actual self is coming out and giving a hand during the battle. He let go of his desire to be an Isekai MC. He is slowly changing himself for the better.
Conclusion
Subaru’s 5 stages of grief works very well as a way to develop Subaru as a character while adding nuances to these changes. Subaru’s development isn’t truly done and this conflict isn’t simply ended. His character arc is a show of how flawed ideologies can build up and can hurt you. It also shows how you can grow out. It also is a criticism of the classic Isekai trope. Subaru was actually wrong, but he can change.

Different philosophies of Subaru Natsuki:

1. Eclecticism (impure and pure)
Subaru is a markedly eclectic character. His behavior mixes elements from different philosophical currents, depending on the situation. In his most impulsive moments, he adopts an impure eclecticism, guided by intense emotions and contradictions. Over time, he matures toward a purer eclecticism, where he combines ideas and strategies in a more coherent and flexible way, adapting to his environment without losing his internal compass.

2. Existentialism
His recurring death forces him to constantly question himself: what is the point of continuing to live if everything repeats itself?
Subaru faces existential emptiness, the weight of freedom, and the need to create his own meaning. As an existentialist character, his growth comes from suffering, responsibility, and personal choice in the face of absurdity.

3. Hermeticism
The series is riddled with hermetic symbols such as destiny, causality, and occult knowledge. Subaru is a seeker of inner wisdom: he needs to understand the logic of the world, but also to confront his inner self, his “shadow,” and be reborn from it, as a kind of spiritual alchemy.

4. Humanism
Despite his pain, Subaru never loses his humanity. He defends the dignity of others, seeks love, and wants to live in a world where people matter. His empathy and struggle to save those he loves make him a defender of the human experience as the core of value.

5. Complex utilitarianism
Subaru tries to maximize the well-being of others, even if it means sacrificing himself. However, his ethics aren't purely utilitarian: they're selective, nuanced, emotional. He finds himself in constant dilemmas where he must choose between the lesser evil and the unattainable good, knowing that whatever he does, someone will suffer.
6. Stoicism (under stress)
Throughout the story, Subaru learns to endure suffering with greater fortitude. He tries to master his emotions, control what depends on him, and resist the inevitable. However, his stoicism is imperfect: there are relapses, crises, but also constant learning.

7. Nihilism (transitory)
There are moments when Subaru falls into total despair. He believes that nothing makes sense, that no one can understand him, that his efforts are futile. It is an emotional and existential nihilism that brings him to the brink of collapse, but also to the threshold of change.

8. Absurdism
Subaru lives in a reality that defies conventional logic: dying and coming back, without explanation, without justice. Faced with the absurd, he struggles, suffers, and sometimes laughs with dark humor. His story embodies the tension between the desire for meaning and a universe that does not guarantee it.

9. Cynicism
Over time, Subaru stops idealizing authority figures or the magical world he finds himself in. He criticizes power structures, rejects manipulation, and becomes more cautious and sharp. This cynicism stems from the loss of innocence, but it does not turn into indifference.

10. Redemptionism
A central motivation for Subaru is redemption. He wants to correct his mistakes, repair the damage he has caused, and be better for others. This quest pushes him to evolve and gives him strength when all seems lost. Redemption, in his case, is both personal and relational.
11. Impure Realism
Subaru starts out as a dreamer, but with each death, each failure, his view of the world becomes more stark. He learns to see reality as it is, without idealization, but without surrendering to it. He recognizes his mistakes, his limitations, and the moral complexity of the world around him.

12. Impure rationalism
Subaru is an evolving strategist. With each cycle, he learns, analyzes, and makes plans. However, his reasoning is deeply influenced by his emotions, his bonds, and his traumas. This makes his rationality human, imperfect, but powerful.

13. Tragic morality
Subaru often faces decisions where there is no clear “right” choice, only the possibility of a lesser evil. This moral conflict places him in a tragic narrative: his choices always come at a cost, and yet he must choose. He cannot run away from responsibility, which makes him ethically heroic.

14. Phenomenology
Subaru is the only one who remembers every timeline. His experience is subjective and unique, which separates him from the rest of the world. He lives in a personal dimension of time and suffering, which makes him a deeply phenomenological figure: reality, for him, is what he experiences, not what others understand.
15. Authenticity (Heidegger / Sartre)
Subaru, when facing his fears and contradictions, struggles to live authentically, that is, to be true to who he really is, not to what others expect. In many moments he acts “for the sake of others,” but as he matures, he begins to act out of internal conviction, not external validation.

16. Categorical Imperative (Kant)
Although Subaru does not always act out of “duty,” he is constantly faced with decisions where he could apply this Kantian ethic:

What if everyone did what I am going to do?
At key moments, Subaru acts not out of convenience, but because he believes it is the right thing to do, even if it costs him dearly.

Samsara: the cycle of suffering and rebirth (very literal with his deaths).

Dukkha: the suffering inherent in existence.

Renunciation of the ego: Subaru has to abandon his pride many times.

Progressive enlightenment: he learns to detach himself from the desire for control and possession.

18. Determinism vs. free will
Subaru lives in constant tension between feeling trapped by fate (the curse of returning from death) and his desire to exercise free will. The series plays with this duality: is he predestined to suffer? Or can he change the future with his decisions?
19. Philosophy of personal identity
Subaru changes with each death, experience, and choice. Is he still the same after everything he has been through? Where is Subaru's “self”? This relates to thinkers such as Locke and Parfit, who raise questions about the continuity of the self through time and change.

20. Ethics of Care
His relationships with Emilia, Rem, Beatrice, and others reflect an ethic that prioritizes emotional bonds, empathy, and mutual care over abstract principles. This ethic—closer to a feminist or communitarian view of morality—is especially evident in how he strives to protect and understand others, not only out of duty, but out of love.

21. The eternal return (Nietzsche)
Subaru literally lives the idea of the eternal return: repeating the same events over and over again. Nietzsche asks:

Would you accept to live this same life eternally, with every pain and every joy, without changing anything?
Subaru, over time, begins to affirm his existence despite suffering, which aligns with this philosophy.

22. Anxiety (Kierkegaard)
Kierkegaard speaks of anxiety as the vertigo of freedom. Subaru fully embodies this:
He can choose, but he knows that every choice can lead to death, pain, or betrayal. That freedom suffocates him, but it also builds him up.
23. Heroic tragedy (Nietzsche / classical literature)
Subaru is a tragic hero: he knows that suffering is inevitable, but he still chooses to act, resist, and love. In this, he resembles the Dionysian hero who embraces pain as part of an aesthetic and affirmative life.

24. Ethical altruism vs. self-destruction
A very fine line in Subaru is:

Is he sacrificing himself for others... or because he hates his own life?
This tension between ethical altruism (giving out of love) and emotional self-destruction (giving because he doesn't feel valuable) is very present, especially in the darker arcs.

25. Narrative identity (Paul Ricoeur)
According to this theory, the “self” is constructed as a story we tell ourselves.
Subaru rewrites his identity in every loop, every failure, every relationship. The Subaru of episode 1 and the Subaru of the end of season 2 are physically the same being, but they have radically different internal stories.

26. Ethics of compassion (Schopenhauer)
Although Schopenhauer is pessimistic, he believes that compassion is the only possible basis for morality. Subaru, when he acts out of love and real compassion (and not guilt or ego), comes close to this way of thinking:

He does not seek reward, only that the other person does not suffer.
27. Redemptive suffering (Philosophical Christianity)
Without getting into religion, Subaru takes on the role of a redeeming figure: his pain saves others.
There is a kind of “existential cross” that he chooses to bear, even though it brings him no glory, because he believes it is worth doing. This can be interpreted as a symbolic redeemer figure.

28. Hegelian dialectic (Thesis - Antithesis - Synthesis)
Subaru represents this cycle:

Thesis: his naive view of the world (saving everyone, being the hero).

Antithesis: the brutal clash with reality, death, failure.

29. The actor's paradox (Hegel / Kierkegaard)
Subaru is caught in an existential paradox: he must act to change the future, but his actions are themselves an expression of his suffering and despair. In this sense, his being is contradiction. This concept can be seen in the work of Hegel or Kierkegaard, who describe how people face the contradiction between being and doing when they try to live authentically.

30. The struggle for meaning (Viktor Frankl)
Frankl, in his book Man's Search for Meaning, argues that even in the most extreme circumstances, people can find purpose. Subaru represents this struggle when he faces utter despair but continues to search for the meaning of his suffering. He does not simply run away from pain, but faces it with the hope of giving it meaning.

31. The Absurd and Rebellion (Albert Camus)
Camus' concept of absurdity and rebellion can be perfectly applied to Subaru. Although he knows that his struggle seems absurd—to live again only to die and suffer over and over—he does not give up. Camus would say that Subaru, by choosing to carry on despite the absurdity, is rebelling against the common sense of the universe and asserting his freedom in a meaningless context.

32. The concept of “the Other” (Levinas)
The philosopher Emmanuel Levinas argued that encountering the other (otherness) is essential to our ethics. Subaru lives in constant relationship with the other (Emilia, Rem, Beatrice, etc.). Throughout the series, his understanding of the ethics of the other changes: it is not just about saving others out of an abstract duty, but out of his direct responsibility towards them, which allows him to reach a deeper understanding of himself through his relationship with others.

33. The “death of the self” (Nietzsche)
As Subaru dies over and over again, he faces the dissolution of his original identity. This process of continuous death can be seen as a form of “death of the self,” similar to what Nietzsche proposes as the overcoming of the limited ego. Subaru not only experiences literal death, but also undergoes a transformation of being, where his “self” is repeatedly reconstructed.
34. The distortion of time (Henri Bergson)
Bergson, philosopher of duration, reflects on how lived time is different from time measured by a clock. Subaru, by constantly reliving, experiences time in a distorted way. Each death and rebirth gives him a subjective sense of time, which can be described as an internal “duration” that distorts his perception of his own life and existence.

35. Self-transcendence (Maslow/Jung)
Subaru, through his suffering and constant confrontation with pain, is in a process of self-transcendence. This idea comes from both Maslow (in his pyramid of needs, where self-actualization is achieved through understanding and overcoming) and Carl Jung, who speaks of the integration of the “self” and the acceptance of the shadow (one's dark side). Subaru, by confronting his own traumas and mistakes, has the opportunity to transcend his being and become something greater than his suffering.

36. Eternity in the moment (Heraclitus / Zen)
Heraclitus said that “we cannot step into the same river twice,” reflecting the constant transformation of being. Subaru, although he revives, is never the same, and, in turn, lives each moment as unique and ephemeral. In Zen terms, eternity resides in the present, and Subaru, by living the same situation so many times, finally understands that every moment of his life, even in his suffering, has intrinsic value.
37. The paradox of the tragic hero (Aristotle/Nietzsche)
Subaru is a tragic hero, similar to characters in classical tragedy (such as Oedipus) who, although endowed with virtues, is trapped by his destiny. Through his deaths and failures, he seeks greatness or redemption, but is always doomed to lose something along the way. In Nietzschean terms, this paradox also reflects the will to power, which is confronted with human limitations.

38. The paradox of identity (Derrida)
Jacques Derrida spoke of the indeterminacy of identity, the constant displacement of being. With each death, Subaru experiences a reconfiguration of his identity. Who is Subaru really? The one from before or the one from now? Derrida would say that Subaru is never “completely” present or defined, as his identity is the result of his relationships and experiences, always unfinished and in process.

39. Existential solipsism
Trapped in a cycle where he is the only one aware of past events, Subaru experiences a certain existential solipsism: his consciousness is so focused on himself and his own internal processes that he can lose touch with the outside world. Although he is surrounded by people, no one else shares his experience, creating a unique philosophical isolation.
40. Transcendental guilt (Nietzsche / Heidegger)
Subaru's guilt at not being able to save everyone leads him to transcendal guilt because every loop possibly creates more and more world's, leaving the others behind, causing Subaru to become more burdened transcendly because he is guilty of failing everyone that died in the past loops because they are possibly and permanently dead, there are other factors of Subaru's transcendal guilt not just because of his friends dying, but just the failed loops in of itself serving a reminder of Subaru's struggles and failures.
41. Philosophy of the mind and the “self” (Descartes / Kant / Hume)
In his continuous travels and deaths, Subaru is confronted with the fragility of the “self.” What constitutes Subaru as a ‘self’? Is it his body, his consciousness, or the memory he possesses? This dilemma resonates with the philosophical problems of the “self” raised by Descartes, Kant, and Hume, who discussed the relationship between body, mind, and the nature of being.

Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy that examines the basic structure of reality. It is traditionally seen as the study of mind-independent features of the world, but some theorists view it as an inquiry into the conceptual framework of human understanding. Some philosophers, including Aristotle, designate metaphysics as first philosophy to suggest that it is more fundamental than other forms of philosophical inquiry.
