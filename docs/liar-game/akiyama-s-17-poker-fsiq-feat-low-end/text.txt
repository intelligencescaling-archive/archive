The Rules of the Game
The game 17 Poker follows the rules of normal poker for the most part except a few things. It has 17 cards total instead of a full deck. Secondly it includes a Joker that can take the place of any card on the deck. So if you have a Joker in your hand, you can use it as any of the remaining 16 cards to get the best possible hand from the cards given to you. The other 16 cards include 4 Aces, 4 Jacks, 4 Queens, and 4 Kings. The hierarchy of the hands is as follows:

The shuffling works as follows:
1. The dealer takes out a fresh deck each round.
2. Uses riffle shuffles on it.
3. The dealer asks both players how many cards from the top they wish to cut
Then the betting starts. The exact rules of betting aren’t really relevant here.

High End Interpretation (Objectively wrong)
The highest end assumes that Akiyama shuffles the cards in his head every time the dealer does. This is about 17 cards shuffled every second or so. So it comes out to 8.5-17 operations per second. However this is a complete wank and directly contradicts Akiyama’s strategy. Akiyama stated that he calculated the best number of shuffles and THEN manipulated the dealer to force that number of shuffles. So if he only figured out the order after the 8th hand ended, and manipulated the dealer before the 9th hand started, the dealer didn’t have the opportunity to shuffle in front of him which eliminates the possibility of him matching the dealer’s speed of shuffling.
Mid End Interpretation (Generous but not incorrect)
This is the mid end interpretation of the feat. In this we assume that Akiyama shuffled the cards in his head after the 8th hand ended and before the 9th hand started. Akiyama couldn’t have known the position of any card in the starting order before the 8th hand since the dealer still used Hindu shuffle before. Thus, we know for a fact that Akiyama confirmed the order during the 8th hand itself. This interpretation assumes that after knowing the exact starting order, Akiyama runs the 8 shuffles in his head using the starting order and decides the number of shuffles necessary for him to win. Between him revealing the 8th hand and him interrupting the dealer for the first time in the 9th hand (he asks the dealer to shuffle one more time to get his desired order) there were around 332 words spoken by all the characters.

PSI Calculation
Apparently English speakers speak 120 to 150 words per minute in conversations. Secondly, the syllables per second speed of English and Japanese are 6.19 and 7.84 SPS respectively. Using unitary method we can estimate:

135 x (7.84/6.19) = 170.99 or 171 (Low Ball)

Another source lists 193 WPM as the average Japanese reading out loud speed. (Mid Ball)
Lastly, this article claims 240 WPM as the average Japanese speaking speed. (High Ball)

Using these 3 values we can get a timeframe of
332/171 = 1.94 minutes (Low Ball)
332/193 = 1.72 minutes (Mid Ball)
332/240 = 1.38 minutes (High Ball)

So at the bare minimum, Akiyama had 1.38 minutes (literally assuming no pauses or breaks between speech) to do the following calculation:
17 Cards x 8 Shuffles = 136 position shifts or operations.

Using the 3 timeframes we can get the PSI in terms of Operations per Second.
136/(1.94*60) = 1.168 Ops/Sec (Low Ball)
136/(1.72*60) = 1.318 Ops/Sec (Mid Ball)
136/(1.38*60) = 1.643 Ops/Sec (High Ball)

I personally think that the WPM should be closer to 240. However the Mid Ball estimate is better as it can account for pauses and breaks.

Depending on the methodology you choose, Akiyama has 2x to 2.4x the PSI of the average human IRL, and 1.1x to 1.2x the PSI of the theoretical maximum (using his Mid Ball PSI of 1.318 Ops/Sec). This would put Akiyama in the same ballpark as the peak human in PSI.

WMI Calculation
I believe WMI and overall Memory is where this feat actually shines. To calculate WMI let’s first set a clear methodology to do this. For this feat I will be assuming 17 cards x 8 shuffles as remembering 136 cards (or elements) in a specific sequence as illustrated in the Excel sheets above. WMI is often calculated using a test called digit span. Here a sequence of digits is shown in a short timeframe and the person has to type it out in the exact order or in the reverse order. If we map the card shuffling feat onto this method, we can reasonably assume Akiyama can keep track of 136 digits in his mind within a short amount of time and type them out in the exact order. For this methodology we are considering a single card as one digit to avoid wank.

Benchmarking WMI
The average forward digit span performance is stated to be 5 to 9. The peak forward digit span performance is held by Simon Reinhard which is 240 digits repeated in exact order. This would put Akiyama’s feat of handling 136 cards in his mind at 15x the average human WMI and 0.567x the peak human WMI. This would put Akiyama in the same ballpark as the peak human in WMI.

The Memory Palace technique falls under this. Which is why it is not unreasonable to assume that Akiyama would have had to use some sort of method of loci to remember the 136 card shuffle positions. Which further supports the VSI component of this feat.
