Figuring out the system’s basic axioms is something any observer could do, since the rules themselves are deterministic and already define everything for you. Now I’ll lay out the rules of the Pandemic Game.
R1.

Two of the players will be chosen as "Infected" at the beginning of the game.

R2.

A player can confirm their status by using one of the four "examination rooms".

R3.

When 2 "normal" players "contact" they will each receive one vaccine.

R4.

When 2 "infected" players "contact" nothing happens.

R5.

When a "normal" contacts an "infected", the normal person will become "infected".

R6.

When an "infected" contacts a "vaccine holder" they will become "normal", while the other player loses a vaccine.

R7.

An "infected" who owns vaccines can click "activate" on their watch to become "normal". As a result, the player will lose a vaccine.
And here’s how you can derive the system’s three fundamental axioms from them.
“If 10 players contact each other in a round-robin and one is infected, the virus spreads to only 2 people.”
Derived from: R1 (2 infected start), R4 (IF+IF → nothing), R5 (IF+NM → NM becomes IF)
Explanation:
Only one infected can turn a few normals before meeting another infected or looping ends. Round-robin contact limits total spread a direct result of R4 and R5.

“If two players are infected, the results are inconsistent.”
Derived from: R1, R4, R5
Explanation:
Two infected players can’t infect each other (R4), and each spreads differently to normals (R5), creating uneven, non-linear infection ratios.

“If two infected exist and one actively contacts three normals first, nine become infected.”
Derived from: R1, R5
Explanation:
Because R5 allows infection on every contact, one active infected spreading early to multiple normals triggers a chain reaction before others start perfectly matching the “9 IF” outcome.

These ideas are already built into the system, Akiyama didn’t really have to think hard to find them since the rules themselves explain everything. You got R5 saying IF plus NM makes another IF, that’s how infection spreads. R4 says when two IFs meet nothing happens, so that’s what slows it down. R3 and R6 are about vaccines, how you make them and how they can fix infection. So his first three premises are just different versions of the same thing, one with round-robin order, one with two IFs meeting, one with free contacts that spread fast. The system’s made in a way that those “axioms” aren’t some genius deduction, they’re just what naturally happens when you let the rules play out.
At this point I think most people would agree with me, so I’ll skip over that part. The only actually impressive inductive step in Akiyama’s whole reasoning is the one used to build the needed algorithm. Since you can already read that inductive reasoning in the document above, I’ll just explain it briefly here.
He assumes two players are the initial infected, then applies the game rules (like IF+NM → IF, IF+IF → nothing, etc.) step by step to test possible contact scenarios. For each scenario, he compares the results with the data revealed at the end of the game. The ones that don’t match are discarded, and the single scenario that perfectly fits the rules is accepted as the correct one. In short, he’s basically using a trial-and-error method to logically figure out which two players were infected from the start.
Not sure if you see the problem here, but this kind of reasoning is just as simple, and the algorithm he builds through induction isn’t even generalizable. What makes an inductive inference actually good is how well it can be generalized, and Akiyama’s one totally fails there. Simulating interactions just to tell truth from lies isn’t some hard thing to think through, and it can’t be generalized cause every new situation would force you to redefine the axioms and rebuild the model from scratch. In Pandemic, anyone could’ve figured that out on the spot, grabbed a piece of paper, and tried it themselves. What makes Akiyama seem impressive isn’t deep understanding or insight, it’s just that he can run that deductive simulation in his head.
* If IF + NM → IF, then when X contacts Y and neither gets infected, that means X isn’t infected (NM) and Y isn’t infected (NM) either. That kind of contact is “NM + NM,” so both of them get +1 vaccine (R3).
A line of reasoning like that is something anyone could come up with. Cause this kind of reasoning is just mechanical, there’s nothing deep going on. If IF + NM makes IF, then yeah, if X meets Y and both stay normal, obviously they were both NM so they each get +1 vaccine (R3). That’s not some big brain move, it’s just following the rules like pressing buttons in order. There’s no creativity, no insight and no comprehension, just basic cause and effect. Anyone who actually read the rules once could figure that out, so I honestly don’t get why people think it’s impressive at all.
So is there really nothing else that makes Akiyama’s reasoning good besides all that simulation stuff? Well actually there is, but it totally depends on how you look at it. His deduction is verifiable, and the fact that it’s so mechanical and lacks creativity kinda makes it flawless and impossible to disprove.
