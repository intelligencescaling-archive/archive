The Third Revival Round of the Liar Game tournament is the third opportunity given to losers to re-enter the competition. The game is divided into two groups. Group A takes place in an internet café and is hosted by Silien, while Group B is held in a nightclub and is hosted by Rabelais.
The participants are all losers from Round IV, and those who win in this revival round will move on to Round V.
Rules of the game:
The game in this round is called Bid Poker. Each player is given a tablet and must register their fingerprint on it so that no one else can use it. Each player also receives 300 gold coins, 1 coin equals 1 million yen, meaning each has 300 million yen. Through the tablets, players use these coins to bid on lots of cards. With the cards, players must form a valid hand, such as three of a kind. First, the lots are presented (labeled with letters, e.g., Lot A, Lot B, etc.), and players have 30 minutes to consider. Then, bidding begins in alphabetical order, lasting 5 minutes per lot. Once bidding for a lot ends, the winner (highest bidder) is announced, and the next lot’s bidding begins. (After 5 minutes, the names and bid amounts of all bidders are displayed on the screen.)
This is the deal sequence: announce lots → 30‑minute consideration → bid on Lot A (5 minutes) → announce Lot A winner → bid on Lot B (5 minutes) → announce Lot B winner → and so on.
After the initial bidding (deal phase) ends, the draw phase begins. Players have 10 minutes to decide which cards to discard; this is the “discard phase.” All discarded cards are grouped into lots in the order they are discarded and by player (for example, if Player X is first and discards the 4♦ and 5♦, Lot A will contain 4♦ and 5♦. If Player Y is second and discards only the 3♣, Lot B will contain 3♣). Each player can create one lot per draw phase. When the discard time ends, the lots are announced and players have 30 minutes to consider. Then bidding begins again in alphabetical order, with 5 minutes per lot; this is the “lot auction stage.” Winners are announced, and the next lot begins. After all auctions conclude, the next draw phase begins, repeating this process. There are five draw phases, after which rankings are determined.
If a player cannot form a valid hand (at least a high card) or has fewer than 5 cards in hand, they are unranked, become a “pig,” and must pay a 150 million yen penalty. If two players bid the same amount on a lot, the one who bid first wins the lot.
The first-place player receives a 100 million yen bonus, the second-place player 40 million yen, and the third-place player 10 million yen. The prize increases if the number of “pigs” increases. For example, with two pigs instead of one, first place gets 200 million yen, second 80 million, and third 20 million. With three pigs, first gets 300 million, second 120 million, and third 30 million.
At the end of the game, each player only returns 100 million yen of the initial 300 million; they keep the remainder as winnings.
Violence results in a 100 million yen penalty.
Stealing another player’s tablet also results in a 100 million yen penalty.
Destroying another player’s tablet is not allowed.
Once a bid is submitted, it cannot be changed or canceled.
Players may draw (discard and bid) five times (five draw phases). Players are allowed to discard unwanted cards, but new cards can only be obtained through bidding.
If a player’s discarded cards are bought, they receive the money. If not, the cards return to them.
This is the discard phase process: players choose cards to discard (30 minutes), discard lots are announced, 30 minutes to consider, 5‑minute bidding on Lot A → announce winner → bidding on Lot B, etc. This repeats five times (five draw phases), then the game ends.
Players are ranked according to hand strength, as in regular poker. (Hand rankings from highest to lowest:
• Royal Flush (A‑K‑Q‑J‑10, same suit)
• Straight Flush (five consecutive cards, same suit; not necessarily 10‑A)
• Four of a Kind
• Full House (three of a kind plus a pair; if two full houses, the higher three‑of‑a‑kind wins)
• Flush (five cards same suit, not consecutive)
• Straight (five consecutive cards, not the same suit)
• Three of a Kind
• Two Pair
• One Pair
• High Card (not in any above), this is considered invalid and a “pig.”)
If a player doesn’t have five cards at the end, they automatically rank last and become a “pig.” If a player has six or more cards, their strongest 5‑card hand is chosen.
“Pigs” (last place) are eliminated. All winners advance to Round V.

Restating the rules for the Change Session:
1. Overview
There are 5 card exchange sessions throughout the game.
Each player is allowed to discard cards and bid for new ones during each session.
Players can only receive new cards through bidding.
2. Discard Phase (30 minutes)
Players select unwanted cards to discard.
Each player is allowed to create only one lot per session.
Discarded cards are grouped into lots, named, and ordered based on the discard order:
Example:
Player X discards 4♠ and 5♠, and is the first to discard → Lot A will include 4♠ and 5♠
Player Y discards 3♦, and is the second to discard → Lot B will include 3♦
3. Announcement of Lots & Strategy Consideration Time (30 minutes)
After the discard phase ends, the list of lots (containing the discarded cards) is announced.
Players have 30 minutes to consider their bidding strategy.
4. Bidding Phase for Lots (5 minutes per lot)
The lots are auctioned in alphabetical order: A → B → C…
Each lot is auctioned for 5 minutes.
The highest bidder wins the lot.
If bids are tied → the earlier bidder wins.
After each auction, the winning bidder is announced.
For the original discarder of the lot:
If the lot is bought, they receive the bid amount.
If no one bids, the cards return to the original discarder.
5. Repeat and End
This process is repeated a total of 5 times (5 exchange sessions).
After the 5th exchange session, the game ends, and final rankings are announced.

So what did Akiyama do? What is his plan and purpose?
Explanation of Akiyama’s plan:
Akiyama’s main goal was to achieve first place and use the large prize money to pay off all the debts of the other players (except the Harimoto group). To achieve this, Akiyama needed to accomplish two things: improve his hand to secure first place and thus have enough money to pay off the other players (excluding Harimoto), and distract the Harimoto group from improving their own hands.
To improve his hand, Akiyama needed to do the following:
At the start of the second card exchange round, Akiyama’s cards were: 10♦, 9♦, 7♦, 4♦, A♦
Saeki’s cards (after Kei stole some) were: 3♦, 6♦, A♥, 9♣
Taninaka’s cards (after Kei stole some) were: 2♣, 5♦, J♦, Q♠
From this, if Akiyama could get Taninaka’s 5♦ and Saeki’s 3♦ and 6♦, he would have a Straight Flush 3♦–4♦–5♦–6♦–7♦, which is stronger than Harimoto’s Straight Flush A♠–2♠–3♠–4♠–5♠. This would secure first place for Akiyama. For this to happen, the plan had to unfold as follows:
Akiyama discards his extra A♦, which is bought by Taninaka for 332 coins;
=> This adds 332 coins to Akiyama’s account, making it 596 + 332 = 928 coins.
Taninaka then discards his 5♦, which is bought by Akiyama for 401 coins;
=> This gives Akiyama the 5♦.
Saeki discards 3♦ and 6♦, which are bought by Akiyama for 427 coins;
=> This allows Akiyama to create the Straight Flush and surpass Harimoto, securing first place.
In a normal situation, this plan seems impossible because, apart from the Harimoto group, there isn’t enough trust between the other players to make this work. From Taninaka’s perspective, he would never be foolish enough to give all his money to Akiyama and risk being robbed (buying cards he doesn’t need with all his money, risking losing it all with nothing in return). Similarly, Saeki might be forced into a disadvantageous position, being made a “pig” with no cards left and losing all money, and knowing Akiyama has no cards to help him improve his hand in exchange, Saeki would very likely refuse to discard his cards under normal circumstances.
Akiyama had to create a situation where they would trust him unconditionally. That’s why Akiyama asked Kawai to act out a play in front of the Harimoto group, crafting a very reasonable scenario in which, through Kawai, Akiyama completely manipulated Kei Kimura.
Kimura saw that Kawai was angry at Akiyama because there was no way to help him, and Kawai defected to Harimoto’s group seeking their help. Kimura considered this normal because she believed Akiyama had given up on helping the other players, having made a profit for himself. Trying to help them only increased the risk and reduced his current money unnecessarily. Of course, this was just Kawai’s act, which Akiyama ordered him to perform so Kawai could infiltrate Harimoto’s group. Kimura would not fully trust Kawai, so Akiyama created a very logical and convincing scenario to make Kei believe Kawai was extremely useful to her, and when Kei tried to exploit Kawai for her own gain, that’s when Kei fell into the trap.
Akiyama used the players’ hands to predict their reactions. For example, he predicted Taninaka and Saeki would exchange cards in the third exchange round. If Saeki gave Taninaka Q♣, Taninaka would have a pair of Queens, ranking him out of being a “pig.” Similarly, if Taninaka gave Saeki 3♥, Saeki would have a pair of 3s, also ranked out of being a “pig.”
Akiyama also logically predicted that they would argue over who would discard first when betting all their money on a card, because after the first discarder received money, there was a high risk they would betray and not bet the agreed amount on the other player’s card, intentionally losing to escape with a large amount of money. This would make them bet a middle-range amount, not too low but not too high, to avoid betrayal.
Akiyama sent Kawai to observe and confirm the exact bet amounts they agreed on, or perhaps he instructed Kawai on how to manipulate them. If they agreed on a high bet, Kawai would warn them of the risk of discarding first; if they agreed on a low bet, Kawai would warn them of the risk of having their cards stolen. The goal was to motivate Kei to steal their cards, if the bet was too high, Kei wouldn’t intend to steal, but if the first discarder betrayed after winning a lot of money (not betting on the other’s card), Kei could easily get the card for a very low price because no one else would compete for it.
By setting a middle bet price, Kei felt he had a chance to steal cards and gain a reasonable profit from those who became “pigs.” The money Kei spent on stealing cards was neither too high nor too low. In reality, the Harimoto group spent 357 million to prevent the four players from exchanging cards successfully, and this money was transferred to the accounts of the four players Akiyama wanted to pay off.
Besides helping Harimoto’s group earn prize money from those who became pigs, stealing cards also set the stage for Kei to earn a large amount from Taninaka and Kawai. (As mentioned above, Taninaka could only accept a high bet on Kei’s card because he had no other option after Kei blocked previous card exchanges, putting players in shortage, and manipulated them into not discarding cards. For Kawai, Kei’s successful prevention of discards made Kawai feel he really helped Harimoto’s group, deserved payment, and as a longtime member of Harimoto’s group, he believed he would be rewarded and bet on the cards Mikamoto discarded.)
Taninaka’s successful card theft further boosted Kawai’s confidence in the deal’s success, leading him to bet money.
