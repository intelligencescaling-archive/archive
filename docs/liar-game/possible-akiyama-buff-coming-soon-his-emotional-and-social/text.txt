Possible Akiyama buff coming soon? (brief analysis and predictions)

In light of the recent announcement of a new Liar’s Game sequel accompanying the anime adaptation of the series, a question has arisen regarding whether this upcoming game will favor or disadvantage Akiyama (a character widely recognized and highly ranked in intelligence scaling and in SCP debates) or whether it will simply maintain the status quo.

As a fan of both the series and the character, I naturally hope to see a complex game that allows Akiyama’s feats to be further showcased, potentially providing grounds to elevate his ranking or reinforce defenses supporting his current position.

Many have expressed skepticism, arguing that such an enhancement would be difficult, given that Akiyama is already exceptionally skilled, and it would be challenging for a brief sequel to introduce developments significant enough to create a substantial impact on his scaling.

This reflection led me to the following question: Akiyama is already very well balanced and has been highly regarded by the community consensus for nearly a decade since the manga’s release. However, given that his build is already broadly competent across the board, how might it be possible to further enhance his feats, if at all?

To address this, rather than focusing solely on amplifying his existing strengths, I first considered his major weaknesses/ blind spots and explored what would need to be developed to elevate his scaling. In other words, considering that he is outstanding in nearly all categories, what factors are limiting him from reaching his full high tier potential, and how could these be addressed in the new chapters?

This analysis is based on the SCD scaling categories and definitions outlined in a Google Doc created by Lawlietfym (on Discord), which I found to be well structured and clearly explained.

Akiyama demonstrates exceptional strategic feats, with intellectual and analytical abilities consistently surpassing those of most participants. Precisely because he excels in categories such as logic, planning, and strategic manipulation, his emotional and social weaknesses become more pronounced, functioning as clear anti feats that constrain his maximum potential within the series.

After a careful review of the manga, I am fairly confident that Akiyama’s most significant anti feats lie primarily in the following domains:

Emotional Understanding (EU): Comprehending complex emotions beyond facial expressions and body language. / Emotional Perception (EP): Recognizing emotions in others through facial expressions, body language, voice, images, and reasoning. / Emotional Recognition (ER): Detecting, recognizing, and understanding cognitive and emotional limitations in oneself and others. / Emotional Awareness (EA): Acknowledging and understanding the current emotions of oneself and others. / Relationship Management: Maintaining and managing social relationships over time. / Psychological Insight: Understanding an individual’s motivations, thoughts, and behavior.

The following three situations in the manga support this conclusion.

First situation:

Akiyama struggles to comprehend complex emotions that are not directly or rationally expressed. He also fails to recognize the emotional impact he has on others and his own emotional limitations. This is particularly evident in Chapter 82, where, after admonishing Nao for trusting Fukunaga, he learns that Fukunaga harbors romantic feelings for him.

“Mr. Akiyama. Do you know why Mr. Fukunaga decided to dress as a woman for this Revival Round?”

“Easy. He wanted to use his charm to mess with the male players' heads, yknow?”

“Ah-ha! So you dont know! That's just his pretense. The real reason... Is because he has a crush on you!”

“WHAT?!”

“I know because was watching him very carefully. His feeligs are genuine. (...)You raally had no ideo, huh?”

“Idea..? That thought never even entered my mind.”

“Yes, that's right. (...) You're extremely sharp and discerning. Mr. Akiyama, but it's as if you were completely clueless when it comes to womenʼs feelings.”

This seemingly minor interaction demonstrates Akiyama’s obliviousness to genuine romantic emotions, which characters like Nao detect with relative ease. It suggests a broader disconnection from everyday emotional reading, particularly when such emotions do not carry immediate strategic value. This indicates to me that Akiyama cannot perceive emotions that fall outside a logical or instrumental framework, especially genuine romantic feelings.

Second situation:

During the Chair Game, Akiyama falls for the fake envelope trap set by Kimura (Shortbob). His failure due to him underestimating the emotional and social complexity of the opponent, failing to anticipate her capacity for deception. This demonstrates an emotional and social realization deficit, he does not fully account for the second order intentions of others, taking her as no more than naive and susceptible to ideological influence.

Third situation:

Although Akiyama can cooperate with other players, his ability to manage relationships over time is limited. He tends to treat alliances as temporary instruments for problem solving rather than as sustained emotional dynamics. This is evident from his personal life, where no close friends or confidants are mentioned, and oitside the game, where he doesn't seem all that interested in continuing a connection with Nao and others. His cooperative strategies also seem to fail to actively manage distrust or fear, neglecting those essential aspects of social dynamics within the Liar’s Game.

Collectively, these anti feats do not undermine Akiyama as a strategist; rather, they humanize him and explain why he never achieves absolute dominance in the game. His primary limitation is not intellectual but emotional and social. Almost as if he comprehends systems, rules, and probabilities but underestimates the emotional complexity of both allies and adversaries.

Recognizing this allowed me to hypothesize how the new chapter could develop or improve this aspect of his character. Specifically, it suggests that enhancing Akiyama’s abilities may require confronting challenges grounded in social and emotional strategy rather than purely logical reasoning. Alternatively, growth might emerge in parallel contexts outside the game itself, particularly in relation to Nao, whose exceptional skills in emotional and interpersonal intelligence have compensated for his.

For example, in the Contraband Game (Chapter 59), Akiyama devises a cooperative strategy, assuming even a hostile actor like Yokoya will respond to rational incentives. However, he fails to grasp Yokoya’s deeper emotional and psychological motivations until it is too late, revealing a critical gap in psychological insight.

“I had a faint idea of what he was up to. (...) But l didnʼt do anything to stop him. I feared the possibility that one mistake could blow our entire plan. (...) I pretended not to see anything... And I let the bastard do as he pleased!”

“(...) What I'm saying is I merely used that group of three. I never intended to trust any of them from the start. Iʼm not too sure what those guys think of me, but... If you consider the very base of my motivation, I'm hardly any different from Yokoya! Yokoya knew that. He saw through that! And that's why he was mocking me!!”

Here, Akiyama reflects on how he handled the three Northern players during the Contraband Game. Outwardly, he presented the situation as “The enemy isn’t the players; it’s the Liar Game Office.” This statement appears altruistic and cooperative. However, internally, Akiyama admits that he never truly trusted them and treated them as instruments to stabilize the Northern faction. Their cooperation was thus strategic and utilitarian, not genuinely mutual. He does successfully recognize a moral inconsistency between his preached cooperation and his actual instrumental approach. Even though his ultimate goal was to minimize harm, his methods involved treating people as means rather than ends. Akiyama uses others to protect the group, demonstrating that he is not a pure idealist; rather, he is a pragmatist presenting himself as one. His cooperation is conditional. His trust is strategic, not emotional, and his moral principles allow for exceptions. This predictability is precisely why Yokoya is able to anticipate his moves.

A notable strength of Akiyama is his capacity to recognize cognitive and moral limitations within himself; however, he does not consistently identify these same limitations in others. This oversight highlights a deficiency in his psychological insight: while Akiyama understands Yokoya’s thought processes, he fails to comprehend the underlying motivations behind his actions until Nao provides further clarification.

In conclusion, it is conceivable that Akiyama’s emotional and relational feats could be developed in future narratives. But this growth is likely to emerge not through the same type of game scenarios that highlight his existing ones but through interactions that challenge his emotional perception and interpersonal understanding. I believe Nao’s role as a relational anchor will remain crucial in addressing this.

While I anticipate a game that will test multiple categories, I am equally intrigued by the potential for cognitive development in the emotional and interpersonal domains. Observing Akiyama grow in these areas would provide a richer, more nuanced understanding of his capabilities. And would serve useful to scale him higher.
