As soon as the lots were revealed, Kimura had already mapped out a sophisticated strategy, looking beyond the value of individual pairs and immediately analyzing the combinations that could be formed across multiple lots.

P1: Every revealed lot contains at least one pair. Lot C contains a pair of Aces, while Lot A contains a pair of 10s.

P2: Lot B contains 10-J-K, while Lot C contains 9-Q. Cards from Lots B and C therefore form 9-10-J-Q-K, a straight.

**C1**: Based on P1 and P2, Kimura identifies two potential routes: collecting all four 10s by securing Lot A and the lots containing the remaining 10s, or combining Lots B and C to form a straight.

P3: Kimura tells Mikamoto that Lot A is the best lot while deliberately making sure Saeki can hear her.

P4: Saeki knows that Lot A contains a pair of 10s, while the other 10s are in Lots B and D.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura deliberately calls Lot A the best lot because she expects Saeki to recognize its importance to the four-of-a-kind route and become interested in it.

P5: The trial round lasts only three minutes.

P6: Nao proposes cooperation with 1:48 remaining.

**Contextual insight**
With only 1:48 remaining, the players have very little time to independently analyze the lots. Nao's sudden cooperation proposal arrives before they have much opportunity to fully evaluate the available combinations.

P7: Mikamoto competes with Saeki for Lot A.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura's attempt to make Lot A appear especially valuable succeeds: Saeki and Mikamoto become occupied competing for the lot she deliberately highlighted.

P8: Lot D is the weakest lot, and Nao voluntarily takes it as the person proposing cooperation.

**Contextual insight**
Nao's acceptance of Lot D gives the appearance of a cooperative structure while leaving Kimura free to pursue the combinations most useful to her group.

**Trial-Round Bidding**

P10: Saeki bids on Lot B to prevent Kimura from obtaining it.

**C2**: Based on P10 and P11, Kimura recognizes that Saeki is willing to spend coins specifically to deny her access to Lot B.

**C3**: Based on **C1**, P9, and P11, Kimura recognizes that Lot B contributes to one of the combinations she identified earlier, making it worth spending 8 of her 30 coins to secure it.

P12: Kimura tells Saeki that he should have cooperated with Nao and taken her King of Diamonds.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura uses cooperation selectively to obtain a useful card without entering another direct bidding conflict, while simultaneously making Saeki's refusal to cooperate appear to be the reason he missed the opportunity.

**Cooperation Act**

**Contextual insight**
Nao's withdrawal allows the cooperative proposal to continue despite there being fewer lots than players, while Harimoto's arrangement creates the appearance that everyone's actions can be monitored and therefore trusted.

**Two-Board Mechanism**

P17: Harimoto possesses a board bearing his name, and Kimura's fingerprint was registered on it beforehand.

P18: Kimura possesses a board bearing her own name, and her fingerprint is also registered on it. This board was initially held by Harimoto and is later given to Mikamoto.

**C4**: Based on P17 and P18, Kimura recognizes that her fingerprint is registered on both boards.

P19: Harimoto appears as the winner of Lot A without visibly performing a bidding action.

P20: Harimoto claims that his spiritual power caused the board to operate automatically.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura identifies a potential method for concealing her involvement: if she can operate either board through her registered fingerprint, she can potentially make bids appear under another player's name while that player is not physically making the bid.

**Contextual insight**
The board arrangement gives Kimura a way to secretly determine which member of her group appears to be making a bid, allowing the group to coordinate acquisitions while concealing her involvement.

**Kimura's Economic Objective**

P27: An unranked player loses 150 million, which is redistributed as 100 million to first place, 40 million to second place, and 10 million to third place.

**C10**: Based on P29 and P30, Kimura recognizes that the group can concentrate valuable lots with Harimoto and later redistribute the cards among its members so that several members can complete valid hands.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura's objective is not merely to maximize the strength of her group's hands. She also wants her group to qualify while increasing the number of outsiders who remain unranked, because each additional unranked player increases the amount redistributed among the qualifying players.

P32: The group's strategy depends on maintaining cooperation among the other players for as long as possible.

**Contextual insight**
Preserving the appearance of cooperation is strategically valuable because it delays open competition and gives Kimura's group more opportunities to acquire and redistribute cards before outsiders realize they are being exploited.

P33: Kimura can voluntarily surrender a lot that she could otherwise contest.

**Contextual insight**
Kimura does not need to win every valuable lot. She can surrender individual lots whenever doing so preserves the cooperative structure and advances the group's larger objective.

**Akiyama Breaks the Plan**

**Contextual transition**
Kimura can no longer rely on hidden board manipulation or the previous appearance of universal cooperation. From this point onward, she must manipulate individual players and their exchanges rather than controlling the game primarily through the cooperative group structure.

**Kimura's Actual Objective**

**Contextual insight**
Kimura turns Kawai's attempt to qualify into a trap: she induces him to spend 290 coins on Mikamoto's lot, leaving him with only 10 coins, and then uses the 285 coins extracted from Taninaka to take 4♣.

Kimura's Intended Final Sequence

* Lot A: J♠ → Taninaka bids 285 → Taninaka wins the card → Kimura receives 285 coins.
* Lot B: Q♥, 4♥, 2♦, 10♠ → Kawai bids 290 → Kawai wins the lot → Mikamoto receives 290 coins.
* Lot C: 4♣ → Kimura bids 285 → Kimura wins the card → Abe receives 285 coins.

Thus, the final exchange simultaneously redistributes resources within Kimura's group and prevents Kawai from completing his proposed qualifying hand.

The fifth and final card-exchange round is the point at which Akiyama's counterstrategy overturns Kimura Kei's advantage. Akiyama had already calculated that, by discarding A♦ and having Taninaka bid all 332 coins, he could raise his 596 coins to 928, enough to secure both lots he needed without interference from Harimoto's group. His planned counterstrategy therefore allows him to take first place and save the other players.

**Canon statement/insight**
Kimura had understood from the beginning that Bid Poker is fundamentally a majority-rule game: the winning strategy is to form a team, pool the most coins, prevent opponents from outbidding the team, and collectively build strong hands.

However, she believed that this strategy was impossible to implement reliably because the player controlling the collective money could betray the others for personal gain. Kimura therefore believed that only her tightly bound group could overcome this problem through faith and trust.

Akiyama ultimately proves her wrong by exploiting this very assumption. He demonstrates that Kimura's belief that everyone is inevitably selfish and untrustworthy is itself the weakness that leads to her defeat.
