Why wasn't this explained that well in the manga?

Manga industry is really known for how strict they are with the number of pages and release dates, at that time the liar game manga already faced delay problems, so probably Kaitani “rushed” the ending, and by rushing I mean the manga did not explained Akiyama's plan that well because the pages limitation, but any time he had the chance, he pointed out through the game that everything was part of Akiyama's plan.

Was processing the 377 combinations necessary?
Calculating the 377 combinations was necessary for Akiyama to ensure the viability of his Stalemate plan. By predicting and anticipating every possible action from the four kingdoms (including his own), Akiyama could:
Validate the Stalemate Feasibility, Mitigate Risks, Anticipate Opponent Strategies, Align Actions with Objectives. This meticulous calculation ensured that Akiyama’s plan could withstand interference and remain effective until the game’s conclusion.
What would've happened if Akiyama couldn't save everyone?

It is really hard to tell what would've happened if the game ended up with losers, since the manga ending left many questions unanswered, such as the real fate of the losers and what would they do with them once the game is over. However, we can't use this to debunk Akiyama’s situation, since he worked with the mental pressure that losers will be sold to the black market, therefore their fate would mean death or worse, and even with this situation, he executed this plan to save everyone.
“The game would have ended in a stalemate even without Akiyama's involvement”
This claim is incorrect. By round 42, Wei had already lost, leaving Wu and Shu with 18 LP. If Akiyama hadn’t introduced the “rich player” lie, any attempt to approach Wu and Shu would have been rejected because his intentions would seem suspicious. It would appear as though Akiyama had a hidden agenda, like striking a deal with another kingdom to cover his debt in exchange for manipulating Wu or Shu into losing. The rich player lie was effective because Yokoya didn’t deny its existence, leading Wu and Shu to accept it as fact. When Akiyama later asked them about the rich player, the realization that none of them revealed themselves as the “rich heir” led to the belief that a spy existed among them. This made Akiyama’s “Anti-Spy Strategy” seem like the best way to prevent betrayal, and they didn't worry the Stalemate keep for all the rounds because with the Anti-Spy Strategy would make the spy betray the rich player and therefor make his team win the game, this is why they didn't bother to see the game kept in Stalemate until the end, since they believed the Spy will betray the rich heir sooner or later. Without this lie, Wu and Shu likely would have played the game predictably, like a rock-paper-scissors situation. For example, in round 43, Wu might determine that setting a wall was the most logical move, prompting Shu to think the same. This would lead to "no action" for that round, causing an LP difference. Without the spy narrative, that’s how the kingdoms might have approached the game.
“Yokoya had no option but to accept the stalemate because he couldn’t bribe anyone from Wu or Shu due to the contract clause stating the general responsible for his team’s loss would bear all debts.”
This is only partially true. If the inability to bribe were the only factor, Akiyama wouldn’t have needed to psychologically manipulate Yokoya into accepting the stalemate. But he did, because Yokoya is incredibly wealthy and could have bribed someone despite the risks. Given that this was the final game and Yokoya’s last chance to defeat Akiyama and Nao, it wouldn’t be unthinkable for him to spend millions to ruin their lives. Akiyama knew Yokoya could potentially break the stalemate, which is why he used psychological tactics to ensure Yokoya wouldn’t interfere.
Here’s how Yokoya could have broken the stalemate:
In round 60, with both teams at 1 LP, Yokoya could approach Wu’s general and offer to pay his debts if he intentionally lost. The general might initially refuse, citing the contract clause that made them personally responsible for their team’s debt. However, Yokoya could analyze the numbers: with each player owing around 150 million yen, plus the 300 million to be returned at the end, the team’s total debt would be about 1.8 billion yen. Yokoya, having earned over 2 billion throughout the game, could afford to cover this. While it would cost him nearly all his money, Yokoya’s hatred for Akiyama and Nao might drive him to make such a drastic move.
After securing Wu’s cooperation, Yokoya could then approach Shu, showing proof of his deal with Wu and convincing Shu not to act in the final round. To sweeten the deal, Yokoya could propose that Shu help cover part of Wu’s debt for example, contributing 800 million yen, since Shu would still gain significantly more in the end. This plan would likely appeal to Shu’s members, as they’d ultimately walk away with more money.
However, this scenario didn’t play out because Akiyama expertly exploited Yokoya’s complex throughout the game. By the final conversation, Akiyama had ensured that Yokoya not only accepted the stalemate but also refrained from interfering, solidifying the outcome.
