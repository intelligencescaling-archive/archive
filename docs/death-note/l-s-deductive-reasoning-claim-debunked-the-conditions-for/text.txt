Now, let me remind you of what Deductive reasoning is as per River's own definition: "It is deductively valid because the conclusions must follow if the premises are true. Deductive validity is governed by one criterion, that being that if the premises are true, it is logically impossible for the conclusion to be false. Hence, the only way for you to disprove the deductive validity is by pointing out a conclusion that does not follow. Arguing about how the premises were generated and whether they are true belong to the separate topic of soundness, deduction is the logical form in which you move from your premises to your conclusion."

Now, for the smart ones, they will realize instantly that the reasoning process given above isn't deductively valid. Also, just a small refinement to the definition itself, it is more accurate to say "Hence, the only way for you to disprove the deductive validity is by pointing out a conclusion that does not **necessarily** follow." Meaning it can be 99.99999% likely to follow and the argument still won't be deductive, because deductive validity requires a direct logical inference, not just high probability. Deductive validity requires that it is impossible (0% chance due to a direct logical contradiction) for the conclusion to not follow.

Let me give you an example.

Premise 1: Pressing the button is 99.9999999% likely to give me money.

Premise 2: I will press the button.

Conclusion: I will get money.

Is this deductively valid? NO! Because there is a tiny chance of you not getting the money, no matter how small it is. Just because the conclusion follows in most possible scenarios does not mean it is deductively valid, because being deductively valid requires that "if the premises are true, it is logically impossible for the conclusion to be false." This is in your own words, and this is the actual philosophical standard as well, but now you will come to realize that even though you know the definition of deductive validity, you don't actually understand its application.

I will explain it for you myself through giving counterexamples. A single counterexample can't necessarily disprove inductive validity, but it can disprove deductive validity because deductive validity means the conclusion has to follow necessarily (in 100% of potential logical possibilities), so 1 counterexample is enough to disprove the claim.
