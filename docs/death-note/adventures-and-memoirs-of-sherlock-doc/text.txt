A Scandal in Bohemia

Sherlock presents a mysterious note mailed to him informing him of a visitor who will shortly meet him. Embedded into the paper is “E,g,P,G,t.” Watson fails to glean anything useful from it, but Sherlock easily explains the meaning behind it. The “Gt” is short for “Gesellschaft,” which is German for “Company.” It’s a common contraction there. “P” is of course for “Papier.” And “Eg” is shorthand for the location. Of the towns that could be shortened to Eg - Eglow, Eglonitz, and Egria - Sherlock narrows it down to Egria, which is in the German-speaking Bohemia, based on his knowledge of events there such as the death of Wallenstein and its renown for its numerous glass-factories and paper-mills, showing his know of even the most obscure tidbits of foreign cultures and his ability to recall and associate that into his thinking.

Not just that, Sherlock also deduces that the writer is a German based on the writing style of the “uncourteous” usage of verbs - “This account of you we have from all quarters received.” A Frenchman or a Russian, he says, wouldn’t write like that. This is based on his knowledge of German grammar, which often has verbs placed at the end, which the writer who wrote it in English couldn’t disguise.

While en route to a new case, Sherlock is able to calculate the speed of his train (53.5 mph)  from the distance he estimates between each telegraph post (60 yds). It’s a seemingly simple calculation on the surface, just estimate how much time it takes to move from one point to another. But he’s limited by her FOV, the closer he is, the more accurately he can just go off how long it takes for the posts to slide past his FOV. The farther he is, the more complicated it gets as the rate at which the move across his vision becomes angular velocity and he’d have to take into account his own distance from the train. With the help of ChatGPT, this is essentially what he did:

If instead he doesn’t use a fixed reference on the train and only judges how quickly the posts move across her view (i.e. measures an angular velocity), then geometry matters and you must convert angular speed to linear speed.
Bearing angle:
θ(t)=arctan⁡(dxpost−vt)

Angular velocity:
omega(t) = vd/(x - vt)^2 + d^2

Maximum angular velocity at closest approach = v/d

Train speed from maximum angular velocity:
v = d*omega

Train speed from measured angular velocity:
v = (omega(x-vt)^2 +d^2)/d

Such advanced mathematics in his head requires very good spatial awareness, a deep understanding of math and its applicability to real world scenarios, and the ability to think almost two different tracks of thought at once as he has to constantly adjust for the train’s changing distance.
