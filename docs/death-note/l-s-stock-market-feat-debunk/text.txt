Lawliet Stock Feat Debunk

Lawliet’s Stock Market Feat:
L earned an impressive 20,000x return on his stock investments in just two years, an outcome that under normal circumstances would be considered impossible. He gained these returns on an initial investment of 1 million pounds and these returns would shatter any known record of trading returns. Such an astronomical gain implies L possesses an unparalleled mastery of economics, financial behavior, and market principles. The magnitude of this return implies that L must have possessed an extraordinary mastery of economics and market psychology, leveraging an in-depth understanding of human behavioral unpredictability and the unpredictability inherent in financial markets. Furthermore, L didn't merely apply standard economic knowledge, he needed to create innovative mental models from the complex data he encountered in order to accomplish this. By synthesizing diverse elements of market behavior and analysis, L had to devise unique strategies to exploit the market that would be impossible for others to implement. L’s ability to evolve traditional economic thought to achieve extraordinary financial success is a showcase of his ability to not just learn complex principles at a young age but expand on known information to surpass it, transcending established economic principles. (Scans at the end of the doc)
This feat is considered a high level showing of L’s learning ability where he was able to take knowledge as a child and apply it in such a way that is beyond even the most skilled stock traders and economists. However, this is not actually the case. The feat is built on multiple false assumptions and a lack of foundation and exposing those issues leads to the feat losing all validity.
I have 2 main reasons why I don’t consider the Stock Market feat valid.
1.       The actual accomplishment of the feat isn’t directly illustrated by “L earning 20,000x returns” on stock performance. This is due to the supposed information L used to accomplish the feat doesn’t allow for it to exist.
2.       The act of earning such extravagant returns doesn’t either showcase OR necessitate LA from L. Even if the feat were possible via economic knowledge, nothing shown would actually prove L used or learned such information.
Claiming that the feat is LA is operating under a series of misunderstandings regarding how the stock market actually works and then the assumption that L would be able to influence it via his understanding of its main principles.
Also, part of this analysis is based on my knowledge of Economics so I will try and keep that part of this as simple to understand as possible. As someone who went to school for and has a degree in both Finance and Economics, that is partially why I recognized this supposed feat violates principles which hold true in economics and market activity.
L's stock trading success is presented as an impossible outcome. A return so large that it surpasses any real-world economic law or market principle. The impossibility of the return is central to the idea that L’s learning ability must have transcended normal human limits to achieve it. However, this is not a correct evaluation of the feat. Market Economics make such returns impossible so L achieving them means one of two things needs to be true.
1)      Either in DN verse, the market and economic factors don’t work the same way as they do in real life and as such the normal economic constraints on the market don’t apply here. While L’s apparent success could be an argument in support of this, there is no evidence or mention of the markets in Death Note operating differently than real world markets. In this case, we also wouldn’t then be able to use L’s economic knowledge of the market as proof of his LA since we would no longer have a basis of comparison. Additionally, if the DN markets do work different than ours, claiming “L has impressive LA since he got better returns than we can in real trading” is no longer valid as a comparison for claiming L has superior LA since you can no longer make the argument that L needed to apply “real world” economic theory and certainly not claim he surpassed real world knowledge if the knowledge isn’t comparable.
2)      The second possibility of how such a return is possible would be outside factors working in favor of L’s portfolio. This would include things like a black-swan event or even just good luck. Either of these would also disqualify the feat as LA since it would no longer be an application of L’s knowledge achieving the results but outside factors influencing it.
As for why specifically that kind of return is impossible without the assistance of massive amounts of good luck, there are many economic factors that play a role in market behavior. His first issue is liquidity, which involves the amount of a particular stock/asset that is available on the market for trading. To earn the kind of returns L did he would need things with volatile movement or else he would never even come close to 20,000x. The issue is the more volatile (e.g. the more the price of something is likely to change) the less liquid that asset is. For example, take a massive company like NVIDIA, AMAZON, APPLE etc. have 10s or 100s of millions of shares traded per day. But those stocks don’t move more than a few percent per day, usually moving slightly in a constant but slow trend up. The kinds of stocks with the volatility to earn greater returns just don’t trade nearly as much. This isn’t a market rule or economic theory which could be “proven wrong” with superior knowledge, but a physical constraint of trading. An example of the other end of the spectrum would be a penny stock which can see significantly more movement in price but has hundreds if not thousands of times fewer volume.
Furthermore, should L make large moves with a stock with low volatility, which he would certainly need to do to reach his extraordinary returns, he would experience something called price slippage. He wouldn’t be able to purchase all those shares at the “current” market price. As he makes market orders the price will climb or fall depending on whether he is buying or selling, and such a change would eat into his profits. This is the same reason why someone like Elon Musk can’t just sell his Tesla shares to pocket billions of dollars in cash. Trying to sell that many would crash the price of the shares meaning his sale would be for FAR less than what it is worth on paper. It is this principle that would make L’s massive returns impossible, because even if L “could” identify an opportunity in the stock market to exploit for massive returns, he wouldn’t be able to use it with as much money as he wants. This leads to the final market factor which would make exploiting such things impossible in large amounts, which is arbitrage. The markets work by eliminating arbitrage opportunities so even if L could identify them, they are small so his ability to utilize them also won’t account for the 20,000x returns. This again continues to point back to luck or other external factors needing to play a role in earning such returns. This doesn’t even account for market factors which would kick in if a major exploitable opportunity occurred, if something in the market moves too aggressively the trading can be suspended, meaning L would lose the ability to take advantage of it.
Another potential factor to consider is that L used a currency swap to make his trades. The thing to consider is that this takes place in the early 1990s so we can actually find historical currency exchange rates from that period. In doing so we learn that buying British Pounds (“GBP”) with yen in this time period was a tremendously bad financial decision. The Pound saw massive crashes during this timeframe which would further point toward L not having perfect market understanding by choosing to invest in GBP. In fact, no matter what stocks he invested in had he just kept his money in Yen during this timeframe he could have had close to double the profits he did have.
I bring all this up to illustrate how L achieving these extraordinary returns doesn’t represent a “perfect understanding of market behavior and economics” since such knowledge wouldn’t allow for his returns. Not only do market factors disallow such returns but there is never any indication of L knowing such things. As such, his returns contain large elements of luck or other non-controlled factors.
All this being said, even if you ignore the former concerns about the feat, it still can’t be considered valid LA. True LA must be evaluated by the process of acquiring, integrating, and applying new information. The description of L’s feat centers almost exclusively on the result, that being his massive returns. However, earning massive returns does NOT prove L has mastery over market behavior knowledge and economic theory, or that he needed to apply mental models or other applications of such knowledge to achieve this result. Both such claims are just baseless assumptions on the premises that they would be necessary to do what L did. Nothing shown in the supposed feat highlights the actual main principles of LA, being the speed at which L acquired the knowledge, the depth and breadth of the knowledge he gained, and his ability to apply that knowledge. Since the feat doesn’t show he learned economic theory (and as I showed earlier, economics principles don’t actually allow for this anyway) it can’t show an acquisition speed since there is no specific knowledge in question. Same for depth since there is no showcase of “how much” knowledge L learned, and his application is flawed in that as I have shown it wasn’t reliant on economic knowledge. This is just the difference between an outcome and a process; therefore, it has no relevance on how much or quickly L learned.
The final point to consider is that L managed to learn and apply knowledge that surpasses what we consider “normal economics theory” which allowed him to bypass normal market limitations. While versions of this point have been used to prop up the feat, it is completely baseless and without any merit. First, it was never even once stated, shown, or even implied that L had learned knowledge which surpassed humanities study of economics. Any claim otherwise is false, so saying “L must have learned and then improved upon all known economic and market theory” is purely head canon or fanfiction. Second, is the claim that L needed to create and use mental models built from his knowledge of economics to earn these returns and again this is a baseless claim. Since I already showed why knowledge of economics and market behavior won’t allow for the returns L had, this disproves the idea that “L needed mental models with this knowledge”. Then again, it was never mentioned or stated that L did anything of the sort. Claims of this nature would be considered extraordinary, and such claims need extraordinary evidence to be considered valid. Since there is no reason to believe that economic models or knowledge can or would lead to such massive returns, L earning such overwhelming returns is NOT evidence of such a claim in any capacity.
Overall, the supposed feat of achieving a 20,000x portfolio return in two years is impossible under real-world market conditions due to constraints such as liquidity, price slippage, regulatory safeguards and more. Even if Death Note's universe allowed for such extreme outcomes, there is no evidence that L acquired, integrated, or applied advanced economic knowledge to reach this result. Without a demonstrated learning process, L’s success cannot be attributed to LA but must instead be viewed as a narrative exaggeration or the product of external factors such as luck or intuition.
