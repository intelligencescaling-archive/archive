**Question 1:** Why did the youth let Chen Ran ask the first question? If Chen Ran asked the Liar's Paradox first, the youth would have made a choice where he knowingly puts himself in front of death for no reason, by underestimating Chen Ran. So the youth only got lucky Chen Ran didn't ask the Liar's paradox. Is he an idiot?

**Answer:**

The youth never agreed to let Chen Ran go first. The youth only SHRUGGED in agreement which is a movement, not a lie. Even nodding in agreement doesn't count as a lie, only verbal agreement would result in the contract being enforced.

Bai Xiuchi's analysis mistakenly said that the Youth underestimated Chen Ran, but her memory is imperfect which is why she misanalyzed what happened. She forgot that the youth never agreed verbally to letting Chen Ran go first. Chen Ran was the one who said "I'll go first," and the youth just shrugged. If Chen Ran asked the Liar's paradox question, then the youth could simply say he never agreed to Chen Ran going first. But since Chen Ran asked a simple question, the youth went on with the game.

Keep in mind that the side characters don't always perfectly analyze the game, they are unreliable narrators looking from their own perspective. Chen Ran and the Youth are smarter than Bai Xiuchi so it's natural she didn't perfectly analyze the depth of their interaction.

You need to realize that the narrator is not completely reliable all the time, especially when the person analyzing is a side character. Even when it's not a side character, it can still be unreliable. Evidence: **\[The words of the three people immediately made Chen Ran realize he had been tricked. His face instantly turned pale, and he closed his eyes in despair.\]** The reality is that Chen Ran was only pretending to be tricked, he was only pretending to despair, but the narrator makes it seem like he actually was tricked, which is false. In other words, the narrator can make false deductions (especially when the side characters are doing the analyses) so you should most likely trust only the descriptions of what physically occurs, not always the deductions or conclusions about the background processes.

**Question 2:** What is with all that weird deduction in the end regarding 1 team and/or 1 person being able to leave?

**Answer:**

Firstly, it was already made clear in these 5 chapters that lying is forbidden.

Secondly, there were 18 participants transported into the game.

Thirdly, in Chinese mythology, level #1 of the 18 levels of hell is where LIARS go, and only people who do not lie can leave.

Fourthly, the old man mentioned that he would tell Chen Ran the method of leaving the secret chamber IF they **teamed up**.

Thus, looking at the patterns, there are firstly two numbers mentioned: level 1 and 18 total levels of hell. 18 people entered the secret chamber, so it can be concluded that 1 must leave. This is not a 100% logically deductive conclusion, but it's the only clue they have, so Chen Ran concludes it is most likely the case that only 1 can leave.

Given that the masked group (almost fully confirmed veteran players) were working together cooperatively, it implies that it's not just 1 person, but rather 1 team can also leave. Therefore either 1 person or 1 team must remain alive. Further supporting evidence is that the old man said he would tell the method of leaving once they teamed up (lying is forbidden so it most likely is the truth). Thus 1 person OR 1 team can leave.

However, the youth set a trap. He knew that the members of his group all told lies due to Chen Ran's scheme, so he wanted Chen Ran to think it is 1 person AND 1 team that must be met as a condition for them to leave, and he did this by leaving Bai Xiuchi alive. Because WHY would he even leave her alive when he could simply shoot her for lying? Naturally to meet the condition for 1 person and 1 team to remain alive (this is what he wanted Chen Ran to believe, just in case Chen Ran wins).

Using this trick, even if Chen Ran makes them all lie, Chen Ran would still spare them in order for the condition of 1 person and 1 team to be met, following this method: Chen Ran joins the youth's group, Bai Xiuchi is 1 person, therefore 1 group + 1 person has been met. Once that happens, the youth can just reveal the truth, that it's actually 1 group OR 1 person who can leave, but it's too late for Chen Ran who has already joined the team, so he'll have to kill Bai Xiuchi at that point anyways and survive along with the other people whose group he joined.

However, Chen Ran saw through this trickery. It doesn't make logical sense for it to be 1 group and 1 person, because that would be 2 in total. Since 18 people entered and the only clues were 1 and 18 (from the mythology of the first layer of the eighteen levels of hell), the most reasonable conclusion (not perfectly deductively valid, but still the most reasonable given the lack of information) is that 1 PERSON can leave. The second most reasonable conclusion is that 1 TEAM can also leave, which is likely true given that the group of veterans were all smoothly cooperating. It simply does not make sense for both a person AND a team (which is 2)  to be able to leave.

Thus Chen Ran kills the remaining people who all lied and passes the secret chamber. All of this trickery and scheming was happening in the background by the way, they didn't say any of it out loud to each other, it was just subtle trickery and manipulation, which is insane.

**Question 3:** What's with that confusing verbal battle between the Youth and Chen Ran?

**Answer:**

The youth said to Chen Ran...

你真的要，不是不能不给

"if you **truly** want it, it's not that I can't not give it"

不 means no/not in Chinese.

This triple negative is basically him saying that it's not physically impossible to refrain from giving the gun to Chen Ran. In other words, it's not physically impossible to reject Chen Ran's request if he says he wants the gun. Naturally this is true because the youth can simply choose to refrain if he wishes.

Chen Ran responded perfectly mirroring the same grammatical structure:

你真的给，不是不能不要。

If you truly give it, it's not that I can't not want it.

The youth replies:

你不要，我不能非要要你要，要不要在你，给不给在我，你不说要，你就不会知道你要是不要我不给。

If you don't want it, I cannot insist that you must want it, want or not want it is up to you, give or not give it is up to me, if you don't say you want it, you then won't know that whether you wanted it or not, I wouldn't give it.

The final sentence is the confusing part. The youth is telling Chen Ran that whether or not he wants the gun, he (the youth) will NOT give it. It is based on the conditional that Chen Ran does NOT say he wants it, and this is not a lie because Chen Ran can't know for certain if the youth will give it once he says he DOES want it. But if Chen Ran DOES say he wants it, the youth's words still won't be a lie because the youth preset the entire phrase with "if you don't say you want it," which would no longer be met if Chen Ran says he does.

Keep in mind the IF conditionals. Let's say I tell you "If you marry me, I'll give you a trillion dollars," but I never intend to give you anything, is this a lie? Maybe from our own perspective it is, but objectively, following the rules of lying in 18LOH, it is NOT a lie unless you actually do marry me and I don't give you a trillion dollars. But if we never get married, then I never lied, because I gave the statement only on the IF condition. If the IF condition is never met, it cannot be considered a lie.

Same with "If I jump, the sun will explode." You might say this is a clear lie and judge me as a liar, but it isn't. If I never jump in my entire life, the statement is never proven wrong. This is what the youth did when he stacked the 2 mutually exclusive IF statements by saying IF you don't say you want it and WHETHER you wanted it or not. If Chen Ran meets the second condition (WHETHER you want it or not), he doesn't meet the first. If he meets the first, he can't meet the second. So the youth cannot be judged as a liar. There's actually even more layers to it, for example if Chen Ran wants to meet the second condition, he'll have to say "I want it" and "I don't want it" which is a contradiction and turn him into a liar, meanwhile the youth still won't be a liar because the first condition would be broken if Chen Ran says "I want."

Chen Ran replies with an even more convoluted sentence that follows a slightly different trick:

你不给，我不是不能不要你给，给不给在你，要不要在我，你不说给，你就不会知道你不是不能不给时我不要。

If you don't give it, it isn't that I can't not want you to give it, give or not give is up to you, want or not want is up to me, if you don't say you'll give it, you won't know that if it wasn't impossible for you not to give it, I wouldn't want it.

Notice how 不 (negation) (pronounced bù or bú depending on the context) pops up 12 times in Chen Ran's reply? Insane tongue twister and he came up with it on the spot, almost instantly replying to the youth's question. If Chen Ran misses even a single negation and accidentally lies (for example by saying something is "impossible" rather than "not impossible), he can be judged as a Liar and killed.

"If you don't give it, it isn't that I can't not want you to give it" means "I have the ability to **not** want you to give it, even if you're the one who makes the choice not to give it yourself." This is in the realm of possibility, so it's not a lie.

The hard part is the last sentence:

If you don't say you'll give it, you won't know that if it wasn't impossible for you not to give it, I wouldn't want it.

This means that even if the youth had the capability to refrain from giving the gun (so he wasn't forced to give it, and only gave it by his own choice intentionally), Chen Ran simply wouldn't want the gun even then. What Chen Ran is saying is that he does not want the gun, whether the youth gives it or not, meanwhile he formats it using possibilities rather than definitive claims about wanting it or not.

They're basically communicating normally, but trying to make each other accidentally lie.

After this convoluted sentence, the youth gave up on making the sentences increasingly more convoluted and asked one more time if Chen Ran wants it. Chen Ran responds in the same manner, asking the youth if he'll give it. At this point the youth gives up on trying to make Chen Ran lie accidentally like this.

According to Bai Xiuchi's analysis of the battle, Chen Ran is not allowed to say "I want it" or "I don't want it" explicitly because...

1: Chen Ran does NOT want the gun which the youth held out to him, since he wants to see how the youth (who is a veteran) uses it. So if Chen Ran says "I want it," he'll be judged as a Liar because he won't be taking the gun after saying he wants it as a reply to the youth holding it out and asking if he wants it.

2: Chen Ran DOES want to gain ownership of the gun EVENTUALLY. If he says he does not want the gun, that would be a lie as well.

In other words, Chen Ran both wants and doesn't want the gun simultaneously. Thus he cannot answer with a simple yes or not. Using his high processing speed, Chen Ran responded with the convoluted sentences that mirrored the youth's statements rather than responding with a simple "I want it" or "I don't want it" which could get him killed. This is all Bai Xiuchi's analysis, naturally, and the narrative confirms it as being her opinion.

Putting aside her analysis, it may be the case that Chen Ran wouldn't have been judged as a liar even if he said he wants it or that he doesn't want it, however Chen Ran did want to make the opponents all lie, so he chose to reply with the convoluted sentences anyways. It is both more rigorous (although risky if he stutters) and potent against his opponent. Another option would be to remain silent since there's nothing forcing him to speak, but that would cause a stalemate and the game wouldn't progress. Rather, it would actually make it even harder to beat the youth's team if things drag and they settle themselves psychologically, so Chen Ran focusing on quickly getting the youth trapped into a lie.

**Question 4:** Why did the youth judge Chen Ran as a liar when he didn't even answer the question. Is he an idiot?

**Answer:**

It is precisely because Chen Ran didn't answer the question that the youth thought he lied. The condition which Chen Ran set for the game was that the participants MUST answer. They cannot refrain from answering. The youth assumed Chen Ran broke this condition, when in reality Chen Ran was only pretending to be unable to answer the question. Chen Ran acted like he was in despair at realizing the paradox was unsolvable, when in his mind he was only pondering on how to answer the question. This lapse in judgement from the youth is what made him judge Chen Ran as a liar. The youth wasn't judging Chen Ran for lying when answering the paradox, but rather for lying when setting up the condition prior to the game (that the participants MUST answer the question, which Chen Ran looked like he was unable to do).
