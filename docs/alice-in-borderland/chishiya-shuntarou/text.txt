  A) Six of Diamonds - Blackjack
On Chishiya’s first day in Borderland, the first game he faced was Blackjack. This was almost certain death for Chishiya because:
1. He didn’t yet understand the core rule of Borderland, that losing means death.
2. He had no knowledge of Blackjack and had never played it before.
3. His opponents were four experienced players with advanced tricks up their sleeves.
4. The starting number of chips was based on the remaining hours on one's Visa. Chishiya only had 5 chips (extremely few compared to the others) since it was his first day, and he hadn’t played any games yet to extend his Visa.
Despite these clear disadvantages, Chishiya remained calm and composed while silently focusing on adapting to the game as quickly as possible.=> AQ
In the first round with the first dealer, Chishiya took the opportunity to learn the basic definitions of the Blackjack-specific terms mentioned in the rule sheet, terms he had never encountered before (hit, stand, split, double down…). He then quickly grasped the core of Blackjack: that as a Player, your fundamental task is to bet against the Dealer's potential to 'beat' you (*)=> Adaptability, FRI, Analysis
After just a few rounds of grasping the basics, Chishiya began steadily increasing his number of tokens, which made a few other players suspicious. In reality, this was because Chishiya had developed a rudimentary card-counting method based on his earlier observations, which helped boost his chances of winning during his turns as a Player.
This method divides the deck into two groups:
* 2 to 8 (Low Group, Advantageous for the Dealer)
9 to Ace (High Group, Advantageous for the Player)
The foundation of this method lies in the fact that the Dealer does not have the option to Stand and is forced to Hit until reaching 17(*). Therefore, the probability of busting increases if the undealt cards contain many high-value cards. Conversely, if the Player chooses to Stand and the remaining undealt cards contain mostly low-value cards, the Dealer’s bust rate drops, while the Player's score stays moderate.
There are 24 cards in the High Group. We can define a temporary ratio called X, which equals the number of undealt cards divided by the number of High Group cards remaining. At the start of the game, this is 52/24 ≈ 2.17 (this is the standard/initial X value). During the match, by observing the cards already played, Chishiya could estimate the composition of the remaining deck:
   * If X > 2.17, it means High Group cards have decreased, so the proportion of Low Group cards has increased. → The deck is becoming more favorable for the Dealer → The Player should lower their bet.
   * If X < 2.17, it means the number of High Group cards remains relatively high while the overall undealt deck has shrunk → the proportion of Low Group cards has dropped → the deck is becoming unfavorable for the Dealer → the Player should raise their bet.

Why Aces are considered advantageous for the Player:
There are two main scenarios:
   1. Ace dealt as one of the first two cards:
Only the Player side can score a Blackjack, so besides being able to count the Ace as 1 or 11, the Player has a relatively high chance that the other card is worth 10 (four 10s and twelve face cards), resulting in an automatic win with a 2x payout regardless of the Dealer’s hand.
Meanwhile, even if the Dealer gets a similar starting hand, they don’t benefit from the special Blackjack rule, and the Ace is simply counted as 1 or 11.
   2. Ace drawn during additional hits:
Even if the Dealer chooses to count Ace = 1 to avoid busting, the Player can do the same. The key difference is that the Player has much more flexibility in Blackjack. They can Stand at any time, whereas the Dealer is forced to Hit until they reach 17 and then must stop.
This means that if both sides count Ace = 1:
      * The Dealer increases the risk of busting with any additional draw (since their total has already increased by 1).
      * If they reach 17, they must stop there; the safest outcome is only 17 points.
The Player, on the other hand, gets a guaranteed +1 boost to their total with the Ace, and can still decide whether to Hit or Stand based on visible cards and card-counting strategy.

After the new rule was announced, even Daimon (the dumbest one) was able to reason that:

→ Conclusion: At this point, the game essentially turns into a rock-paper-scissors between 0, 1, and 2–100.
That preliminary conclusion alone wasn’t enough, so Daimon decided to break down each possible scenario in more detail. Below is the detailed explanation behind the table Daimon inferred:
Let “Other” represent all numbers from 2 to 100.
Let A, B, and C be the three players. A(x)/B(x)/C(x) means player A/B/C chooses number x, with x ∈ {0, 1, Other}.
For example, C(1) means player C chooses number 1.
Now, assume you are player A. We’ll analyze things purely from A’s perspective and only in terms of probability/value.
The number of combinations for each case A chooses = the combinations of B(0), B(1), B(k) and C(0), C(1), C(k) = 3² = 9 combinations.
Note: Since this is the phase where we analyze which of the three choices,0, 1, or Other, is the most optimal, we assume that each option has an equal probability of being chosen. Let’s analyze player A’s three possible choices:
A chooses “Other” – A(k):
Scenario 1 (Combo Group 1):
B and C both pick either 0 or 1, and they match → A always wins.
               * B(0), C(0)
               * B(1), C(1)
Scenario 2:
B and C both pick either 0 or 1, and they pick differently. → A always loses.
               * B(0), C(1)
               * B(1), C(0)
Scenario 3:
Both B and C choose numbers from the “Other” group.
               * B(k), C(k)
The outcomes:
                  * B(k) = C(k) ≠ A(k) → A wins
                  * A(k) = B(k) ≠ C(k) → A loses
                  * A(k) = C(k) ≠ B(k) → A loses
                  * A(k) = B(k) = C(k) → Tie/all lose
                  * A(k) ≠ B(k) ≠ C(k) → If A(k) is the smallest, A wins
→ Because the chance of two players choosing the same number from 2–100 is negligible (1/99²), we can ignore overlap in the “Other” group. Thus, only the highlighted red case is likely.
→ In that case, choosing A(2) is the best strategy, as it's the smallest number in the “Other” group, giving A the highest chance of being the lowest number.
Scenario 4:
Only B or C picks from “Other”:
                  * B(k), C(0) → If A(k) < B(k), A wins
A(k) ≠ B(k) → A(k) < B(k), A win
A(k) = B(k) → A loses (Negligible probability)
                  * B(k), C(1) → If A(k) < B(k), A wins
                  * B(0), C(k) → If A(k) < C(k), A wins
                  * B(1), C(k) → If A(k) < C(k), A wins
→ In all 4 cases, A(2) almost guarantees a win.
✅ Conclusion: Among all “Other” numbers, A(2) is statistically the best. So from here on, we simplify by treating “Other” = 2.
Theoretically: A(2) wins in:
Scenario 1 (2 wins) + Scenario 3 (1 win) + Scenario 4 (4 wins) = ~7 out of 9 combinations → Approx. 77.7% win rate
A chooses 1 – A(1):
Scenario 1:
                  * B(0), C(0) → A wins
                  * B(1), C(1) → Tie/all lose
Scenario 2:
                  * B(0), C(1)
                  * B(1), C(0) → A loses in both
Scenario 3:
                  * B(2), C(2) → A wins
Scenario 4:
                  * B(2), C(0) → A wins
