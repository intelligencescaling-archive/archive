[Chapters 46-60] Sōma makes a challenge to Hayama that he will make a curry better than his, despite not having the ability to recognize a curry leaf, the most basic spice in curry, and knowing about Hayama’s nose. There are typically about forty different spices used in most curry recipes. Ten to twenty are picked for each individual dish and blended. Megumi states that there must be over one million different possible combinations just from the spices alone (with ChatGPT calculating that it would be 250 trillion (take that with a grain of salt)), and that’s all excluding the choice of using the spice whole, ground or fresh, whether to enhance the scent of the spice by cooking in oil, dry roasting, fermenting or any variety of ways. He spends nearly an entire month trying to perfect his recipe. The fall classic begins, and everyone starts cooking up the dish they worked on the whole summer. Sōma in particular decided to create a “curry risotto omelette”. A rich, thick mix of chicken and beef bouillon. Ground beef and onions sauteed in butter until savory and tender, their umami-filled juices soaking into the rice. The creamy risotto melding into one with the soft, mildly sweet egg. That, combined with oyster sauce accented with a touch of honey, making a mildly tart flavor that is thick and heavy, creates two different levels of flavor. Hayama uses his sense of smell to try and guess how the flavor is so deep. The strong aroma and hint of bitterness means he used cumin and cardamom. The sting on the tongue comes from cloves, but all of these are surface things. He then catches the smell of mango in Sōma’s dish. Sōma had used his family’s homemade mango chutney. Also spelled "Chatney" or "Chatni," chutney is a south asian condiment. Spices and herbs are mixed with mashed fruit or vegetables and then simmered into paste. A wide variety of combinations are possible, resulting in chutneys that can be sweet, spicy or even minty. The mango acts as an axle, running through and connecting the disparate flavors of all the spices and giving a deeper, full-bodied flavor to the overall-dish. In a way, it's practical, applied spice tech. In India where it originated, chutneys are always served on the side as condiments. It's only in japan that chutney is added directly into a curry, so it’s unconventional from the standpoint of original india curry. But by using the chutney, he massively improved the flavor and richness of the overall dish without resorting to using an excess of oils or animal products. From just 3 out of the 5 judges alone, Sōma got 93 points, only losing to Hayama’s dish by a single point. [Chapters 62-] His first matchup in the quarter finals was against Alice Nakiri, someone so skilled in molecular gastronomy that she won almost every single award in an international competition when she was less than ten years old. He decides to create a seaweed bento prototype. He beer batters it, because alcohol is more volatile, it evaporates out of the batter faster than water does when dunked in the frying oil, making for a lighter, crispier texture. He also used pickled leeks and salt-pickled shibazuke veggies instead of pickled relish for the tartar sauce. That makes the flavor mellow as opposed to tangy. It works really well with the rice too. Plus, keeping the sides on top of the hot rice makes it harder for them to go cold in the bento box. Its mild, salty aroma and mellow savory flavor also brings balance to the entire dish. The seaweed bento has the best flavor cohesion and overall savory power, which is why Soma believes it has big hidden potential, and why he was to improve it in order to beat Alice.
