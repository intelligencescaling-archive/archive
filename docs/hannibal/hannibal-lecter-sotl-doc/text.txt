Palazzo Vecchio (literally - Old Palace) is a building in Florence, on the Piazza della Signoria, built in 1299-1314 according to the design of Arnolfo di Cambio. One of the most famous buildings in the city.
The Palazzo Vecchio complex combines a palace with halls on two floors, courtyards, and the Arnolfo Tower with a clock. Once there was a prison in the tower, where criminals awaited sentencing, and today there is an observation deck with views of the Arno River, the Duomo, tiled roofs and Tuscan landscapes.

Structure (General description)
The majestic building on Piazza della Signoria resembles both an impregnable fortress and fragile lace. The building, which today houses the town hall, is both a museum and an observation deck. And if you add to this the magnificent interior decoration and royal interiors, coupled with a magnificent collection of works of Italian art, you get a striking landmark of the city of Florence.
Externally, the castle resembles a fortress - simple forms, without any special decorations. The facade is divided into three levels:

1. First floor
On the first floor of the Palazzo is the city hall, occupying one wing. In addition to the rooms in the Palazzo Vecchio, on the first floor are located: "The Hall of Five Hundred", an office for alchemical experiments, the room of Francesco Medici and others. "The Hall of Five Hundred" deserves special words. It was named so because 500 members of parliament sat in it. The walls of the hall are painted with frescoes depicting battle scenes, and on the ceiling you can see paintings (39 ceiling panels) glorifying Duke Cosimo Medici himself.

2. Second and third floor
The second floor is occupied by a museum. Actually, the second floor is several thematic halls. For example, the Hall of Lilies.  The pride of this hall is not so much the frescoes depicting saints and popes, but the ceiling, reminiscent of a golden honeycomb - in each of the cells on a blue background, golden lilies are painted, the symbol of the French Anjou family. In addition to this hall, on the second floor are the Hall of the Four Elements and the Hall of Maps. In the Hall of Maps, all the walls are occupied by 53 geographical maps painted by the monk Ignato Danti, telling about the geographical ideas of the Florentines. The center of the hall is occupied by a huge globe, installed in 1581 - a model of the globe of the late 16th century.

Fresco of the history of Marcus Furius Camillus in the Hall of Justice in the Palazzo Vecchio.
The building is crowned with a crenellated gallery, the windows of which serve as loopholes. Below the windows are small niches in which you can see heraldic signs.  There are nine of them - according to the number of guilds that headed the Florentine Republic. Entrance
The main entrance to the building is remarkable in itself - it is guarded by two lions, which is why the entrance was called the "Lion Gate". Between the lions on a blue background there is a Latin saying: "Rex Regum et Dominus Dominantium" (translated as "King of kings, lord of lords", is the personal monogram of Christ).

There are also two famous sculptures at the entrance: "David" by Michelangelo and "Hercules" by Baccio Bandinelli. However, the statues are skillfully made copies - the originals can be seen on the 2nd floor, in the Gallery. To get into the Palazzo building, you need to go through a small courtyard. The graceful columns supporting the vaults and the bas-reliefs with views of the cities were also made by Michelozzo.
The topmost tier of the palace is the Terrace of Saturn. It offers a wonderful view of the historical part of Florence.

Tower
The Arnolfo Tower (named after its creator) was built on the foundations of an older one, bearing the sonorous name of "La Vacca" (the very loud voice of the bell of this tower was like the roar of a bull - vacca). It was completed much later than the construction of the main palace - in 1314. More than 95 meters height, it rises above the Palazzo, simultaneously serving as an observation deck.

If you overcome a large number of steps and climb the tower itself, then the reward will be an excellent view of the city and its surrounding.

PHAZE I: Killing method

Context: As punishment for Miggs' death, Chilton took all the drawings and books from the cell, in short, everything except the bare necessities. During a casual conversation with Clarice, Hannibal decides to help with the Buffalo Bill investigation in exchange for a transfer to a federal hospital under the pretext of "a good view from the window" and says only one thing about him: that he lives on the second floor.

But how did Hannibal find this out?

Hannibal uses his reasoning to reconstruct the chain of events that led to the victims' deaths:

First, Hannibal knew two things:

1. There was no clear connection between where Bill kidnapped the young women and where he dumped them.

2. In some cases, the bodies were found quickly enough to determine the exact time of death.

Based on these two premises, Hannibal makes several logical conclusions: Bill kept them alive for some time. These victims died only a week to ten days after being abducted → This meant that he must have had a place to keep them and a place where he could work in privacy → This meant that he was not a vagrant. Second, Hannibal deduces Buffalo Bill's method of killing. Both victims had high ligature marks and displaced cervical vertebrae – two clear signs of hanging. Hannibal, from his own experience, understands how difficult it is for one person to hang another against their will. It is difficult to hang someone else – even when tied up, the victim still manages to stand up if there is any support. The killer must have somehow completely subdued the victim, but how is that possible? Well, it is possible if the victim knows nothing about the killer's intentions.

This leads him to conclude that the hanging was carefully planned.  This is not just murder, but the result of a cunning plan involving psychological manipulation. The key idea is that for a successful hanging, especially if the victim is capable of resistance, the killer must create an atmosphere of trust. The victim is blindfolded and led to believe that they are being taken to a safe place (such as a bathroom), thereby lowering their guard and caution.

This is how the whole process looks through the eyes of the victim: Well, he blindfolded me so that I couldn't see his face or look at the house, and probably in this case he will let me live, which gives me hope. He told me we were going to the bathroom. I have nothing to fear from death, at least for now. I will obediently submit to him to increase my chances of survival.

The killer then leads them to the stairs himself, puts on a hood, puts on a noose, and throws them off the top step, tying the rope to the railing of the landing. This makes sense if we are talking about a two-story house with stairs. If Buffalo Bill did not have a staircase, he would probably have resorted to another method of killing.

Hannibal highlights two key aspects: first, the physical act of hanging, which requires certain conditions and manipulations, and second, the psychological preparation of the victim, which allows him to avoid resistance during the act.

Here is Hannibal's chain of logical reasoning:
1) From the two facts known to me, I conclude that BB kept his victims alive for another week → 2) He has a specific hideout → 3) Based on two characteristics, I note that both of BB's victims were hanged → 4) I know from my own experience that it is incredibly difficult for one person to hang another, especially if the victim resists → 5) The killer gained the victim's complete trust, which is only possible if he hid his intentions → 6) The killer has a plan: to blindfold the victim under the pretext that she is going to the bathroom → 7) The killer wants to hang the victim on the stairs, which is only possible in a two-story house → 8) Buffalo Bill lives in a two-story house.

This is an incredibly complex thought process that Hannibal went through in a couple of minutes.

What is this smell?
Trans-3-methyl-2-hexenoic acid is an organic compound belonging to the carboxylic acid class. The base structure is 2-hexenoic acid, which is a six-carbon carboxylic acid with a double bond located between the second and third carbon atoms, counting from the carboxyl group. What is catatonia?
Catatonia is a psychomotor disorder in which a person exhibits abnormal motor and behavioral responses. It can manifest as stupor (complete lack of movement and reaction to external stimuli), excitement (meaningless or repetitive movements), negativism (resistance to attempts to change posture or follow commands), mutism, strange postures or repetitive movements, echopraxia (repetition of other people's actions), and echolalia (repetition of words heard).

It's impossible to recreate catatonia in a tangible form, namely through the smell of goat sweat: catatonia has no physical substance; it is a complex psychomotor syndrome that combines motor, emotional, and cognitive manifestations. Catatonia includes stupor, in which a person is completely immobile and does not respond to external stimuli; excitement, manifested by meaningless or repetitive movements; mutism and negativism, when the patient resists attempts to change position or follow commands; strange, fixed postures and echopraxia with echolalia. All of these manifestations are recorded through clinical observation and standardized scales, such as the Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS), and can only be assessed indirectly, through observation of the patient's behavior, activity, and reactions. Empirical data from psychophysiology and psychiatry confirm that people with schizophrenia and catatonia may exhibit specific changes in biochemical and physiological markers, but these are highly variable and nonspecific. For example, studies of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in sweat in psychiatric patients have shown that sweat composition may change in some schizophrenic states, but these changes do not correlate with the severity of catatonia and are highly dependent on individual factors: gender, diet, stress levels, and medication. Analysis of electrophysiological data, such as EEG, reveals only general patterns of decreased activity or dysregulation of cortical rhythms, but they do not provide a direct insight into a person's mental state. Actography and motor activity monitoring record decreased or abnormal motor activity, but this is a superficial external projection of internal mental processes, not a complete analysis. Imago concept

Imago (from Latin imago, meaning "image" or "likeness") is a psychological term referring to an unconscious internal image of a significant other, formed in childhood and exerting a profound influence on perception, emotions, and interpersonal relationships in adulthood. First introduced by Carl Gustav Jung, this term was developed in psychoanalysis and especially in Harville Hendrix's Imago Therapy. The essence of the phenomenon is that a child, interacting with parents and other important figures, creates stable internal images – emotionally colored representations of what love, care, power, approval, and rejection "are like." These images do not disappear but become unconscious matrices through which a person interprets other people and chooses partners. In short, Imago is a psychological construct that combines the traits of early objects of love and pain. Without realizing it, adults tend to look for partners who combine the positive and negative qualities of their childhood figures in order to intuitively "finish" their old emotional history, heal their inner wounds, or restore their lost sense of integrity. Therefore, many relationships repeat the same scenarios – not because "that's how it turned out," but because unconscious Imago patterns are at work. The psychological function of this image is to organize perception and behavior: it determines who we find attractive or dangerous, whom we trust, whom we reject, and what qualities we consider normal. In therapy, working with the Imago is aimed at realizing and separating the real person from the projection of the inner image, which allows us to build mature relationships free from childhood scenarios. In other words, the Imago is a deep mirror of our past through which we see the present until we learn to look directly.

Telepathy is the ability to directly transmit and receive thoughts, images, feelings, and unconscious states from one mind to another without using known sensory channels (e.g., verbal language or visual signals). The term was introduced in 1882 by Frederic W. H. Myers, one of the founders of the British Society for Psychical Research. In the absence of empirical evidence in controlled experiments, such a phenomenon is only possible in science fiction; it inevitably follows that if X possesses telepathy, his abilities will exceed the cognitive capabilities of any human being ever known to the world.
