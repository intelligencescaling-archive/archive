Used Difficulties for Outcome Estimation:

NOTE: Difficulties of an outcome are given estimations by the person scaling the characters in question.

Legend:

- Difficulty estimations can find themselves in between certain difficulties, representing a middle ground between the two.
Example: No-Very Low Diff; High-Very High Diff;

- The symbols:
 (+) - Implies the difficulty could be higher than what is estimated.
 (-) - Implies the difficulty could be lower than what is estimated.

Scaling:
Will take a standard character A vs B matchup for reference:

No Diff – Almost nothing would be able to change the outcome of this match. Character A would have every advantage and be significantly stronger than Character B in almost every aspect. Character A should still be able to guarantee victory even if they were to self-sabotage or to place themselves at a massive disadvantage.

Very Low Diff – This means that Character A’s victory would provide little challenge at all. Even their basic skill set would be enough to ensure victory. At this point, even if Character A were to underestimate Character B, that would do little to help Character B’s chances of winning given the massive overall difference in skill. Character A could still ensure victory even from a significantly disadvantageous position.

Low Diff – This means that Character A would not need to put forth their full effort to guarantee victory. They have shown lesser feats or skills which would be enough to guarantee victory. This could also mean that Character A is superior to Character B is most or all facets of outsmarting. In that case Character A wouldn’t have a weakness that could even potentially allow Character B to win. Character A could ensure victory even from a disadvantageous position.

Mid Diff – This means that an average showing from Character A is enough to guarantee victory over Character B. They wouldn’t likely need to utilize their top end skills to ensure their win and Character B even with their best showing wouldn’t have a chance to win.

High Diff – Character A would need to utilize their full potential, but doing so would guarantee their victory. Character A using their full skillset and effort will ensure they come out ahead.

Very High Diff – Character A would need to use their full skillset and be fully prepared against an opponent who isn’t too far below them. However, as long as they don’t underestimate Character B, their full effort will be enough to ensure their win. It will take Character A’s best showing to ensure this. Despite this being close Character A’s efforts should manage to beat Character B.

Extreme Diff - This means that Character A’s victory would prove to be extremely difficult to guarantee, this represents the highest level of opponent they could beat more often than not. Situation variety and context are almost as relevant as they can ever be in determining the winner, as for example Character A may be superior to character B in some facets of outsmarting, but Character B might counter Character A very well. This could also be opposite where Character A counters well despite a slightly inferior skillset.

Could Go Either Way - Represents a 50/50 matchup. In this case the matchup is too close to declare an overall winner. Neither character’s full effort would be enough to guarantee victory against the other without some kind of additional circumstances. One character ever so slightly underestimating the other for example may be the difference between victory and defeat.

AI Detailed Category Explanation: Work in progress
A synthesis for all the mentioned categories in full scale, explaining their meaning and scaling application.

Full Scale Intelligence Quotient
Or known as “FSIQ” is an intelligence Scaling concept stemming from the General Factor Theory of Intelligence, which is used in psychometrics to determine how intelligent an individual is.
The standard used from General Factor theory into a practical quantification is done through a specific IQ test/scale, these lay out the proper assignments and criteria of evaluation done while respecting g theory. Examples include the Stanford Binet and the Wechler’s Scaling tests, both coming in various models which improve as time passes.
In SCD the standard IQ test used for scaling is the one by Wechler, specifically WISC-V (featuring 5 primary indexes in the form of WMI, PSI, VSI, FRI, VCI)
The problem with WISC-V’s application in SCD edits is that it is an IQ test designed specifically for children, as its full name suggests - Wechler’s Intelligence Scale for Children - V. It is not meant to be used for majors despite there theoretically not being an issue with comparing the specific metrics used in the test on adults and fictional characters. The reason WISC-V is used over WAIS-IV (Wechler’s Adult Intelligence Scale) is because WAIS is an older IQ test which is not as intricate and developed as the latest version of WISC is.
This will be a solved problem when WAIS V appears in September 2024.
For this reason, the Full Scale Intelligence Quotient I use is something I call a custom deviation of WAIS IV derived from WISC V, as an improvisation until WAIS V appears, due to personal preference over using the specific indexes and scaling normalized by WISC V.
All the Indexes and Subtests used in this deviation come from WISC-V, even if they are applied as WAIS technically.

Working Memory Index
WMI measures one’s ability to take in, retain, and recall pieces of information.
Working memory is a mental control process characterized as a system which can ACTIVELY retain quantities of information for a short period of time. New information in working memory is temporary. It is either encoded into long-term memory or it decays or is replaced. Information in working memory has a short duration of around 20+ seconds unless we rehearse it or attend to it.
Working Memory is involved to a degree in any cognitive task, as they all require information storage. Feats in working memory, either taking in info, retaining it for periods of time or recalling information from LTM all are measured based on how much information was worked with / how little the timeframe of the execution/memory storage was.
For a complete distillation of the category, the 3 subtests used which measure working memory to a high degree are:

Digit Span - assessing one’s ability to recall numbers he was given to listen to in a specific order. - Auditory + Verbal
Picture Span -  assessing one’s ability to recall pictures he was given to view by giving him a selection of tasks testing how well he can recall their order and similarities. - Visual + Non-Verbal
Letter-Number Sequencing - assessing an individual's ability to manipulate and remember sequences of letters and numbers. - Visual + Verbal

Processing Speed Index
PSI measures the speed and accuracy of visual identification, decision making, and decision implementation. Emphasis is put on all 3 of the mentioned tasks if they can be together, as the more of these a specific feat carries, the more correlated with what CPI is.
Active processing of information via thinking or using cognitive processes beyond just retaining information in one’s memory is an ideal example of processing speed involving perception, decision making + implementation.
In Processing Speed, there’s 3 subtest used which measure one’s capacity in the index if there’s ever need for a distillation:

Coding - assessment through viewing a series of numbers written in rows on a page, then drawing a corresponding symbol under each number.
Symbol Search -assessment through displaying symbol association in a limited period of time.
Cancellation - assessment that requires scanning a structured arrangement of colored shapes and marking the targets while avoiding distractors.

Verbal Comprehension Index
VCI measures the individual’s ability to access and apply acquired word knowledge, meaning accessing his vocabulary, expressing himself in a meaningful manner, and applying reasoning skills to information presented verbally. It also demonstrates the breadth and depth of knowledge acquired from one’s environment, as it measures the retrieval from long-term memory of such information.
VCI has 4 relevant subtests:

Similarities - assessment through viewing a set of words and then describing how they are specifically similar. It primarily involves use of verbal analogies. So feats of solving verbal puzzles usually provide primary scaling for this subtest.
Vocabulary - assessment through understanding the meanings/definitions of words. Generally measures specific word knowledge and understanding in A SINGLE language, and does not encompass general linguistic skills.
Information - assessment of general cultural knowledge, long-term memory, and acquired facts. Straight forward enough.
Comprehension - assessment of environmental knowledge, knowledge of general principles, practical judgment in various situations encountered daily, social maturation, and development of moral conscience.
Basically, understanding practical uses of words to navigate social activity, comprehending information (either verbally or visually) by understanding its social applications, common sense and moral conscience.

Fluid Reasoning Index
FRI measures one’s ability to detect the underlying conceptual relationships and use reasoning to identify and apply rules.

Matrix Reasoning - assessment through analyzing colored matrices and visual patterns with something missing, and then selecting the missing piece from a range of options. Matrix Reasoning measures visual processing, abstract reasoning and problem solving. Most importantly, it has a very high use of Inductive Reasoning.

Figure Weights - assessment through presenting several balance scales with objects placed on each scale to make them balance, with one side of one scale left blank.  The individual will be selecting an item from a list of objects that can be placed in the blank space to balance that particular scale.  These items also become increasingly complex as the subtest progresses.
It has a high use of both Inductive and Deductive Reasoning.

Picture Concepts - assessment through analyzing several rows of pictured objects and indicating the picture from each row that shares a characteristic in common with the ones from the other rows. It mainly measures nonverbal, categorical, abstract reasoning. A supplemental subtest with high correlation for not only FRI, but VSI as well. It has a very high use of Inductive Reasoning.

Arithmetic - assessment of one’s numerical accuracy, logical reasoning and mental arithmetic ability. This is a supplemental subtest with high correlation for not only FRI, but WMI as well. Additionally, it has a very high use of Deductive Reasoning.

Visual Spatial Index
VSI measures the individual’s ability to evaluate details and understand and manipulate visual/spatial relationships.

Block Design - assessment through analyzing and synthesizing an abstract design and reproducing that design from colored plastic blocks. Spatial visualization and analysis, simultaneous processing, visual-motor coordination, and nonverbal concept formation are involved.

Visual Puzzles - assessment through selecting 2D figures from a group that can be arranged in order to match a presented figure.  The shapes can be flipped and rotated as necessary, but the shapes are visually evaluated and their arrangement is determined without physical manipulation.
This is a timed subtest, with the shapes becoming more complex as it progresses. This subtest involves Observation, working memory and abstract reasoning, as well as the same qualities in Block Design.

Intelligence Concepts
Crystallized Intelligence -The accumulated knowledge, skills, and information acquired through education and experience over time. This includes vocabulary, factual knowledge, learned procedures, and more.

Fluid Intelligence - The ability to reason, solve novel problems, and adapt to new situations independent of previously acquired knowledge. It involves abstract thinking, pattern recognition, and quick learning.

General Factor (g factor) - A theoretical construct in intelligence research proposing that a single, general intelligence factor underlies and influences performance across various cognitive tasks and abilities. Basically scaled on whoever has better overall cognitive abilities has a better G factor.

Gardner’s Theory - A theory proposes Multiple Intelligences, suggesting that intelligence is not a single, unified capacity but consists of distinct types of intelligences.  These intelligences include the following:

-
Linguistic - The ability to effectively use language for communication and expression.
Logical-mathematical - The capacity to think logically, solve mathematical problems, and analyze data.
Spatial - The ability  to visualize and manipulate objects and spatial relationships in one's mind.
Musical - The aptitude to recognize, create, and understand musical pitches, tones, and rhythms.
Bodily-kinesthetic - The ability to control one's body movements and handle objects skillfully.
Interpersonal - The capacity to understand and interact effectively with others.
Intrapersonal - The ability to understand oneself, including one's thoughts and feelings.
Naturalistic - The skill to recognize, categorize, and understand the natural world.
Existential - The capacity to tackle deep questions about human existence and meaning.
-

Triarchic Theory - The Triarchic Theory of Intelligence, developed by Robert Sternberg, posits that intelligence consists of three components: Analytical Intelligence (problem-solving and logical reasoning), Creative Intelligence (innovation and novel thinking), and Practical Intelligence (everyday adaptability and "street smarts"). It emphasizes the importance of balancing all three aspects It also suggests that intelligence is more than just academic abilities, and proposes that people can be intelligent in different ways

PASS Theory -The PASS Theory of Intelligence posits that cognitive functioning comprises four interrelated processes: Planning, Attention, Simultaneous Processing, and Successive Processing. These processes work together to form the basis of intelligent behavior, with Planning acting as the executive function that utilizes information from the other three processes. This theory views intelligence as a dynamic interaction of cognitive processes rather than a single, fixed ability.

Tactical Potency - Effectiveness or power of a given tactic in achieving its intended result.

Tactical Synergy - Tactical synergy occurs when different tactics or elements within a plan work together in such a way that their combined effect is greater than the sum of their individual effects.

Tactical Management - This refers to the management of resources, personnel, and operations at a tactical level. It involves planning, organizing, directing, and controlling tactical operations to meet strategic objectives.

Field Skills
Setting Traps - The strategic act of creating situations or devices, whether physical or psychological, designed to deceive, capture, or influence, where the unsuspecting target is led into a scenario that exploits their behaviors, decisions, or vulnerabilities for the trapper's advantage.

Contingencies - Specific single moves incorporated in a specific set of actions as backup, meant to deal with unfavorable situations in case the main plan is partially or not successful.

Risk Calculation - Assessing risk within offensive or active tactics, reliant on tactical analysis, verification, considering variables, self awareness and more. Reminder, risk calculations only refers to assessing risk, not at the methods of dealing with it.

Information Gathering -   Information gathering is the systematic collection, organization, and analysis of data or facts related to a specific topic, problem, or question.

Multitasking - Multitasking refers to the practice of engaging in more than one task or activity simultaneously or in rapid succession.

Counteraction
Trap Evasion - The skill of recognizing and avoiding physical or strategic traps set by an opponent. This includes identifying potential dangers and maneuvering to prevent entrapment.

Improvising - The ability to create and execute unplanned solutions in response to unexpected situations. It involves quick thinking, creativity, and adaptability to use available resources effectively.

Tactical Responsiveness -The capacity to rapidly assess a situation and react appropriately at a small-scale, immediate level. This involves making quick decisions and adjusting plans in real-time based on changing circumstances.

Field Navigation - The skill of orienting oneself and moving effectively through various terrains or environments. This includes reading maps, using compasses or GPS, understanding topography, and selecting optimal routes. Or, the ability to effectively move through situations or social environments.

Strategy
Strategy Logistics - The planning and management of resources, information, and operations necessary to execute a strategy effectively. This involves coordinating various elements to ensure smooth implementation of strategic plans.
Strategy Alignment -  The process of ensuring that all components of an organization or system are coordinated and working towards the same strategic goals. This includes aligning people, processes, and resources with the overall strategy.
Strategy Fortitude -  The ability to maintain commitment and perseverance in pursuing a chosen strategy, especially in the face of challenges or setbacks. It involves resilience and the strength to stay the course when difficulties arise.
Strategy Flexibility -The capacity to adapt and modify strategic plans in response to changing circumstances or new information. This involves being responsive to feedback and willing to adjust tactics while maintaining overall strategic direction.

Strategy Complexity -  The level of intricacy and interconnectedness in a strategic plan. Complex strategies often involve multiple variables, contingencies, and layers of action. This can refer to both the depth of the strategy and the challenges in implementing it.

Strategy Efficiency - The optimal use of resources in executing a strategy to achieve maximum results with minimal waste. This involves streamlining processes, prioritizing actions, and focusing on high-impact elements of the strategy.

Planning
Preparation Process - The process of a plan’s preparation scales the character’s ability to logically align different tactics and/or strategy over the course of a plan’s lifespan.

Planning Versatility - The ability to create plans that are adaptable to various scenarios and can be effectively applied across different contexts or situations. This involves developing strategies that can be easily modified or repurposed as circumstances change.

Planning Intricacies - The level of detail and complexity incorporated into a plan. This includes the ability to consider and integrate multiple layers of action, contingencies, and subtle nuances that can affect the plan's execution and outcome.

Planning Formulation - a measurement of the character’s ability to come up with a logically coherent plan within a relatively short amount of time.

Planning Applicability - The degree to which a plan is relevant and can be effectively implemented in that situations. This involves creating strategies that are not just theoretically sound but also practical and executable given the available resources and constraints.

Planning Coverage - The coverage of a plan shows the character’s ability to take into account as much as possible when developing a scheme. This includes various outcomes, variables, potential hazards as well as minimizing any uncertainties within the plan’s lifespan through ensuring guarantees.

Thinking
Critical Thinking - The objective analysis and evaluation of an issue to form a judgment. It involves questioning assumptions, examining evidence, and reasoning logically.

Analytical Thinking - Breaking down complex information into smaller parts to understand relationships between them. It focuses on examining components systematically to draw conclusions.

Systematic Thinking -Approaching problems or situations in an organized, step-by-step manner. It involves creating structured processes to understand or solve issues.
Analogical Thinking - Using comparisons or analogies to understand new concepts or solve problems by relating them to familiar ideas or situations.

Associative Thinking - Connecting ideas, concepts, or experiences that may not have obvious relationships. It often leads to creative insights or novel solutions.

Holistic Thinking - Considering systems or problems as wholes rather than just as collections of parts. It focuses on understanding how components interact within a larger context.

Decompositional Thinking - Breaking down complex problems or systems into smaller, more manageable components to analyze and solve them more effectively.

Linear Thinking - Processing information in a straight-line, step-by-step manner. It follows a clear, logical progression from one point to the next.

Non Linear Thinking - An approach that doesn't follow a straight line but instead explores multiple directions or possibilities simultaneously. It often involves intuition and creativity.

Convergent Thinking - The process of finding a single, well-established answer to a problem. It involves applying rules or logical reasoning to arrive at one correct solution.

Divergent Thinking -The ability to generate multiple, unique ideas or solutions to a problem. It involves creativity and the exploration of many possible outcomes.

Abstract Thinking - The capacity to think about intangible qualities and concepts that are not physically present. It involves understanding complex ideas, recognizing patterns, and making generalizations.

Reasoning
Deductive Reasoning - A logical approach that moves from general premises to a specific conclusion. It involves applying broad principles or rules to reach a logical and certain conclusion about a particular case.
Inductive Reasoning -A method of reasoning that moves from specific observations to broader generalizations or theories. It involves drawing likely conclusions based on patterns observed in specific cases.
Abductive Reasoning - A form of logical inference that seeks the simplest and most likely explanation for an observation or set of observations. It's often described as "inference to the best explanation."

Caution - The trait of being extremely careful in one's actions to avoid potential risks or mistakes.

Bias Management - The ability to recognize, control, or mitigate personal or systemic biases in decision-making processes.

Ruthlessness - The quality of lacking pity or compassion for others, acting solely in one's own interests or towards one's goals without regard for the consequences to others.

Judgment
Decision Making - The cognitive process resulting in the selection of a belief or a course of action among several possible alternative options. Decision making can be based on explicit or tacit knowledge and involves assessing risks, benefits, and potential outcomes. It can be rational, based on logical processes, or intuitive, relying on gut feelings.

Situational Awareness - The perception of environmental elements and events with respect to time or space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their future status.

Intuition - The ability to understand or know something immediately, without the need for conscious reasoning.

Metacognition - Metacognition is the awareness and understanding of one's own thought processes; it involves the ability to control and reflect on how you learn, think, and solve problems.

 3. Explaining passive-aggressive comments:
