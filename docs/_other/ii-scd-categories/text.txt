Introduction and Notes:

For newcomers to SCD, the most difficult aspect to grasp is the definition of these categories. Because SCD is an evolving community that has formed step-by-step over a long period, it has gone through many phases to reach its current state; new categories have been added, and some have been phased out. Furthermore, there is no single academic source where all of these categories are compiled and defined. In addition, most volunteer documents that attempt to explain them are either outdated or lack sufficient scope in this regard. The purpose of this document is to make SCD categories understandable for everyone. Please remember that SCD is still evolving, and there is no strict consensus on the point systems. Because of this, it is highly likely that you will encounter variations in point allocation or come across new categories—such as using "Scheming" instead of "Strategy and Planning," or "Theory of Mind" instead of "EQ." However, the ultimate goal of this document is to systematize the SCD categories as much as possible and explain the traditional, generally accepted ones.
Full Scale IQ
Full Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) is a comprehensive scoring system that measures an individual's cognitive capacity. It represents the total score obtained from the Wechsler Scales, such as the WISC-V for children or the WAIS-IV for adults—the most widely used intelligence tests in the world—and is used to express human intelligence numerically.
Although the sub-categories of the IQ test vary from version to version, it generally consists of five widely accepted tests: Processing Speed Index (PSI), Working Memory Index (WMI), Fluid Reasoning Index (FRI), Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI), and Visual Spatial Index (VSI).
* PSI
Processing Speed Index (PSI) is the speed at which an individual perceives visual stimuli, processes the relationships between these stimuli mentally, and subsequently produces a motor response. PSI essentially measures three inseparable stages: visual perception, cognitive processing, and motor response. Efficiency across all three of these stages is reflected in the PSI score. It measures how rapidly and accurately you process visual information and convert it into a response.

* WMI
Working Memory Index (WMI) measures the capacity to actively hold information in the mental workspace, perform simultaneous processing on that information, and subsequently produce an output. As an example of this, we can cite an individual mentally sequencing numbers they have seen. Typically, in WAIS/WISC tests, you are presented with a series of numbers and asked to list them either in the order you saw them or in reverse order. However, these do not necessarily have to be numbers; pictures (similar visual elements) or sounds can also be incorporated into this process in the same manner.

* Example feat for this 2 categories
The best example feat we can provide in this regard is Ayanokoji Kiyotaka's FMA (Flash Mental Arithmetic) feat. In FMA tests, you must first visually perceive the data that flashes on the screen in a fraction of a second. After perceiving it, you need to hold these numbers in your mind and simultaneously add them up. In other words, you are required to instantly process the visual information, manipulate it mentally (perform mathematical operations on it), and ultimately put it into practice by producing the correct answer (output).
* FRI
The Fluid Reasoning Index (FRI) is an index that measures an individual's capacity to solve new and unfamiliar problems they encounter, independent of what they have previously learned. Three fundamental forms of thinking come into play here. The first is inductive reasoning: the individual looks at multiple clues presented to them and independently discovers the underlying common rule; finding the missing matrix piece in the Matrix Reasoning subtest requires exactly this. The second is deductive reasoning, and contrary to popular belief, it is also used in the FRI; in the Figure Weights subtest, the individual derives a rule of balance from observations on a scale and applies this rule to a situation they have never seen before, meaning they first find the rule and then use it. The third is abstract reasoning: the individual can think solely through shapes and relationships without needing concrete objects or words; this characteristic makes the FRI one of the indices most independent of cultural and educational background. It also covers how you adapt to new situations and problems; since most of the questions you’re asked involve new and unique patterns, they require you to adapt to these problems.
(Note: Inductive, deductive, adaptability and abstract reasoning are not individual subtests; these concepts are used to explain which thought processes the subtests activate.)
Matrix Reasoning subtest is a non-verbal assessment that measures an individual’s ability to identify the hidden rule in a visual pattern and fill in the missing piece. In this test, you are presented with a grid of shapes arranged according to a specific logic, and you are asked to identify which option should be placed in the empty box.

Figure Weights is a visual subtest that measures an individual’s logical and quantitative reasoning skills. In this test, you are shown a set of balances with various geometric shapes on them; you are then asked which options should be placed on the empty pan to keep the final balance in equilibrium.
* VCI
The Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) is an index that measures an individual's capacity to understand language-based information, establish relationships between concepts, and express them verbally. The fundamental difference between the VCI and other indices is that it directly reflects the knowledge base the individual has acquired up to that point, their vocabulary, and their ability to think through language; therefore, the VCI is considered the index most influenced by cultural and educational background.
* VSI
Visual Spatial Index (VSI) is an index that measures an individual's capacity to perceive visual details, understand visual-spatial relationships, and mentally or physically construct geometric designs. The fundamental difference between the VSI and other indices is that it requires analyzing the positions of objects in space, the relationship of parts to one another, and whole-part connections without the need for verbal explanation. One of the important points is that the VSI does not necessarily require visualization; understanding visual-spatial relationships is also VSI and does not necessarily need visualization, and not every visualization is VSI.
* Last comments about FSIQ
Familiarizing yourself with the subtests can contribute to your understanding of these categories; however, what is truly important is grasping what each category measures at its core. The reason for this is that in real life, or when evaluating fictional characters, no one sits in a testing room answering standardized questions. Therefore, to make sense of the cognitive profile of a character or a real individual, you need to understand the fundamental mechanisms underlying these subtests, not the subtests itself.

EI (Emotional Intelligence)
Emotional intelligence is an individual’s ability to recognize and understand both their own emotional processes and those of others, and to use this understanding in a functional way to guide their thoughts and behaviors.
* Emotional Perception
Emotional perception is the capacity of an individual to detect and recognize the presence of emotions in their own internal states as well as in other people, objects, works of art, or environments through perceptual channels. It involves more of a superficial perception of an emotion than a deep analysis llike understanding that someone is angry from their facial expression, but understanding why they are angry is no longer emotional perception.
* Emotional Understanding
Emotional understanding goes beyond simply recognizing emotions as labels; it is the capacity to grasp the relationships between emotions, the meanings they convey, and how they evolve over time. the ability to discern subtle nuances in emotional concepts; the ability to interpret what emotions reveal about relationships and contexts; the ability to simultaneously grasp complex and contradictory emotional patterns; and the ability to anticipate under what conditions one emotion might transform into another.
* Let’s clarify the difference
EP refers to understanding emotions at a superficial level by using our perceptions; to illustrate this, we can describe Ed Rothstaylor’s feat in Chapter 11 of the web novel.
In Chapter 11 of the novel, after Ed Rothstaylor meets with Dean McDowell, he throws out bait by praising himself in order to confirm the trap set by the Princess. Hearing this, Dean McDowell is momentarily surprised. Ed describes this as lasting less than a second, meaning it is a micro-expression. According to Ed’s observation, Dean McDowell is surprised by this (EP), and the reason for his surprise is that Ed managed to figure out the Princess’s test and immediately adapt to it, despite having such limited information (EU).
* Emotional Facilitation (Formerly known as Emotional Engagement)
Emotional facilitation is the ability to use one’s emotional states in a functional way to support, guide, and enrich thought processes. In this process, emotions do not act as an obstacle to thinking; rather, they function as a tool that sets thinking in motion, prioritizes it, and deepens it.
* Emotional Management
Emotional management is an individual’s ability to manage both their own and others’ emotional states and to do so in a way that supports emotional and intellectual growth.

SI (Social Intelligence)
* Social Cognition
Social cognition is a cognitive process that describes how an individual perceives, processes, and interprets other people, social situations, and group dynamics. While Emotional Understanding (EU) focuses on an individual’s ability to directly read emotions, social cognition is concerned with understanding society’s unwritten rules, hierarchical structures, status balances, and the roles individuals play within this complex network.
* Social Integration
Social integration is your ability to adapt strategically and functionally to the norms, values, and internal dynamics of the social structure, group, or community to which you belong. This process involves an individual transitioning from being an outsider to becoming a legitimate part of that social network by constructing a social identity (persona) aligned with their own goals.
General Intelligence
General intelligence refers to the entirety of the mental capacity an individual consistently demonstrates across different cognitive domains. That is, intelligence is not a single skill, but rather the sum of multiple cognitive processes that support each other and work together.
* Fluid Intelligence
Fluid intelligence refers to an individual's capacity for on-the-spot reasoning and problem-solving in situations they have never encountered before, independent of their accumulated knowledge. In fact, the relationship between the FRI and fluid intelligence requires an important note: the FRI can be considered equivalent to Gf to a certain extent, as the primary purpose of the FRI is to measure fluid intelligence in a controlled testing environment. In SCD, however, fluid intelligence is evaluated by two criteria. In terms of learning ability, the character demonstrates this by how quickly they can grasp a situation or system they have never been exposed to before; the determining factor here is not memorization or experience, but the capacity to extract rules from within that moment. In terms of adaptation, on the other hand, the character stands out by how quickly and flexibly they can restructure their existing framework when conditions change unexpectedly.
* Crystallized Intelligence
Crystallized intelligence refers to how effectively an individual can use what they have learned throughout their life when faced with real situations. That is, the point is not how much knowledge is accumulated, but how functionally that accumulation can be activated in times of need. A person may have studied a subject for years; however, crystallized intelligence is measured by whether or not that knowledge is put into practice in a concrete situation in real life. Therefore, an individual with strong crystallized intelligence does not have to solve a problem they encounter from scratch; they can draw upon their past experiences and what they have learned to move directly to application.

Adversity Capacity (Stoltz CORE Model 1997)
Adversity Capacity refers to the quality of one’s response to challenges, obstacles, or unexpected adverse conditions, as well as the resilience demonstrated during this process. What is measured here is not the challenge itself, but the nature of the cognitive and behavioral response to that challenge.
* Control
Control is the perception of the extent to which one can influence a situation when faced with a challenge or adverse circumstance. Control does not mean taking complete control of the situation; rather, its essence lies in being able to identify a scope for action—even if only in one aspect of the challenge—and acting accordingly. High Control involves recognizing that there is a point of intervention even in a seemingly hopeless situation; low Control, on the other hand, entails accepting the situation as unchangeable from the outset and remaining passive.
* Ownership
Ownership refers to the extent to which one takes responsibility for the consequences of an adverse situation. This dimension emphasizes accountability for how the situation will be resolved, rather than focusing on its source. High ownership means actively taking ownership of the resolution process, regardless of who or what caused the situation. Low Ownership, on the other hand, involves shifting responsibility outward, adopting a passive stance, and avoiding the creation of solutions.
* Reach
Reach is the perception of how much an experienced difficulty spills over into other areas of life. If you have a high reach, a single negative experience spreads into independent areas such as work, relationships, or identity and creates a destructive impact there as well. Low Reach, on the other hand, refers to the ability to keep the boundaries of the difficulty narrow by confining that experience strictly to the domain where it truly belongs. In this dimension, healthy functioning is indicated by low scores rather than high ones.

* Endurance
Endurance is the perceived duration of an experienced difficulty and its effects. High Endurance refers to evaluating a negative situation as a temporary process rather than seeing it as permanent and inevitable, and maintaining hope and persistence accordingly. Low Endurance, on the other hand, brings with it the belief that the difficulty will never end and a subsequent tendency to give up.

Thinking
Thinking is the process of establishing connections between information, images, and concepts in an individual's mind. Unlike reasoning, thinking does not always have to be systematic or goal-oriented; recalling a memory, daydreaming, or arriving at an intuitive judgment are also forms of thinking. Reasoning, on the other hand, is the structured and goal-directed form of thinking; in this sense, thinking constitutes the broader mental foundation within which reasoning also takes place.
* Linear Thinking
Linear thinking refers to an individual's capacity to process information in a step-by-step, sequential, and consecutive manner. In this mode of thinking, the mind operates on a single chain of logic progressing from point A to B, and from B to C; each step is the natural consequence of the preceding one, and there is no branching or search for parallel paths throughout the process.
[A] ──> [B] ──> [C] ──> [D] ──> [E]
A unidirectional, sequential logical chain. Each step is the natural consequence of the previous one.
* Non-Linear Thinking
Non-linear thinking refers to an individual’s ability to process information not along a single sequential chain, but by establishing multiple connections, possibilities, and relationships simultaneously. In this form of thinking, the mind does not follow a single path; instead, it works by jumping back and forth between different points, discovering unexpected connections, and evaluating multiple perspectives simultaneously. While linear thinking moves from A to B and then from B to C, non-linear thinking allows A to connect simultaneously to C, E, and even an entirely unconsidered point X. This mode of thinking is particularly evident in areas such as creative problem-solving, multivariate analysis, and understanding complex systems. What is crucial here is the individual’s ability to build meaningful bridges between concepts that appear distant from one another and to sustain the thought process in multiple directions simultaneously.

 A versatile, branched network of ideas. A single node can be connected to multiple nodes at the same time.
* Critical Thinking
Critical thinking is the ability to use one’s cognitive skills to question, analyze, and evaluate the information, claims, or situations one encounters, rather than accepting them at face value, and to reach a well-informed judgment as a result of this process. The key point here is not merely “what” one thinks, but “how” one thinks. Critical thinking is concerned with the quality of the mental process leading to a conclusion rather than the conclusion itself. The most important characteristic that distinguishes critical thinking from other mental processes is that the individual is also aware of their own thought process. In other words, it involves questioning not only external information but also one’s own inferences, biases, and logical errors.
* Abstract Thinking
Abstract thinking is an individual’s ability to think at the level of concepts, relationships, symbols, and ideas without relying on concrete objects, directly observable phenomena, or firsthand experience. The most important feature that distinguishes abstract thinking from other mental processes is its ability to operate independently of context. Concrete thinking is tied to a specific situation; when that situation changes or ceases to exist, the thinking process is disrupted. In abstract thinking, however, an individual can mentally consider a scenario that has never existed, an event that has not yet occurred, or a relationship that cannot be directly perceived as the object of thought. This characteristic makes abstract thinking one of the cornerstones of both creative and analytical processes.
* Analytical Thinking
Analytical thinking is the ability to break down a whole (such as a problem, a system, an argument, a situation, or bla bla) into its components, identify the relationships and structural patterns among these components, and use this fragmented understanding to arrive at a deeper and more accurate conclusion about the whole. The key point here is not merely the ability to break something down into its parts, but to understand how those parts relate to one another and to the whole.
* Convergent Thinking
Convergent thinking is the ability to systematically narrow down multiple pieces of information, clues, or possibilities to a single, most accurate solution. In this form of thinking, the mind starts from a scattered and multifaceted starting point, progresses within an increasingly narrow framework, and ultimately arrives at a single solution or decision. The key point here is not merely finding the correct answer, but reaching that answer by eliminating existing options according to a specific logical and structural criterion.

A structure that converges toward a single point from multiple starting points.
* Divergent Thinking
Divergent thinking is the ability to generate as many diverse and distinct ideas, possibilities, or solutions as possible, starting from a single point. In this form of thinking, the mind expands in every direction rather than narrowing down to a single correct answer; the focus is on creativity, not restriction. The key point here is not merely to generate a large number of ideas, but to produce ideas that are genuinely distinct from one another and capable of forming unexpected connections.

A structure that expands in all directions from a single starting point and multiplies by branching out.
* Holistic Thinking
Holistic thinking is the ability to understand any system, situation, or problem not merely by focusing on its components, but by simultaneously grasping the entirety of the relationships these components establish with one another and with the whole. In this form of thinking, the mind focuses on perceiving the network of interactions between the parts and the meaning this network produces as a whole, rather than examining the parts individually. The most important characteristic that distinguishes holistic thinking from other mental processes is the shift in focus from parts to relationships. Analytical thinking breaks a whole down into its parts and examines the structural characteristics of those parts; holistic thinking, on the other hand, operates in the exact opposite direction.
* Systems Thinking
Systems Thinking is, strangely enough, both very simple and the hardest concept to explain to those new to SCD. I’ll try to explain it by taking a different approach from my previous explanations. In fact, Systems Thinking, Game Theory, and Psychological Profiling—a truly fascinating concept that’s interconnected. That’s why I recommend you research what Systems Thinking is on your own (I suggest reading the book “Thinking in Systems” by Donella H. Meadows).
If I had to summarize systems thinking in a single sentence, it would say that to understand why something behaves a certain way, you shouldn’t look at just that one thing, but rather at how it relates to everything around it. It might resemble holistic thinking, but I’ll explain the difference.
As you may be familiar with from “Linear Thinking,” most people construct a logical sequence when thinking about a system: A happened, which led to B, which in turn led to C. This is true but insufficient, because in practice, a system typically operates in a loop: A affects B; B affects C; but after a while, C loops back and affects A as well. In other words, the chain is not a straight line but a closed loop. Systems thinking is about being able to see this loop.
To illustrate this, let’s consider a simple example: The crime rate in a country rises. The government deploys more police. The crime rate falls. The police budget is reduced. The crime rate begins to rise again. This is a cycle meaning the starting point, having moved along the chain, eventually reproduces itself.

For a more detailed explanation, you can read the book I recommended; Figures 31 and 42 in that book provide a more detailed explanation.
Now I’ll give a very basic example using this diagram:
In the center, there is a Stock (Population).
On the left side, there is a Reinforcing Loop (R – Births) entering the system. As the population increases, births increase; as births increase, the population increases (a growth trend).
On the right side, there is a Balancing Loop (B - Deaths) exiting the system. As the population increases, the number of deaths increases, which pulls the population down (Balancing Trend).
Another key aspect of systems thinking is that the effects of an action are not always immediately apparent. The effects of a medication on the body may not become evident for days; the consequences of an economic policy may not be visible for years. This delay can lead someone who misreads the system to intervene either too early or too late, thereby unwittingly making the system even more unstable.
As previously noted, holistic thinking refers to a general mental attitude that prioritizes the whole, relationships, and context when approaching any situation. Systems thinking, on the other hand, is the structured and instrumentalized form of this mindset: it is a methodological capacity that models a system using specific conceptual tools—such as feedback loops, delay effects, and emergent patterns—and derives predictions from this model. In this sense, holistic thinking lays the mental foundation for systems thinking; systems thinking, in turn, is the structure built upon this foundation.
* Associative Thinking
Associative thinking is the ability to establish connections between concepts, images, or ideas that appear to be distant, unrelated, or belong to different fields. Rather than following a rigid logical chain or a structural framework, the mind establishes direct connections from one concept to another.

Reasoning
Reasoning is a mental process that proceeds systematically and purposefully toward a specific goal based on available information, observations, or premises. As noted earlier, thinking does not always have to be systematic or goal-oriented; recalling a memory, reaching an intuitive judgment, or the mind wandering on its own are also within the scope of thinking. Reasoning, however, is a form of thinking that takes on a specific structure and direction, moves from premises to conclusions, and in which every step can be justified.
* Deductive Reasoning
Deductive reasoning is the process of reaching a specific conclusion through logical necessity, starting from a general premise or an accepted rule; it means that when the premises are accepted as true, it is logically impossible for the conclusion to be false. For deductive reasoning to be considered valid, the premises do not actually need to be true or sound (I am not talking about the quality or validity of the feat, but the validity of the categorization); it is solely sufficient that the logical structure leading from the premises to the conclusion is formally constructed correctly. The soundness of the premise itself is a separate question and falls outside the scope of deduction.
Here, to provide an example of deduction, I will not give the classic Socrates example; they usually show that as an example, but I couldn't understand it for a long time, so I will try to explain this by giving a few different examples. First, to help you understand the deductive format I mentioned, I will give an unsound example.

An example that meets the format but has an unsound premise:
P1: You stepped out of the house and saw that the ground was wet.
P2: For the ground to be wet, it must have rained.
C: Therefore, it rained.

This inference is in a deductive format: the moment you accept P2 as true, you necessarily arrive at C. However, P2 is not sound. Someone might have watered the ground, a water pipe might have burst, or dew might have fallen. Still, this does not invalidate the deduction itself; it merely makes the truth of the premise debatable.

An example that meets the format but has a debatable premise:
P1: X is a strategic character.
P2: Strategic characters set traps at all critical points on the route in advance.
C: Therefore, X has set traps at all critical points.

This inference is also in a deductive format. The moment you accept P2, C is inevitable. However, P2 is debatable. Factors such as the complexity of human nature, resource constraints, or strategic flexibility weaken the universal validity of P2. Nevertheless, if the premises were true, the conclusion would necessarily be true; this shows that the formal structure of the inference meets the criteria for deduction.

An example that meets the format and has a sound premise:
P1: The only light source in this room is a single lamp.
P2: When the lamp is turned off, no other light source remains in the room.
C: Therefore, when the lamp is turned off, the room plunges into darkness.

In this example, both the formal structure and the premises are sound. P1 and P2 can be verified by observation, no variables are left out, and there is no need to go beyond the premises to reach C. If the premises are true, it is logically impossible for the conclusion to be false.
* Inductive Reasoning
Inductive reasoning is the process by which an individual reaches a general conclusion or rule based on specific observations, examples, or repeating patterns. The more observations accumulate, the stronger the conclusion becomes; however, it never gains deductive necessity.
* Abductive Reasoning
Abductive reasoning is the process of reaching the conclusion that best explains the incomplete or partial observations you have. Deduction derives a necessary conclusion from premises; induction generates a general rule from repeated observations; abduction, however, differs from these in that it seeks to identify the most plausible cause or hypothesis that could explain the existing observations. For this reason, abductive reasoning is often defined as “inference to the best explanation.”
* Let’s compare all three in detail
The key difference between abductive and inductive reasoning is that inductive reasoning gives you a general rule or principle, while abductive reasoning gives you the most plausible explanation for a situation.
Inductive reasoning example:
P1: It rained.
P2: The grass got wet,
C: So the rain wets the grass, the rain falls.
Here, you observe a relationship between rain and wet grass and derive a general rule from it. That’s why this is inductive reasoning.
Abductive reasoning example:
P1: The grass is wet.
C: Then it probably rained.
Here, you don’t have certain evidence. Instead, you form a hypothesis that best explains the fact that the grass is wet. This hypothesis is reasonable, but not certain, because the grass could have also been watered by a gardener. In abductive reasoning, the conclusion is always probable, not guaranteed.
Deductive reasoning example:
P1: The grass is wet.
P2: The grass only gets wet when it rains.
C: Therefore, it rained.
This is a deductive reasoning because you’re explaining the wet grass using a general rule (the assumption that grass only gets wet when it rains). However, if the premise is weak or false, the conclusion will also fail, even if the logic is valid.

Sensory
Sensory is the overall capacity of an individual to receive and perceive environmental data from the physical world through their senses and to convert this data into raw input for cognitive analysis.
* Observation
Observation is the act of consciously, intently, and systematically scanning, examining, and gathering visual information about one’s surroundings. This process requires active mental effort. When a character enters a space, identifying exits and cameras, noticing micro-details on the person across from them (such as a stain on their clothing, a bulge caused by a hidden object, or asymmetry in their posture), or intentionally searching for physical anomalies in an area all fall under the scope of observation. In short, observation is a structured measure of attention focused on what is where and on physical details.
* Perception
Perception is the sharpness with which an individual instantly senses and detects stimuli in their environment such as sound, smell, vibration, changes in temperature, or spatial movements without direct focus or deliberate seeking. For example, hearing the silent footsteps of someone approaching from behind, detecting a very faint chemical odor in their food, or sensing a change in the airflow in the environment.

Analysis
Analysis is the ability to break down a complex system, situation, argument, or dataset into its logical components in order to better understand it, grasp its fundamental workings, and unravel its dynamics. This category measures the ability to systematically examine not only the surface-level outcomes but also the structural mechanisms that produce them, the cause-and-effect relationships, and the interactions among components.

* Deconstruction
Deconstruction is the ability to break down a complex system or action into its constituent parts in order to understand it. It refers to the ability to identify and isolate the structural elements, underlying assumptions, and fundamental logic that underpin a given structure, rather than evaluating it solely as a whole.
* Tracing
Tracing is the ability to work backward from a final result, a clue, or a current situation to identify the starting point, root cause, or steps in the sequence of events that led to that result. It is a process that reveals a source, chronology, or sequence of actions that have been attempted to be concealed by establishing an unbroken chain of causality regarding the history of an apparent trace or action.
* Probing
Probing is the act of collecting data by applying actively structured stimuli (pressure, manipulative actions, fake vulnerabilities, or tests) to a system, individual, or situation in order to uncover its hidden rules, limitations, and immediate reactions.
* Mapping
Mapping is the ability to mentally organize the connections and relational nodes between scattered or complex data, individuals, events, or environmental factors. It refers to the ability to create a mental map of the whole situation or system by identifying the networks of interaction and the directions of influence between its components.
* Classification
Classification is the process of dividing large amounts of unstructured data, characters, observations, or phenomena into logical groups or categories based on their common characteristics, behavioral patterns, and underlying rules.

Insight
Insight is the capacity to grasp the essence of a system or problem in a given situation directly and deeply, going beyond surface data and step-by-step logical chains. While analysis involves breaking down a structure into its parts and examining its mechanics, insight is the ability to use this acquired information, past experiences, and mental models to solve the problem.
* Calibration
Calibration is the process of adapting one's own predictions, mental patterns, or physical/mental actions based on immediate feedback received. It is the ability of a character to recognize the strength of their opponent, the severity of the situation, or the margin of error in their own actions, and to adjust their next move based on this newly updated judgment.

* Integration
Integration is your ability to effectively incorporate new knowledge and completely unrelated skills into your existing system and actions. It can also be defined as transforming elements that appear scattered or unrelated into a single, synchronized action that supports one another.
* Optimization
Optimization is your ability to develop actions or judgments that will use the resources at your disposal (not only tangible ones, but also abstract ones such as cognitive costs or emotional burdens) with the least waste and maximum efficiency. The focus is not merely on solving the problem but on pruning the process to eliminate unnecessary steps, achieving the highest impact with the fewest moves, and utilizing the system’s boundaries in the most efficient manner.
* Expertise
is the deep, specific, and technical knowledge you acquire through intensive practice and experience in a particular field (such as psychology, computer science, etc.). This allows you to identify nuances and exceptions that you would not normally notice, thanks to your “crystallized” enables you to identify nuances and exceptions you might otherwise overlook and ensures their immediate processing within advanced rule sets.
* Heuristics
Heuristics is the ability of an individual to make quick and accurate decisions by bypassing lengthy and tedious analytical processes in situations characterized by high uncertainty or time constraints, relying instead on practical rules derived from past experience and pattern recognition skills.
Foresight
Foresight is the ability to mentally understand future scenarios and potential outcomes by utilizing existing information, patterns, and an understanding of how the system operates.

* Anticipation
Anticipation involves reading the dynamics of the current situation, the patterns of the opposing person or system, and the likely course of events, in order to position oneself in advance for a development that has not yet occurred.
To put this into concrete terms: a character who senses that an opponent is about to make a specific move and shifts into a defensive position before that move is made is demonstrating anticipation. The key point here is that the response is formed before the event occurs, not after.

* Prediction
Prediction is the process of generating a concrete outcome about a particular situation or system by analyzing available data, observations, and patterns. Contrary to popular belief, prediction doesn't have to be solely future-oriented. It includes predicting how an event that hasn't yet occurred will turn out, and predicting exactly what happened in the past based on available evidence. What distinguishes prediction from other inference processes is that the output is specific and testable; it's not a vague feeling like "something might go wrong," but a concrete prediction like "person X will do Z under condition Y."
* Let’s clarify the difference
A character notices that their opponent always makes the same move under certain conditions. They mentally process this and conclude, "If these conditions occur, they will make that move" (Prediction). Then, they position themselves accordingly (Anticipation)
Anticipation doesn't necessarily have to involve prediction; in other words, if you intuitively realize there's going to be a trap and take precautions against it, that's anticipation.
Psychology
Psychology is the scientific discipline that studies human and animal behavior and mental processes. It encompasses a wide range of topics, including thought, emotion, perception, motivation, personality, behavior, and interpersonal relationships. It aims to understand, explain, and predict how the mind works at both the individual and societal levels.
* Theory Of Mind
Theory of Mind is the capacity to understand others’ mental states such as beliefs, intentions, desires, knowledge, and perspectives. As distinct from one’s own mental states. In other words, a person can mentally model how another person perceives the world, what they know and do not know, and what motivates their actions. Thanks to this capacity, others’ behaviors are not merely observed; the underlying mental processes are also understood and predicted. Today, due to its broad scope, it is also used in place of EI
* Psychoanalysis
Psychoanalysis is a theoretical and clinical framework that seeks to explain human behavior and mental processes through unconscious processes, repressed content, defense mechanisms, and early life experiences.
