The claim about writing and storytelling is one of the most disingenuous and dishonest moves people make when they want to downplay, downscale, or debook someone, using it as a desperate attempt after they've been proven wrong multiple times.

There's one common pattern among people who make these claims: they want the quick thrill of a gotcha moment, even when their argument is wrong by their own standards and those of others.

"I got you! 😈 Hahaha!" (BTW, someone actually commented something like that in the sub.)

I find this claim particularly disingenuous because it's a poor attempt to shame the work, the author, and the reader while displaying little to no understanding of what they're talking about. They have no idea or clue about what those stories actually provide to the reader, especially when THIS IS SCD.

**We literally have such good standards that we filter out poor material before considering it relevant.**

# 1.false writing grading

When people bring up writing, I've always felt like it's about shaming others and disregarding scaling by saying it's anything while ignoring that the writing of a story is subjective to readers (different people can have different reviews and ratings). They make it about writing quality, which is a real thing, but even then, they ignore the analysis of the text and many other things in favor of their argument.

Plus, people who bring up the writing of a series or character often target series that are known for their higher writing quality among other people. They do this, intentionally or unintentionally, either to show that X is superior or to get a reaction, despite knowing that writing is subjective to readers. A 10/10 for one person could be a 6/10 for someone else, even if the writing quality is good.

But when you point out that a piece of media is superior despite its writing quality, they would gladly tell you that it's subjective and that other things are subjective experiences as well.

They resort to using buzzwords when they can't rate or grade something fairly, but even that is a poor, desperate attempt.

Most buzzwords don't apply, and even their complaints about a series belonging to XYZ genre don't always apply when you look inside the series. Sometimes, it's not really XYZ genre, or the genre-based complaint doesn't apply at all. I've personally noticed that genre-based complaints fall flat when you look at what actually happens inside the story and compare it with other media in the same genre.

***It's almost as if SCD has some great filters and such good standards that we can filter out poor material before considering it relevant.***

# 2. SCD SLOP

This is ironic. Wits are themselves part of writing. What, when, and how something is executed, and its effects on others, tell us about a character's methodology, modus operandi, characterization, and personality.

Puzzles, brainteasers, and riddles come under writing too; they're a different dimension of it. Writing logic and principles isn't an easy feat because it involves many things, such as planning, execution, conclusions, climaxes, etc.

I've seen people saying Liar Game and Akiyama have no writing aside from games and mind games. I disagree. I'm not the biggest defender of Liar Game, and it's not my favorite, but saying Liar Game and Akiyama don't have that type of writing is a bit of a cop-out. You can absolutely analyze Liar Game and Akiyama through their games and mind games and come to conclusions about their overall character writing. Being a simple and plain character ≠ bad character writing. Characters don't exist entirely in isolation.

Another case is 18LOH, where people claim it's bad and written only for SCD purposes. I find this conflicting because the second layer clearly has writing, such as themes and character portrayals through wits and smarts.

18LOH is wonderfully written in the sense that it incorporates rising stakes and utilizes its concepts naturally for the characters and readers, allowing readers to follow and get a glimpse of their intuition while going through the various brainteasers. It incorporates SCD elements while keeping tension, stakes, and conflicts.

People who make these criticisms fall into two categories: first, those who don't grasp what's in the text, so they think it's all bluff; and second, those who don't want to acknowledge what good methodology and chains of logic look like.

It's true that the early arcs of these series are really bland and plain and make necessary logical leaps, but I've found that authors always improve their craft and caliber. However, I've seen people not talk about this, possibly due to their biases.

Another point is when they say, "It gets better at XYZ chapter, but it doesn't." This falls short again when it's literally about a different dimension of writing, such as puzzles. To know whether the puzzles and riddles are much better at chapter X, you need to examine their construction, execution, and logical complexity.

# 3.TRUE scd elements claim

Some people genuinely pretend like they've discovered an objective way to scale SCD. And would you know it? Their supposedly objective standards somehow perfectly conform to what they enjoy.

This diminishes how the plot, character interactions and dynamics, buildup and payoffs, storylines, and character-arc development work, all just to demean a character.

"🤓😈 X faced dumb characters, or everyone around X was dumbed down to make him look smart."

This isn't necessarily true. Most stories literally tell and show us the backgrounds of their characters, including the supporting cast and main cast, and establish how they compare to the main POV character (the SC). These details help establish why the SC is smart instead of making their intelligence entirely dependent on everyone else being dumb.

And you know what's funny? The people who make these claims often have their own preferred series with the same supposed problem, but they won't apply the same criticism to them. They'll come up with excuses about how their preferred series explains everything, yet refuse to acknowledge the explanations in the series they're criticizing.

I've seen this claim used against COTE and *Monster*. Funny enough, both series literally tell and show us how intelligent the other characters are compared to the main focus.

**Johan and the "Everyone Is Dumbed Down" Claim**

The claim that Johan makes everyone around him dumb is an oversimplification.

Johan's identity was never something everyone in the storyline could easily understand or even properly identify. People underestimate his influence and how far his reach extends, including his ability to manipulate people and influence criminal organizations. The series repeatedly shows how far Johan's influence reaches. His past and the mystery surrounding him help explain why he's such a threat.

You can criticize specific moments or question particular feats, obviously. But simply claiming that everyone is dumbed down around Johan ignores the context the series provides.

**ANHS and the "Dumb Students" Claim**

The claim that ANHS has dumb students or that Class A isn't elite is similarly questionable.

Literally, Volume 1 establishes that ANHS has its own criteria for selecting and developing elite students, with the goal of creating ideal citizens.

The different classes have their own strengths and weaknesses. Class A has internal divisions, Class D struggles with cooperation and coordination, Class C is ruled by a tyrannical leader, and Class B appears comparatively better because of its softer leadership.

Having higher points or a better class ranking doesn't automatically mean that every student in that class is superior in every relevant respect.

There are different problems and advantages across the classes, even when the surface-level results suggest otherwise.

Ayanokoji's past and the clues surrounding his background also help explain why he's so difficult to counter.

Again, you can criticize specific feats or argue that particular moments are poorly executed. But you need to engage with the actual information the series provides instead of dismissing everything.

The Problem With the "TRUE SCD" Standard,These claims also ignore the different tropes and types of smart characters. There are different kinds of intelligent characters, and they can function differently depending on what the story requires.Dismissing a character because they don't fit your preferred archetype is just removing them from consideration because you don't like what they represent.

They want authors to cater to their preferred types of characters instead of evaluating what the story actually establishes and how those characters function within it.

When they're proven wrong, they suddenly switch to:

*"Oh, X is better according to XYZ scaling system."*

Okay? Being good under one scaling system doesn't automatically mean you're better at outsmarting someone in every context.

A scaling system's goal is to evaluate characters according to its criteria, not magically determine every possible matchup or prove that one character is universally superior.

Then there's the appeal to authority:

*"This person agrees with me!!!!!!"*

And it turns out that the person they're appealing to actually scales that character pretty well but considers the character unreliable or unsuitable for fair matchups.

So what exactly did you prove?

*"Damn, he got me!"*

# 4. "SMART PLOT & Realism"

This criticism is even more questionable. When these people talk about a "smart plot," they often fail to consider the intricacies of the storyline, the plot itself, and how effectively the author executes it within the context of the story.

Foreshadowing, clues and hints, small details, ongoing plotlines, character drama, and interactions are often completely ignored because, according to these critics, the story supposedly isn't smart enough.

Another common claim is that a character doesn't face enough difficulty or adversity. But how exactly are schemes and displays of wit supposed to be evaluated? SCD is fundamentally about solving problems, overcoming obstacles, and demonstrating intelligence through the execution of plans and strategies. It's not as though difficulty and adversity are automatically absent from a story simply because the main character doesn't face the particular level of opposition these critics expect.

This kind of criticism can invalidate practically anything, including schemes and feats, by disregarding the planning and execution involved. A character's planning may demonstrate that they accounted for multiple variables, anticipated potential outcomes, and avoided mistakes that could sabotage their own plans. These aspects shouldn't be ignored simply because someone believes the character didn't face enough difficulty.

That's why I dislike statements such as, "Oh, this character didn't face any difficulty." Even if I acknowledge that the opposition might not have been particularly challenging, if the schemes and feats demonstrate competent planning and execution, why shouldn't they be considered?

I'm not arguing that opposition and intellectual adversity should be ignored. However, I've noticed that people who make a big deal out of these factors sometimes refuse to acknowledge similar flaws in the opposition and intellectual adversity presented in their own preferred series. They completely disregard contextual and situational adversity to portray a character's feats as inferior.

Bringing up realism is another strange approach. Most anime and manga aren't necessarily realistic when compared with real life or even certain television dramas. They're fictional works, so why should realism automatically determine whether their intelligence feats count?

The primary concerns should be the story itself, the context of the situation, and whether the reasoning and execution are adequately supported by the material. Real-world empirical evidence can be relevant when evaluating the plausibility of a particular strategy or situation, but that doesn't mean every fictional work must conform to strict realism.

People often bring up realism to devalue a character's feats, even though the same criticism could be applied to many of the shows and series they enjoy. Yet when their own preferred media faces the same criticism, they suddenly introduce qualifications such as "stylized realism" to defend their argument.

I understand the importance of groundedness and believable characterization. However, these qualities aren't universal requirements for every fictional story. Most fiction portrays characters who are above average human in aspects, at the very least. Groundedness can serve particular themes, motifs, and other storytelling purposes, but its absence doesn't automatically invalidate a character's intelligence.

You can bet that some of these people would conveniently ignore moments of realism and groundedness in the very media they criticize.

"i*t's stylized realism bro*"

# 5. featless and no theories & filling gaps

This criticism often amounts to saying, "I want everything spoon-fed to me with a perfectly explicit chain of logic."

Apparently, reading between the lines, understanding subtext and metatext, "show not tell "and considering context are things that don't exist.Authors can communicate information through explicit explanations, implications, foreshadowing, symbolism, and details that readers must interpret. Not everything needs to be explained directly.There's a difference between an unsupported assumption and a reasonable interpretation supported by the text.

"*Character can't be smarter than his creator, that's a hard cap. play you like fiddle 😈" \~ someone really said like that btw*

No, that's not how it works.

Authors specialize in planning, structuring, and executing the events that make their characters appear intelligent. Writing a convincing intelligent character requires the author to manage information, construct logical sequences, connect pieces of evidence, coordinate plot developments, and check for inconsistencies.

The author doesn't need to personally demonstrate every skill possessed by the character in real life. They need to construct a convincing portrayal of those skills within the story.

The claim that a character cannot be smarter than their author is often used as a shaming tactic to discredit the character's intelligence.

However, authors can deliberately write characters who possess specialized knowledge, reasoning abilities, or strategic capabilities that the authors themselves don't personally possess to the same degree.

The author can research a subject, develop a methodology, construct a complex scenario, and communicate the character's reasoning through the information provided in the text.

The author is the one who plans how events unfold, which information is available to the reader, what information remains hidden, and how the character's methods connect to the overall narrative.

In other words, the author's planning and execution are precisely what allow a fictional character to be portrayed as intelligent.

Authors use various storytelling techniques and devices to construct complex or interconnected plots. Yet some critics refuse to acknowledge that work because they're too focused on discrediting the author or the character.

If the available interpretations and evidence support a particular conclusion, that conclusion shouldn't automatically be dismissed just because it requires interpretation.

Of course, interpretations can be wrong. They should be evaluated according to the evidence, **context, consistency, and logical plausibility(hal rubik cube example )**. But if an interpretation is supported by the text, dismissing it merely because it isn't explicitly spelled out is not a sufficient counterargument. taking low ball interpretation also works

Theorycrafting and uncovering a character's true potential through evidence can be one of the best ways to understand a character. It's also genuinely fun because it can recontextualize existing material, shed new light on a character, and provide a deeper understanding of how a story functions according to its own principles. It also helps readers develop their tastes and appreciation for different kinds of storytelling.

I've seen people complain about these things, but their complaints often aren't really about the methods themselves. Instead, they seem to be about the people who use them. My reaction is always the same: why not ask questions, verify the claims, check the evidence yourself, or establish reasonable restrictions that can be consistently applied?

Nope. Instead, they just want to complain because a character they thought was merely smart turns out to be even smarter than they expected, and they can't accept it.

Of course, scaling and ranking can become complicated. However, scaling through a particular system is an evaluation, not an absolute measurement of a character's intelligence.

We cannot evaluate a character's wits while completely removing the story and its narrative from consideration. In some cases, we can rely on pure methodology, stop scaling, and logical scaling. In other cases, we cannot, and we have to consider theories and interpretations.

However, interpretations and theories shouldn't automatically be frowned upon in discussions and debates. They should be evaluated according to the evidence, context, consistency, and logical plausibility rather than dismissed simply because they require interpretation.

Furthermore, some verses and forms of media cannot be meaningfully equalized or stat-equalized because of the nature of their stories, their underlying mechanics, and the ways their characters' intelligence is portrayed

*A character can only be as intelligent as the author portrays them through the details, reasoning, and logical methodology provided in the story.*

# VERDICT

If I had to think of why people make these poor claims, I'd give two reasons:

1. Rules for thee, but not for me

The same people wouldn't apply these standards to their own favorite or even non-favorite media, yet they expect others to apply them to different series. They'll criticize one series for having narrative reliance, unrealistic schemes, weak opposition, or flawed methodology, but when their preferred series has similar issues, suddenly they have excuses and explanations for everything.

They'll use one standard to criticize a series they dislike and another to defend a series they enjoy. It's almost as if their supposedly objective standards conveniently align with their own preferences.

2. Scapegoating.

They use writing quality, realism, and other buzzwords as scapegoats to dismiss characters and their feats instead of properly engaging with the arguments and evidence presented.

Sometimes, it feels like they're more interested in discrediting the series and its characters than actually analyzing what's presented in the text. Often, it also feels like they're projecting their own biases onto the criticism so they can continue enjoying their favorite characters while dismissing other media for having the same flaws they overlook in their own preferred series.

**But one thing is common among these people: they insult readers and authors for enjoying a work they consider below average or mediocre despite the work being above average or decent , instead of acknowledging its strengths despite its flaws.**
