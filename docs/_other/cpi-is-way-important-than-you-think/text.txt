I think people severely underestimate CPI. Some even go so far as to say that CPI
does not have even a 1% effect in their own scaling. The exact purpose of writing this
text is to show why this is problematic.
But before discussing why this matters, we need to define this 3 concepts: CPI,
Judgement, and Computation.
• CPI (Cognitive Proficiency Index) - CPI is an index that provides an estimate of
how efficiently cognitive information is processed for the purposes of learning,
problem solving and higher-order reasoning. In the Wechsler scales, it is based
particularly on the Working Memory and Processing Speed components.
Pearson's description of the WISC-V directly defines CPI as “an estimate of the
efficiency with which cognitive information is processed in the service of
learning, problem solving, and higher-order reasoning.”
• Judgement - The capacity to determine which inference, decision, or action is
better by evaluating the available information, conditions, and possible
outcomes.
• Computation - The mental transformation and processing of information
according to certain rules and procedures in order to reach a conclusion.
After defining these three concepts, we can move on to the main issue:
What is the relationship between Computation and CPI? CPI is the measurable form
of Computation. No matter how correctly you make a decision, if you cannot hold that
decision in your mind, process the steps sequentially, and do so quickly enough, that
decision remains on paper. Working memory is your capacity to hold information in
your mind and manipulate it, while processing speed is how quickly and accurately
you can do this.
From this perspective, our judgements are completely dependent on Computation.
But here I need to underline what I mean by "judgement." for prevent misconceptions
as i defined i do not restrict Judgement, as others do, to a narrow ability set such as
decision-making. When making an reasoning, whether you call it inference or
reasoning it doesn't matter, judgement is present. When constructing a plan, when
weaving a strategy, meaning when scheming, judgement is also present. So what
truly separates these processes from another? Frankly, nothing. They are all simply

It has been shown that planning problems can be translated into axioms of
propositional logic and solved with a general-purpose logic solver. (Kautz, H. &
Selman, B. (1992). Planning as Satisfiability.) If a planning problem can be
transformed into an inference problem without losing anything from its meaning and
solved with a general method developed for inference, then planning does not require
a separate ability beyond making inferences. In other words, what makes planning
possible is already exists within what makes reasoning possible.
The conclusion that follows from this is that the difference between reasoning and
planning lies on the content to which the process is applied. In one, we work with
premises and conclusions, and in the other, with states and actions. However, in both
cases, what we are doing is finding a valid sequence of steps that reaches a goal from
a starting point within a given set of rules. Building a strategy does not fall outside
this definition either. Moreover, we can see this idea in game theory as well. With
backward induction, the best strategy in a game is found by starting from the possible
endings of the game and reasoning backward by logically deriving what the players
will do at each step. Therefore, treating these processes not as separate abilities but
as applications of a single ability set, namely judgement, to different domains is both
more consistent and requires fewer assumptions.
So what is required first in order to make a judgement? First, an observation, meaning
an input. Second, a system capable of taking this input and processing it, which in
SCD is most commonly a brain. This is exactly what Computation is. If you cannot take
the data in front of you and process it, you cannot reach any judgement from that
data. However, an important distinction must be made here. Our judgements depend
on information, and one of the biggest factors determining the quality of a judgement
is Computation. In contrast, the judgement ability itself, in terms of its nature, is not
dependent on Computation. Judgement only depends on a certain set of information.
Let me explain this with a simple example. Imagine two sets:

B = {x₁, x₂, x₃, …, x₆₀}.
Here, A is a subset of B, and the nature of the data in both sets is the same. In this
case, a judgement based on set B has a higher probability of being accurate, because
B not only contains everything that A contains, but also covers many more possible
scenarios on top of it. The same judgement ability, when applied to two different sets,
produces results of two different levels of quality.
This is why Computation is important. Set B is in front of you, but your mind can hold
and process only twenty pieces of data at the same time. In this case, even though the
remaining forty pieces of data in B are there, they are effectively nonexistent for you.
Your judgement effectively works on set A. In other words, Computation determines
which information judgement can access. No matter how strong your judgement
ability is, it can only work with as much data as Computation allows in. This is why
the nature of judgement is independent of Computation, but the quality of the result
that judgement can reach is directly dependent on Computation. Moreover, in systems
where the result can be logically derived, this is a necessity. In such a system, every
step leading to the correct conclusion is already present in the data. Higher
Computation means being able to follow more of these steps. Therefore, higher
Computation can never produce a worse result here.
