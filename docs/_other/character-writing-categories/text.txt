THEORY
INTRODUCTION
For a long time, I sought an objective way to evaluate the «writing» of a character using categories, but without success. This led me to develop a new system that deconstructs every aspect of a character and their connection to the world.
CHARACTER: CIRCLE WITH TRAIT POINTS
Foundation
A character begins with their behavior, worldview, and core identity. These elements must intertwine with the themes explored by both the character and the work as a whole. My model rests on two pillars:
Profiling — External and internal traits (personality, appearance, actions).
Themes — Philosophical, ideological, or emotional concepts the character embodies (e.g., sacrifice, subjectivism).
These elements are inseparable from the character, much like an axis cannot be removed from a wheel in a car.
Geometry of Character
Imagine the character as a circle dotted with points. Each point represents a chosen trait (e.g., «handsome») or theme (e.g., «loyalty»). Initially, these points are «empty» — they gain meaning only when filled with narrative content (exploration).
Example: Take an intellectual character with acne, short stature, and glasses. Their themes are «sacrifice» and «woe from wit». We plot these points on the circle, then populate them:
Why «sacrificial»? Because they risk their safety for friends.
How does «woe from wit» manifest? Their intellect isolates them from society.
DEPTH AND COMPLEXITY: CONNECTING LINES
Populating points
Each trait or theme requires narrative validation. For instance, a character described as both «handsome» and «unattractive» creates an oxymoron, explainable through subjective perceptions by other characters. Such contradictions add multidimensionality.
Connecting Points
Points are linked by narrative lines that illustrate how traits and themes interact. For example:
«Beauty ties to loyalty because the character sacrifices their appearance for duty».
These lines form patterns (straight, jagged, spiral), reflecting the character’s complexity. Crucially, these connections exist outside the story’s timeline — they are static, like a map analyzed post-reading.
Reverse Approach
Start by extracting traits from the text, marking them as points, then reconstructing their connections. This reveals how cohesively the author developed the character.
THE WORLD: A MULTITUDE
        Our characters do not exist in isolation — they are surrounded by numerous other characters, each with their own traits and connections. Thus, a multitude of circles forms along our narrative line!
METANARRATIVE: INFINITY BEYOND THE PLOT
This realm lies outside and includes:
Symbolism (e.g., the character’s glasses as a metaphor for blindness to others’ struggles).
Allusions (references to other works).
Parallels (to other works of art or related to the narrative itself)
Authorial messages not directly embedded in the plot.
The metanarrative connects to the main narrative via abstract lines. For instance, a character’s «subjectivism» might reflect the author’s philosophical stance on the nature of truth.

CATEGORIES
PROFILING
        1. Characterization
        Core traits: Appearance, mannerisms, key actions. This is the «skeleton» the reader notices first.
        Example: A piercing gaze, scars on the hands, a habit of twisting a ring when stressed.
        1.1. Concept
        The character’s central idea — their role in the story or a unique trait that makes them memorable.
        Example: «A warrior with a fractured psyche» (struggling between cruelty and compassion).
        2. Psychology
Logic of actions: How the character’s personality and past justify their decisions.
Example: Due to betrayal trauma, the hero avoids intimacy, even if it harms the plot.
Key: Actions must stem from the character’s beliefs, not plot convenience.

EXPLANATION
We are not considering the external conflict itself, but the characters involved in it, its validity (authenticity), and their attitude towards it, which is psychology; more precisely, the «validity of certain actions».

3. Themes
Ideas the character embodies or challenges.
Examples:
Sacrifice — The hero is willing to die for others.
Freedom vs Duty — Conflict between personal desires and obligations.
4. Metanarrative
Layers of meaning beyond the direct narrative.
4.1. Symbolism
4.1.1. Direct
Objects/actions with explicit meaning (e.g., time and broken clock in «1 rittoru no namida»).
        4.1.2. Indirect
        Hidden associations (e.g., the name «Cain» alludes to the biblical fratricide; the name Lev Myshkin embodies two sides of the same coin: «Lev» (lion) — strong, self-sufficient — and Myshkin (derived from «mysh», meaning mouse) — weak, meek. Similarly, the surname of Evgeny Bazarov alludes to his «boisterous» yet «hollow» nature).
        4.1.3. Reference
        Nods to other works, myths and authors (e.g., a Shakespearean quote in dialogue; «Cyrano de Bergerac» and «Faust» in «Subarashiki Hibi»; Philemon and Baucis from Ovid’s «Metamorphoses»; The Trojan cycle of myths (Helen of Troy); The myth of Pygmalion (parodied in Wagner’s creation of the Homunculus); Walpurgis Night (a fusion of biblical allusions, cross-cultural folklore, and ancient Furies) in «Faust»).
4.2. Parallels
4.2.1. Characters
Protagonist and antagonist as two sides of the same coin (e.g., both seek justice but through opposing methods; e.g., Takuji and Tomosane (creation and destruction); Mephistopheles and Faust).
4.2.2. Situations
        Virtually identical situations that symbolize differences in narrative, direct character development, or symbolic distinctions (for the first example — the first and last glances between Walter and Jesse from «Breaking Bad»; for the second — «The Strand» and «The Bed» chapters in Ulysses [identical symbolic aspects, fueled by the same narrative style]).
        4.2.3. Art
        Narrative echoes of other works (e.g., «Ulysses» mirroring «The Odyssey»).

DEPTH
5. Connections
How elements interact.
Example: The trait «cynicism» + theme «disillusionment with people» + symbol «locked doors in his house» = a cohesive portrait.
6. Exploration
Depth of trait and theme.
Example: The theme of «loneliness» isn’t just stated — the hero loses friends, stays silent in key moments, and lives in an empty home.

COMPLEXITY
7. Contradictions
Example: The hero preaches honesty but lies for a «greater good». This isn’t weak writing — it reflects inner conflict.
8. Integration
Weaving traits into the plot.
Example: The character’s cowardice isn’t just mentioned — it prevents them from saving a friend, triggering tragedy.

9. Development
Character evolution across the plot.
9.1. Direct
Example: The hero starts as a naive idealist but becomes a pragmatic realist after betrayal.
9.2. Indirect
Example: The character doesn’t outwardly change, but their worldview shifts through internal monologues.
9.3. Catalysts
Example: A turning point — the mentor’s death shatters their faith in the system.
9.4. Consistency
How well is the character «written» throughout his narrative development?
9.5. Peaks
Key moments in character’s narrative development.
9.5.1. Culmination
Highest character peak leading to catharsis on y-axis.
9.5.2. Quantity
9.5.3. Quality

EXPLANATION
«Narrative development» is an x-axis measure, our circle moves along this axis, and inside the circle, both direct and indirect development occur. «Narrative development» is not a category, but the narrative’s measure itself. Our y-axis is a measure of a character's catharsis.

EXPLANATION
        In my opinion, it should be used. In reality, the influence of a character on the narrative cannot be assessed in any way other than by introducing a new category. «Integration» in «complexity» does not fully address this, as it considers only specific characteristics of the character as a whole. The world and the hero are inseparable things; even if we try to describe only the character, there will still be «nothing», and therefore, the character’s influence on this «nothing» is incredibly important.
        Emotions are subjective, by which I mean that some will feel sadness in a certain situation, while others will feel indifferent to it, but this is art — it evokes different emotions in all of us. I don’t want to turn it into a «purely mathematical model», so I add this subjective aspect of our perception.
        The influence on all art as a whole is also an incredibly important aspect because it points to the quality and quantity of ideas revealed by the character.

PRACTICALITY
        Theory is simple, but practice is incredibly complex. This is because applying this system in action requires analyzing every sentence of a work and documenting each character’s traits along with their narrative representation (their exploration) to achieve a comprehensive evaluation.
        As already explained, we use contradictions, exploration, connection, and integration to describe the «how» that explains the «why». This method is called CECI (ConnectionExplorationContradictionIntegration).
        To use this method, we extract each element of every aspect from our narrative categories (except for depth and complexity, as they serve as methods) and describe them using CECI.
