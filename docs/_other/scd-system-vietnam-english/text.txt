* Leadership: building consensus and guiding a group to cooperate so as to turn a shared vision into reality, carry out the set strategy or plan, and thereby help the group achieve results.

Kain designed a very careful performance-evaluation mechanism to limit dissatisfaction within the group.

* Inductive Reasoning: (Inductive reasoning generalizes about a problem from a certain amount of observation. It is a scientific method that has been used extensively throughout history: forming hypotheses from small samples and thereby finding rules that apply to larger samples. It is extremely effective when used properly. However, if you use inaccurate or unrepresentative data, the conclusion may be flawed. The core of inductive reasoning is the ability to look at results, events, ideas and observations and draw a unified conclusion from them.)
Real-life example: if you notice that in the sequence of numbers 2, 4, 6, 8, you conclude, "This sequence is a sequence of even numbers and the next one will be 10." (The rule is to add 2.)
Or: after running many experiments and observing that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius, we can conclude that "All water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at standard pressure."

* Abductive Reasoning (or abduction): reasoning by assumption; a form of logic used to "guess" causes in order to explain an effect. In essence, abductive reasoning is similar to deductive reasoning. However, deduction is certain, whereas abduction is hypothetical and takes the most probable case.
Example: B is driving on the road. Ahead of B are three turns, all leading to B's workplace. On the left turn, B sees a long line of cars bumper to bumper, unable to move forward. On the road straight ahead, B hears sirens and sees an ambulance's warning signals half a kilometer away. B reasons that an accident has happened ahead. B listens to the news and learns that the turn on B's right is under road repair and temporarily closed to traffic. After weighing it up, B decides to go straight, since that is the best solution given all the available data. In this case, even though there is an accident, traffic flow is still better than on the left and right turns. This is abductive reasoning.
An extremely easy-to-understand illustration:
A mother gives her child 6,000đ to go down to the market to buy a lemon.
The lemon costs 4,000đ.
Vietnamese currency has no 6,000đ note, so there are many different possibilities.
1/ Mom gives: 5k + 1k, so the child pays 5k and the seller gives back 1k + the lemon => the child brings home 2k to Mom.
2/ Mom gives the child three 2k notes => the child hands the seller two notes = 4k for the lemon => the seller gives no change.
3/ If Mom gives the child one 2k note and four 1k notes …, then …
4/ Mom gives the child two 2k notes + two 1k notes … then …
"Mom gives: 5k + 1k, so the child pays 5k and the seller gives back 1k + the lemon => the child brings home 2k to Mom."
First, to reach the conclusion of which notes the child holds when they have 6k, there are shared premises and a conclusion for each case, as follows:
Case 1:
General premise (generalization):
Vietnam has 1k and 5k notes.
1k + 5k = 6k
The child has 6k (specific case)
Conclusion for the specific case:
The child holds a 1k note and a 5k note.
Case 2:
General premise (generalization):
Vietnam has a 2k note.
2k × 3 = 6k
The child has 6k (specific case)
Conclusion for the specific case:
The child holds three 2k notes.
=> This reasoning goes entirely from the general to the specific.

To see the complexity of a strategy, I will take 2 examples to compare.
Example 1: Goal: create a surprise birthday for B.
Strategy 1: A messages the whole group to come to A's house at 7 p.m. Then A invites B over to A's place to play games as usual. When A opens the door, everyone sets off fireworks.
This is simply "tricking B into coming over to play and then surprising them". Are there risks? Yes. What if someone in the group arrives late and runs into B at the door? Or B happens to see someone buying a cake? What if B gets suspicious and grills A: "Why did you suddenly invite me over today?"
Strategy 2: A considers the entire group of friends.
+) A knows that C in the group is someone who often lets things slip -> instead of making C keep the secret, A tells C that the party will be held tomorrow for some reason. Only at the last minute does A call C over -> B cannot extract information from C because C himself doesn't know.
+) A knows B is very suspicious, so A deliberately lets B see that A is booking a restaurant -> B smugly thinks, "Ah, they're planning a surprise birthday at the restaurant, I see through it" -> B mentally prepares to go to the restaurant. But B never expected that the restaurant is just a "fake trap". While B is on the way to the restaurant, A asks B to stop by A's house to pick something up -> the real surprise party is at A's house.
We can see that with the same goal, Strategy 2 is much more complex than Strategy 1: it has to account for multiple targets, B and C, and set additional fake traps that can mislead B -> creating the surprise (the main goal) while controlling the fewest variables.
Example 2: Robbers want to rob a bank and come up with 2 strategies:
1: The bank robbers cooperate with teammates to go in and rob the bank, retreat right when time is almost up for the police to arrive, and have a prepared route so that along the way they can swap vehicles, clothes, etc. Finally they escape with the money.
2: The robbers run a strategy that deceives the police into thinking they want to hijack a cash-transport vehicle and bomb the city. But in reality it is only a ruse to split the police's resources and delay the pursuit; everything else is as above.
Here, the complexity is shown in that the robbers set up a deception strategy to solve the problem that, if police resources are too large and they get news early, it will be hard to escape.
* Strategy Adaptation: the ability to recover a strategy.
Example:
A's bank-robbery plan was a long strategy in cooperation with B, but when the time to execute comes, B is arrested. A immediately carries out a series of tactics to shift blame or falsify clues so that the police are misled into thinking they arrested the wrong person, and thereby release B, and so the original strategy can continue.
* Strategy Efficiency: the efficiency of a strategy.
Example:
Facing the enemy's fortified fortress with supplies at less than one third of the quota, General A understands that this battle cannot be won with firepower. A forbids the whole army from launching any large-scale siege, and instead turns every single bullet into a deliberate tool for applying pressure. Artillery and soldiers fire only at reasonable moments on key areas along with some direct locations (meaning striking non-key places), so the enemy cannot determine whether the other side still has plenty of firepower; at the same time, A has scouts locate the positions of water sources and provisions while the enemy is being misled by the earlier firepower.
Through this handling, the enemy did not dare to attack and decided to hold on defensively, and A took the opportunity to request aid.
Once aid arrived, A ordered attacks on the positions that had been scouted beforehand.
Meanwhile the enemy soldiers saw that A's side, though firing constantly, still had a great deal left + they were losing food and water -> morale dropped -> defeat.
Here A used scarce resources sensibly to deceive the enemy; of course this is only an example and basic, so it is not at all advanced.
* Strategy Effectiveness: the effectiveness of a strategy.
Example:
Context: Trình Thực needs to save his teammates in the future but must make Bạch Phỉ willing to do it without suspecting his own identity and without suspecting that he is doing it deliberately.
Trình Thực poses as "Chân Dịch" to manipulate the entire situation. When Bạch Phỉ is curious about the true prophecy, he uses time pressure and her curiosity to force her to cooperate before he will reveal it. He asks Bạch Phỉ to plant a bomb in the main laboratory, giving as the reason that he wants to create a "destructive fireworks show" as a joke that will last hundreds of years. At the same time he deliberately stirs up Bạch Phỉ's ambition by hinting that the God may take notice if she destroys a stretch of history.
Trình Thực then, through a series of tricks, makes Bạch Phỉ bitterly resentful; by the time the request is carried out, Bạch Phỉ not only wants to wipe out that stretch of history as an offering to the God but also to ruin Chân Dịch's "fun". Out of resentment at being deceived and teased, she decides to plant bombs throughout the entire laboratory area, leaving out only the main lab in order to bankrupt his plan.
All of these reactions were within Trình Thực's calculations from the start. He deliberately planted in Bạch Phỉ a craving to be noticed by the God, then acted as though he only wanted to play around, to provoke her into resisting by quietly modifying the plan. Trình Thực pretended not to know that Bạch Phỉ had changed the bomb location, and left.
As a result the entire laboratory area exploded except for the main room, and that allowed the group PTT, BL, TTT, QN (Trình Thực's future teammates) to survive. But saving people was only a secondary goal. Trình Thực's real goal was to have as many people die as possible to repay his debt to the God of Death. He even deliberately used the belongings of the believer "Yên Diệt" as sacrifices to ensure the God of Death would be interested in "collecting the bodies".
The entire strategy ultimately let Trình Thực:
* Repay his debt to the God of Death.
* Deceive everyone with the identity "Chân Dịch" so as to avoid grudges being held against himself.
* And at the same time save his teammates.

* Planning flexibility: flexibility in a plan.
Example:
When a robber wants to rob a bank to get rich, he chooses to draw up a plan to rob Bank A.
The plan sets out steps from: finding teammates, planting inside men in the bank (guard, janitor, …), finding security loopholes by observation, finding the loosest time, checking the priorities of the police in the area, buying specially made tools, having the inside men monitor and report so the remaining members can act, the whole group entering the bank, causing large-scale chaos, the inside men helping subdue bank employees, holding hostages and grabbing the money, fleeing and avoiding areas with police, …
This plan is completely unstable: can an inside man actually be planted, does a security loophole really exist, can it be carried out before the police arrive, can a specially made safe-cracking tool be built, …
So when the robber carried out the plan, he flexibly adjusted it:
* + have more than one person apply for or take the job; the two pretend to compete with each other so as not to be suspected of being on the same side, pressuring the old janitor. This step will take a lot of time so it must be done carefully.
* + create a security loophole himself, such as the inside man habitually inviting other custodial staff to lunch => at noon there will be fewer eyes and ears in the bank.
* + create a minor car collision before the robbery is finished, staging a scuffle fairly close to a police station but in the opposite direction from the bank, slowing the police's progress to the bank robbery.
* + the perfect moment arrives before there are enough lock-breaking tools; however, considering that the supplies had reached 75%, he risks going early, and the rest will be replaced with ordinary, less effective equipment.
* + when cracking the safe, it turns out they completely lack the ability to crack it. Instead of giving up or being stubborn, he draws up a substitute plan: swap clothes with bank employees, leave just 1 accomplice to guard the hostages, and the rest, dressed in bank uniforms, move out as a group with the half of the hostages who were released first. These members quickly get into a vehicle and head to Bank B, which has weaker security, without anyone noticing. After successfully robbing Bank B, the accomplice left behind at Bank A also slips out the back door into a vehicle and flees; the police rush in and wrongly interrogate bank employees who are wearing the robbers' clothes.
=> The plan had to be flexible many times and continuously. Although it did not achieve success as per the original goal, it still achieved the purpose, which was the money.
=> Flexibility in a plan can mean creating backup options and being able to change the priorities in the plan if important factors arrive earlier than secondary ones, so as to advance the plan without delay. When information is inaccurate, one can adjust and correct based on the new factors, without stubbornly insisting on following only the original plan.

Logical Scaling
A primer on the type of scaling used in Vietnam
Unlike the West, which uses normal scaling, Vietnam uses logical scaling, which usually does not use narrative/statement/off-screen feats, and only uses realistic, logical and onscreen ones.
1. Not accepted:
Statement/Narrative: the "label" the author gives a character, valid only within that character's own universe. Narrative/Statement is only strong within its own universe. For example, character A in series B is asserted to be "the smartest person in the world"; however, in world C of character D there is no one named A bearing such a title, so how can we recognize it when the two have nothing to do with each other? On the other hand, even if the story does acknowledge it, under Vietnam's way of scaling we need the feat to be demonstrated, and if it is merely acknowledged and then never shown => featless. An easy example is the arguments over whether L or Kudo is smarter: instead of focusing on comparing feats, the argument turns into a contest over whose story gives the bigger title, and then L > Kudo because the world's best detective must be better than the Detective of the East; meanwhile, anyone who participates in debates knows how far apart L at tier C and Kudo at tier A are.
Off-screen feats: achievements that do not show the thinking process. Off-screen feats are things a character achieves without any depiction of the thought process, like "I solved 100 cases in 5 seconds". This is called off-screen because the author never describes what the character solved or how, and if the character does not demonstrate it there is almost no information to grade; most off-screen feats work only as narrative and statement, for the simple reason that without a demonstration there is no concrete information to grade.
Unrealistic: roughly, "not realistic": the character does fantastical things without any concrete logic. Whether something is unreal is determined by whether it actually exists in real life; examples are magic or hax. These things cannot be recognized as the individual's own thinking ability; for example, instead of having to read people to obtain information, having the character use hax to easily monitor that person's current mind is simply not fair to put on the scale for comparison. However, we must still distinguish unreal from extraordinary intellectual ability: if a character is omniscient, then normally a character who knows information that drops from the sky would be treated as hax, but if that character, right on the page, demonstrates the ability to analyze phenomena one by one in thorough detail => this is a very strong knowledge feat, and this feat will not be denied. For example, a robot that has a process of analyzing information and data — however fast it is, this process is still an undeniable feat.
2. Accepted:
Onscreen feats: those that show the character's thinking process. For example, if you are thinking => thinking; if you are reasoning based on information => reasoning; if you are calculating and analyzing problems and information => FSIQ. Each thing you display, if it is "clear" or can be proven "clear", will be recognized as an onscreen feat; at that point we classify the feats and then draw conclusions about the ability each part of the feat shows.
Realistic/Logical: In works of fiction, the realism of a strategy does not lie in copying real life completely, but is measured by its feasibility and internal plausibility within the established context, where the audience can clearly see the operating principle of each small step leading to the final result. Unlike reality, where every action is bound by strict logical laws and cannot be skipped, fantasy worlds tend to simplify processes to serve the pacing of the story. For example, for the same scene of hitting a bullseye from 300 meters, an ordinary work might idealize it in a single sentence, but a more outstanding work will actively weave in details about aiming technique, psychological pressure or environmental factors to increase authenticity. It is precisely this meticulousness in building the logical foundation and explaining the operating mechanism that determines whether the plot is solid and persuasive, while also showing the author's thinking and respect for the reader's intelligence.
* Logical is the degree of realism of a feat — that is, it is done completely, with clear, easy-to-understand logical explanation. The feat must be written coherently by the author in order to show the degree of logic it has. Realistic feats, if built on a real-world foundation, are easily rated highly; for example, reducing luck or character intuition to the minimum and avoiding building the feat on random chance makes it more effective in evaluation. Logical feats presented step by step will be rated higher than feats that simply jump from question to answer. A typical case is the 17-card poker game: same game and same trick, yet Akiyama in the manga is portrayed as stronger than Doyoung in the live action, because Aki in the story shows his thinking down to each card combination and how it operates, whereas Doyoung cannot do the same. So for logical feats, the deeper it is shown in the text, the stronger it is.
3. Additional definitions:
Debunk: pointing out an absurdity or impossibility in any given feat. Pointing out the absurdity can reduce or even nullify the value that the feat contributes to the character overall. However, we must know that debunking a feat does not mean nerfing that character: a debunk only nerfs when the debunked feat is a buff-cate feat for that character. An example here is L's feat of narrowing down where Kira is. This feat is built on the premise that Light has to watch TV, and whether Light watched TV and let his ego rise is something L had no way to guarantee => this feat is luck; because its premise is luck, this narrowing-down feat cannot count as a feat and is eliminated entirely; the fact that this feat is debunked does not mean we ignore all of L's later feats.
Antifeat: feats that overlap each other in value, strong at times and weak at times in each cate without staying stable. This is actually uncommon, and if encountered we still choose the strongest feat.
Theory: a hypothesis. Using theory in scaling is for obtaining feats that the author has somewhat hidden from the reader; these feats still have value, even stronger than the surface feats created for us to see easily. For a theory to be recognized as a feat, the scaler is responsible for proving that the theory can truly exist, and the evidence is usually hints, dialogue, strange elements, …
Resources: resources are the premise for everything in battles of wits. Resources include information, materials, authority, … It cannot be said that someone has no resources; if they truly had none, they simply could not take part in the game. After having resources, the thinking process is also shown through how they exploit and use those resources. There are 2 issues that newcomers very easily get wrong. The first is unreal verses such as Cổ Chân Nhân, Long Phù, etc.: here the resources and forms of hax are richer than in a real-life setup, so many people think the feats of these characters are unrealistic and unsuitable, but we must accept that, by the way they are constructed, this hax is merely an aid to the character's thinking ability, and denying the resources of unreal verses is no different from denying the resources of real-life verses — the most common one we run into being money hax. The second issue is confusing hax with the character's thinking ability: for example, it is not necessarily right to deny a feat just because the character has some hax assisting in gathering information; we also need to see whether the process still includes reasoning and selective filtering of information, and whether there is a process of piecing information together. The hax element can nerf it to some extent but does not always negate it entirely.
4. Notes:
* An unreal feat may be invalid, but the thinking used in that unreal feat can be considered if it is valid.
* Besides reviewing and comparing pure feats, we also evaluate the brainpower used to write the process of showing the feat; however, this factor is only used when the comparison is too evenly balanced and cannot be clearly decided.
* Onscreen but unreal cannot be used and is discarded. Realistic but offscreen is likewise discarded. Typical onscreen-but-unrealistic feats are Tề Hạ's 70-year plan and Phương Nguyên's 500 years of experience: in real life no one can make a long plan that is detailed and accurate to 100%, even with hax like Tề Hạ; even for Phương Nguyên's plans, Phương Nguyên himself has to keep spamming Spring-Autumn Cicada (Xuân Thu Thiền) to obtain information + revise the plan continuously, so we can clearly see how unrealistic it is. Even for characters like CEOs or presidents, long-term plans only reach a few years at most, and they cannot calculate for incidents with 100% certainty like Tề Hạ, let alone 70 years with absolute precision.
* For a feat in one cate or one subcate, only the strongest feat is chosen, not accumulated (except knowledge). For example, if feat A produces a stronger IQ feat than feats B, C, D, … then we only use feat A when assessing this area. However, if within IQ feat A is strongest for FRI and PSI, feat B is strongest for WMI and VSI, and feat C is strongest for VCI, then when assessing IQ we must take each of these strongest subcates separately for the overall comparison.
* Narrative/statement is not always useless. For example, some are recognized: in the musical chairs game the player is the cult leader, but when we get to the poker bid game we know Kei Kimura is the one behind directing the old cult leader to follow => the musical chairs feat counts as Kei's feat since she is the one who thought it up, not the cult leader; Beyond Birthday's case in the novel, where we follow the process of solving the case and finding the answer, yet the story confirms that L had solved it earlier, so we can bridge the feat to some extent. A feat by narrative/statement will appear when the author has written some feat earlier; in that case both only serve to confirm whether a character is able to do the feat that has already been written.

How Points Are Calculated and How Characters Are Ranked
1. Cate:
2.5 points
* STRATEGY
* PLANNING
* Tactics
1.5 points
* Thinking (strategic, critical, analytical, abstract)
* Manipulation
* DECEPTION (misdirection, fabrication, infor control)
* MIND READER
* REASONING
* INFORMATION GATHERING
* Overall EQ (EU, EP, EM, EE)
* FSIQ (PSI, WMI, FRI, VCI, VSI)
* Psychology (insight, profiling, warfare)
* Overall SQ (Social awareness, Persuasion, Negotiation, Leadership)
1 point
* FORESIGHT
* ADAPTABILITY
* CREATIVITY
* KNOWLEDGE
2. How points are calculated:
POINT DATA
* = : 0% of the points
* >= : 25% of the points
* Close −: 50% of the points
* Not clearly ahead (−): 75% of the points
Diff multiplied by the category coefficient:
* mid-high to high: ×1
* mid+: ×1.25
* low-mid: ×1.5
* low: ×1.75
* no-low or no: ×2
The maximum multiplier is ×2; however much stronger, it is still only ×2.
If the word "or" appears, take the average first and then multiply by the category's point coefficient.
EXAMPLE: Akiyama Shinichi vs Souichi Kimura (Hal)

* In strategy Hal wins by −, so we take 2.5 × 0.5 to get the points Hal receives.
* Similarly, in reasoning Aki wins by ×1.25, which means we take 1.5 × 1.25 to get the points Aki receives.
* In SQ, Aki gets (−) or ×1, which means we take the average and then multiply by the coefficient -> (0.75 + 1)/2 × 1.5.

Note:
Benchmarks for the gap in diff within one cate:
* 1 major tier (e.g. mid 3 versus mid 4) is ×1
* 2 major tiers is ×1.5
* 3 major tiers is ×2
3. Character types:
Detective type (Hệ Thám): characters with an extremely large degree of brainpower/tier in reasoning and information gathering, while categories such as strategy, planning, tactics, deception, and manipulation are extremely low.
Example: Decker has a Reasoning brainpower level of Creative Smart but is only at Smart overall because of his disadvantage in the categories above.
Strategist type (Hệ Chiến Lược Gia): characters with a degree of brainpower in strategy, planning, tactics, deception and manipulation, but extremely low in reasoning and information gathering.
Example: Viên Phương has Super Genius planning and strategy but is only EG (Extraordinary Genius) because of his disadvantage in reasoning and information gathering.
All-rounder type (Hệ Toàn Diện): characters whose brainpower levels are extremely even, or not too lopsided, such as Trần Nhiên and Akiyama.
*Neutral cates such as overall EQ, SQ, Psychology, Mind Reader, Adaptability, Foresight, Knowledge, FSIQ, and Thinking (although Thinking favors the Detective and All-rounder types a little more), and Creative will not be discussed.
Note:
We must also deduct for the character's type when scoring cate comparisons.
All-rounder type > Strategist type > Detective type
For each type, 1 point is deducted after the take is done, and it is deducted from the overall score.
For example, in the case above: if B is a Detective type and A is a Strategist type, and at first A leads B by 4 points, then 1 point is deducted, i.e. A leads B by only 3 points.
4. Conditions for moving up in rank/tier:
Characters who are 4 points apart move up one step.
Example: if char B is base tier C− and char A is 4 points ahead of B, then char A is tier C.
