I welcome you to the RFES scaling document.
What RFES evaluates
RFES scales a character’s intelligence by asking: How cognitively demanding would it be for a human author to conceive the feats that the character performs?

Perspective (Rule 0)
Given this is the metric, RFES uses the authorial perspective rather than the narrative one.
If you truly want to get the theoretical basis for this scaling, I’d suggest to read this doc: 
-

Author Perspective Explanation
Trait 0: what happens in the story was decided by the author himself.
Trait 1: the author has semi-omnipotence (they can make nearly everything happen in the story).
Trait 2: the author has full access to real-world knowledge (science, prior art, game theory etc.). This is an assumed trait.
Trait 3: one of the main limiting factors for the author is his intelligence, since it’s finite. His intelligence being limited is what makes the comparisons meaningful.
-
“Cognitively Demanding”
RFES acknowledges that effort (time/energy spent crafting a feat) can *aid* cognitive transmission, but it is merely taken into account indirectly: production time or resources are not scored themselves; they “matter” only insofar as they increase the cognitive density and coherence embedded in the feat.
This is because what matters is merely the output.
-
“Does RFES scale how smart the authors are ?”

The answer would be: in most cases no, and at best there are only indicative correlations.

It depends on the amount of effort the author chose to put *specifically* for the writing of the intelligence feats. And even then, it’s limited to the types of intelligence one can express through fictional works.

-
“Why is it not the case most of the time?”

1- usually, multiple people work behind projects.

2- We take into account individual characters, not the whole cast that the author created. Some characters could display different types of intelligence or have different levels of it.

3- we do not take into account personal achievements.

4- story structure is irrelevant, even though that would be a major indicator of intelligence.

5- The amount of time that an author had to work is irrelevant. Even though obviously that would create great discrepancies.

What is evaluated within this system?
1- Cognition/Intelligence evaluative concepts that can be hard to write coming from the authorial perspective.
2- Cognitive / Intelligence concepts that have to be “science based”. By “science based” I mean: a- there have to be studies about them, b- these concepts have to lack vague definitions, c- their causal relationships must be somewhat known (which parts of the brain are involved and so on).
3- Since not all scientific concepts are fully developed (e.g. planning), and since there are some practical measures we must take (given the fact we are scaling fictional characters and not real people), we will then adapt these concepts later.

Context explanation
Real world standards will count as context in every situation. The only case in which this standard can be contradicted, is when the author deliberately creates “new variables” within the story. In such a case, the created variables override the ones of the real world standards, and the evaluation will be based on them.

The 3 cases in which this happens are: characters with abilities (Nagito’s luck), unrealistic capabilities (Shiro’s calculator prowess) and behaviours (Junko’s personality).

These “new variables” will be approximated as context due to 2 reasons:
1- reasoning based upon them, would actually require pattern recognition: recognizing that creating a certain trait in a specific environment, would then lead to certain patterns being created that could be then exploited.
2- accusing this “pattern recognition” to be “artificial” and so less valid/invalid, doesn’t make sense, given this is the authorial perspective. Meaning everything else, including all the realistic variables, are *also* “artificial”.

A feat is unrealistic if, in order to work as presented, it must rely on not taking into account variables that should be accounted for using real-world probability accounting which would then result in the feat having low probability.
Context explanation: any explicit or, very strong, but implicit claims/structures within the story will count as context, and for anything else, real world context standards will be applied.

Example of the second: human bodies in Danganronpa are and work the same as with real human bodies (unless specifically stated otherwise).

“a feat is the more unrealistic the more the cause and effect relationship established by such a feat are related to ignoring variables that should be taken into account judging it by real-world probability using the in-universe context”.

B- EFFICIENCY. Technically speaking, LOW chances BY THEMSELVES would not be enough to determine whether a feat suffers from the point of being unrealistic or not (lack of critical thinking etc.), if other ideas with way higher success chances weren’t possible.
The thing is that *this* would be how it would work in real life, where people have to *adapt* in the purest form to unknown/semi-unknown environments.
In fiction, though, this is not a real thing since the Causality Environment is entirely decided by the author himself.
Meaning in the end, everything with *low* chances of happening must be inherently reduced in value (nerfed).

C- REPLICABILITY. Replicability applies only when the cognitive mechanism behind a feat is sufficiently abstract, general, and independent from the specific narrative conditions in which the author placed it. In such a case, the unrealism rule doesn’t apply.
If the feat works because of a method, not because of the scene, then the method is replicable.
Replicability does not apply when the feat relies on highly specific, ad-hoc.
The more a feat depends on its particular setup, the more the unrealism rule applies.
In short:
abstract cognitive patterns → replicable
situation-dependent outcomes → non-replicable

Working Memory Index (WMI)
To evaluate WMI, three things must be determined:
How much each piece of data “weights” on WMI

Which reasoning configuration we are talking about

Dependency

1)- Each single piece of data will be evaluated based on its relative "weight" — essentially, how hard it is to retain that data in working memory.
The weight will depend on how much Abstract Thinking and Metacognition that information requires to be thought.
2)- Reasoning Configurations:
Type A: Puzzles that require multiple data points to be combined in a single cognitive effort. The working memory must condense everything into a single move. The best case in this, would be if each piece alone would be meaningless and only connected together they start making sense as it would obviously require more Abstract Thinking and/or Metacognition.
Type B: Puzzles that depend on a sequence of steps to reach a conclusion, where each step is dependent on the previous one. Working memory only needs to account for coherence with prior steps, so the effort is much lower.
Type C: Puzzles that use as a basis Type B (basically as premises) onto which it builds upon a Puzzle Type A.

Evidently, model A is superior — but by how much?
To answer this and other questions, the final concept comes in: dependency.
Model B becomes more valuable the more deeply dependent the connections between steps are. Not every connection has the same weight, and this happens because some connections only increase the likelihood that something happened/will happen, and are only meant to enhance the intuitive perception of realism in the reasoning. They are certainly valid, but don’t carry as nearly as much weight on working memory as Type A puzzle pieces.

In both models A and B, active manipulation to reach a conclusion can occur. In that case, the feat enhances in terms of value within the Abstract Thinking and/or Metacognition, adding to the “weight” and making the feat more valuable.
In summary:
For evaluation of Type A:
 number of data points * average weight of each data point

For evaluation of Type B:
 number of data points * average weight of each data point * Average Dependency Coefficient
For evaluation of Type C:
[ number of data points * average weight of each data point ] (for the Type A part) * coefficient of difficulty since Type B baseline

(B Area) Common Grounds For Evaluation

-1 Relative values. We don't know the absolute maximum for each area of intelligence. Instead, we use the *best observed feat* in each area as the current maximum. *Analogy:* We don’t know how many bananas monkeys *could* get in theory—so we look at the one who got the most and use that as our benchmark.

-2 Proportionality principle. Every character’s score is calculated as a proportion of the current maximum. *Example:* If Monkey A gets 5 bananas and the max is 10, he scores 50%. But if later Monkey B gets 15, A’s score drops to 33%.
Why?
1- this ensures coherency and reduces bias
2- since measuring cognition is vague, the more relative distances you have to respect, the preciser you become in your judgement

-3 Points. To enact rules 1 and 2, we assign points to quantify the value of a character's feats.

-3 Quantity is a relevant factor (with limitations of course) in “EQ” and “Strategy” subcats. Reasoning for EQ being: 1- human emotions are displayed in plenty of ways, and in order to safely say you “mastered” them, only one proof will never be enough and 2- it’s a really crystal intel based category already and creativity lacks, so more displays necessarily mean better skill as long as they are original. Reasoning for Strategy being: everything that is not related to computational skills (Reasoning is the only computational based category so far) is incredibly fluid and necessarily requires environmental adaptability, which the more you show the more credible you are. Planning would technically need more feats too but for practical reasons (99% of characters have 1 feat at most in it that was seriously worked on) we will only take into account one feat.

-4 Irrelevance of diversity by itself. This is already implied if you fully understood the previous things, but I will explicit this: if you "no diff" someone in EVERY cat but one, that character can still win as long as in absolute values he is better. For example: char.1 is better in X,Z,Q things with a total of 3 points of margin, but char.2 is better in P by a margin of 4 points, and so he wins.

-5 Relevance. Avoiding the use of categories-subcategories rarely shown by characters (like Emotional Realization) unless it’s a REALLY core aspect of outsmarting (such as emotional manipulation). Also avoid redundancies when deciding which categories-subcategories to use unless there is evident need to highlight a certain aspect of that intellectual ability.

-6 Usage of nerfing categories. Nerfing categories are tools to implement rules when they are not to be applied in "dark" or "white" cases, which is the most usual case. Eg. Solidity.

POINT SYSTEM EXPLANATION
Difficulties are represented as percentages, calculated based on proportional calculations within singular feats.
Difficulties per percentages:
(0–30% No concept of difficulty) > (30–40% Negative difficulty) > (40–45% No difficulty)
(45–50% No–Low difficulty) > (50–55% Low difficulty) > (55–65% Low–Mid difficulty)
(65–78% Mid difficulty) > (78–83% Mid–High difficulty) > (83–92% High difficulty)
(92–96%Very High difficulty) > (96–100% Extreme difficulty)
We will use the “+” sign when we are close to the upper tiers by 33% and the “-” sign when we are close to the bottom tiers by 33%.
How this all practically works:
Light has 9 points in reasoning, 9 points are the maximum for reasoning as a category let's say, and Near has 6 points in it. Since 6:9=⅔=66%, this means that Light mid diffs (-) Near, since it's in the “mid diff tier” and is also in the bottom 33% of such tier.
Now let's say that Kouji is presented and he high diffs + Light in it. Let's say that Light is now 90% (within the “high diff +” range) of Kouji’s reasoning. This means that now Near's reasoning evaluation will be changed from 6 points, to 6*90%=5.4 points, since the relative maximum changed.

Advanced RFES: The Slot System
The Slot System puts in practice the rules from Area B, in particular the quantity-quality balance. It divides subcategories into slots, each representing a separate “feat space”. The slots will have the total amount of value of the subcategory split between them in ways that would ensure quantity-quality balance.
This means that the more slots there are, and the more the percentage is similar between the slots, the more quantity is needed. The fewer slots there are, and the more disproportionate the percentages of distributions are, the less quantity is needed.
This is how it works:
Slot System Example
Subcat: Strategy Complexity (let’s say Strategy Complexity is being valued at 10 points)
Number of feats required (which is the same as to ask “how many slots are there”): 4 slots
Distribution of percentages: medium-high quantity needed(30-30-30-10)%
Characters: PJ, RJ
PJ’s feats = 10/10 feat + 10/10 feat
> 10/10 * 30% * 10 (points of the subcat) + 10/10 * 30% * 10 = 6/10 total points obtained
RJ’s feats = 9/10 feat + 9/10 feat + 8/10
> 9/10 * 30% * 10 + 9/10 * 30% * 10 + 8/10 * 30% * 10 = 8.1/10
So this would mean that RJ mid diffs (+) PJ in Strategy complexity (6 / 8.1 = 74%)

Droplet System
In order to minimize the problem of the Slot System favouring quantity too much, while also taking into account quantity at the same time, I invented this auxiliary system.
The way it works is that each feat will have a percentage of “decay” of value onto the other slots. It will still count as a feat in the other slots though, so no accumulations are possible.
If the feat pertains to the first slot, it will have say 40% decay onto the second slot, 30% to the third, and 20% to the fourth.
So the way it would work is this:
Slot 1 = 30%, Slot 2 = 30%, Slot 3 = 30%, Slot 4 = 10%
Subcategory: Strategy Complexity (10 points)
Decay value (based from the starting position): S1 100% > S2 40% > S3 30% > S4 20%
We have a 10/10 feat which translates into this:
10/10 * 30% * 10 * 10/10 * 100% + 10/10 * 30% * 10 * 40% + 10/10 * 30% *10  * 30% + 10/10 * 10% * 10 * 20% = 5.3/10 in Strategy Complexity
I want to underline this: this mechanism makes it so that quantity is still relevant (we are not subtracting percentages from the other slots) while at the same time giving quality the value it needs.
Also another thing is that the decay value starts where the last feat is based. So for example if you have 2 feats, and both are 10/10, the second one will have a decay of 40% in the third slot and 30% in the fourth slot. Because if you had the decay starting from the first spot, the decay value would be less. This way you prevent things like “I had 3 out of 4 perfect feats, but I could still get a disadvantage if an opponent had 4 strong but not perfect feats”.
Let me show how it would workout:
Slot 1 = 10/10 (first feat) * 30% * 10 * 100% = 3 points
Slot 2 = 10/10 (second feat) * 30% * 10 * 100% = 3 points
Slot 3 = 10/10 (second feat decay) * 30% * 10 * 40% = 1.2 points
Slot 4 = 10/10 (second feat decay) * 10% * 10 * 30% = 0.3 points
Total points = 7.5 points

If this wasn’t a thing and the decay could only be from the first slot this is how it would look:
Slot 3 = 10/10 (first feat decay at third place) * 30% * 10 * 30% = 1 point
Slot 4 = 10/10 * 10% * 10 * 20% = 0.2 points
Total points = 7.2 points

Creativity and Solidity Evaluation
Underlying Principle (UP) is the functional mechanisms that enable a feat to work. This means it’s not tied to a specific situation.
Contextual Application (CA) is the use of functional mechanisms adapted to a specific situation.
Obviously UP is much more important for both originality and solidity evaluation compared to CA.
Talking about Creativity: this gap in importance is really high. So justifying one feat being above another, by using the fact that one has an UP that is original and the other doesn’t, is a strong form of argument.
Talking about Solidity: if your plan/strategy relies on an unrealistic UP, the chances of having the “Replicability” case are almost zero, meaning you won’t get “excused” even slightly if your feat is unrealistic. Parco di TovelI welcome you to the RFES scaling document.
What RFES measures
RFES scales a character’s intelligence by asking: How cognitively demanding would it be for a human author to conceive the feats that the character performs?

In other words, how much intelligence has been transmitted from the author into the character. Only the output matters (and this is a matter of logical consequence, not an imperative order).

Perspective (Rule 0)
Because the metric is “difficulty for a human author,” RFES uses the authorial perspective rather than the narrative one.
-
Authorial vantage and degrees of freedom
From the author’s perspective:
1- The author has semi-omnipotence (they can make nearly everything happen in the story).
2- The author has access to real-world knowledge (logic, science, strategy, prior art, empirical data).
-
The binding constraint (reason I said “nearly”)
Despite these freedoms, one key restriction remains: the author’s cognitive / intelligence prowess are finite. This finitude is what makes comparisons meaningful.
-
About Effort
RFES acknowledges that effort (time/energy spent crafting a feat) can *aid* cognitive transmission, but it is merely taken into account indirectly: production time or resources are not scored themselves; they “matter” only insofar as they increase the cognitive density and coherence embedded in the feat.
This is because what matters *is* merely the output.
