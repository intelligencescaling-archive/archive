TIME LOOP CASE
The victim, Vakita Keigo, 24, was found murdered at around 10 p.m. It is known that he worked as a newspaper deliveryman. Tsukumo and Midzuno are tasked with investigating this first murder. After finding illicit objects at the crime scene, Midzuno believes that drug trafficking is at the heart of the case. However, Tsukumo practically dismisses this proposition and, analyzing the situation more critically, questions what the murder weapon might have been (thereby avoiding what he considers to be a deductive error on Midzuno’s part)
Vakita was killed, and a bloodstained stone containing the murderer’s fingerprints was found near the body. It is precisely when information that genuinely appears relevant to him emerges that Tsukumo’s approach changes completely, demonstrating that he seems to distinguish with precision what is and is not relevant to the investigation. This information is an envelope found at the crime scene. From this clue, Tsukumo naturally begins looking into Vakita’s acquaintances and the people who worked alongside him. However, his primary suspect is specifically a boy who also worked as a newspaper deliveryman. His first major hypothesis is Makoto, who, besides having a relationship with the victim, also has a sister hospitalized in a local hospital. The latter information is based on the envelope found at the crime scene, which Tsukumo quickly associates with a type of envelope used by hospitals in the city to store money.
Knowing that the murderer’s fingerprints were left on the weapon, it would be enough to compare those prints with Makoto’s to determine whether there was a match. Naturally, Tsukumo also takes advantage of the situation to apply psychological pressure on Makoto, who already appeared to be emotionally vulnerable.

In this way, his perception functions as a kind of investigative filter: it does not merely register what is before his senses, but constantly questions the meaning of each element and its possible relationship with the rest of the evidence.
One of the best examples occurs during the first murder, when Tsukumo notices small bloodstains near Vakita's body. While the other investigators apparently attach no particular importance to these marks, Tsukumo examines their shape and identifies that they correspond to the outline of an envelope. The feat is particularly relevant because it requires more than precise visual perception. He must recognize an unusual shape within a scene filled with information, mentally isolate it, and subsequently establish a correspondence between the observed pattern and a specific object. (This is later used to infer the Temporal Loop.)
This demonstrates a high level of sensory interpretation, in which visual information is not treated as independent elements but as data that may represent something beyond their immediate appearance. The bloodstain, by itself, is merely a stain; however, Tsukumo is able to interpret its geometry and transform it into the possibility of an envelope, creating a new investigative lead.
The same principle can be observed in his analysis of Makoto. Tsukumo notices that, despite the heavy rain, the boy was not soaked. This information could easily be dismissed as circumstantial, especially because it does not constitute direct evidence of involvement in the crime. Tsukumo, however, recognizes the discrepancy between the external conditions and the physical state he observes. From this inconsistency, he establishes a new premise for questioning Makoto's alibi.
This type of reasoning demonstrates that his perception does not depend solely on what is explicitly present, but also on what should be present under certain circumstances and is not. In other words, Tsukumo is capable of perceiving anomalies by comparing the observed state with the expected state. His perception, therefore, possesses a strong inferential component.
This characteristic also manifests in the way Tsukumo interprets the envelope found at the crime scene. He does not merely recognize the existence of the object. He associates its appearance with a specific type of envelope used by hospitals to store money and subsequently connects this information to the existence of Makoto's sister, who was hospitalized. A seemingly mundane sequence of information—an envelope, its design, its use, and the existence of a hospitalized person—is transformed into a much broader hypothesis concerning the killer's identity and possible motivation.
What is most impressive, therefore, is the density of the associations Tsukumo is able to construct from them.
His sensory interpretation appears to operate through a process similar to:
Stimulus → identification of anomaly → contextualization → association with prior information → hypothesis formation → search for confirmation.
This also explains his ability to adapt his investigation as new information emerges. When he realizes that Makoto does not respond to conventional pressure, Tsukumo does not interpret this lack of reaction simply as innocence or indifference. He treats the observed behavior as a new piece of sensory and psychological data, seeking to understand what that reaction—or lack thereof—reveals about the individual's internal structure.
From this, he mentalizes Makoto as someone with a high degree of emotional control and realizes that logical stimuli or incriminating evidence would probably not be sufficient to disrupt his stability. Consequently, he searches for another stimulus capable of producing a significant reaction: his sister.
When Makoto finally reacts to the provocation involving Shiori, Tsukumo immediately interprets this behavioral change as confirmation of his previous hypothesis. Thus, a simple alteration in the suspect's expression and behavior ceases to be merely an emotional reaction and instead becomes evidence regarding his psychological and motivational structure.
This is a fundamental point for understanding Tsukumo's level of sensory interpretation. He does not merely observe what a person does, but also how they respond to different stimuli, comparing these responses to determine which elements possess greater psychological relevance for that individual.
Consequently, his perception is highly contextual in nature. The same stimulus does not necessarily have the same meaning for everyone, and Tsukumo demonstrates an ability to understand this. Makoto's reaction to the accusations, his lack of reaction to logical pressure, and his behavioral change when Shiori is mentioned are analyzed together, allowing Tsukumo to gradually reconstruct his motivational structure.
This reaches an even higher level when considering his ability to connect seemingly unrelated pieces of information to produce hypotheses on a much larger scale. The most extreme example of this characteristic is his interpretation concerning the Temporal Loop. In this case, the distinguishing factor is not simply perceiving an isolated phenomenon, but being able to recognize that certain seemingly independent events, patterns, and inconsistencies may possess a common structural relationship.
The inference of a Temporal Loop therefore represents a natural extension of the same ability demonstrated in smaller cases. In the case of the envelope, Tsukumo connects a visual characteristic to an object, then to the hospital, subsequently to Shiori, and finally to the possible motivation behind the crime. In Makoto's case, he connects weather conditions to the state of his clothing and transforms a physical discrepancy into a potential challenge to his alibi. On a much larger scale, interpreting the Temporal Loop requires him to group multiple anomalies, recognize recurring patterns, and search for a single explanation capable of accommodating information that initially appears incompatible.
This demonstrates an extremely advanced form of pattern recognition and sensory synthesis. Tsukumo appears capable of transforming a large quantity of fragmented stimuli into a coherent representation of the investigated scenario. The greater the amount of available information, the more possible associations he can explore.
Tsukumo's specialty lies in the combination of Observation and Perception, where he first identifies details that others fail to notice and then interprets those details within a broader context, assigning them meaning and connecting them to other pieces of information.
His greatest distinguishing characteristic is precisely his ability to find information within what appears not to be information.
A bloodstain can be an envelope.
An envelope can indicate a hospital.
A hospital can indicate Shiori.
Makoto's reaction can reveal his greatest vulnerability.
The absence of water on his clothes can challenge his alibi.
And multiple seemingly disconnected anomalies can, on a much larger scale, contribute to identifying an anomalous temporal structure.
Therefore, Tsukumo demonstrates an exceptional level of sensory interpretation because his perception does not end the moment he observes something. Every stimulus is potentially a piece of a larger system, and his ability lies precisely in discovering how those pieces fit together. It is this capacity to transform microscopic details into macroscopic hypotheses, associating seemingly irrelevant information until he constructs complex and coherent explanations, that makes his sensory perception one of the most extraordinary characteristics of his investigative method.

THE CAPTURE OF DETAILS AND THE ABILITY TO TENSE HIS OWN HIPPOCAMPUS
HyperAcuity
Tsukumo’s extraordinary interpretive ability is directly related to his innate capacity to capture and retain an exceptional amount of sensory information. His distinguishing trait does not lie solely in his ability to analyze what he perceives, but in the fact that his observation appears to operate with a scope far beyond that of an ordinary individual. Tsukumo does not limit himself to recording information that is evidently relevant to the investigation; he seems capable of absorbing even information that, at the time, would naturally be classified as trivial, unnecessary, or completely disconnected from the case.
This is particularly evidenced by his own statement about “stretching the hippocampus to its maximum.” By instructing the nurses to force their memories to recover any detail related to Makoto or Shiori, even those they considered insignificant, Tsukumo demonstrates an understanding that the relevance of information is not necessarily determined at the moment it is perceived. An apparently useless detail may later acquire enormous value when associated with other pieces of information.
This conception appears to directly reflect his own cognitive functioning. Tsukumo treats the investigative reality as an extremely broad set of data, avoiding prematurely discarding any element simply because it has no immediate utility. This allows his memory to function as a genuine reservoir of sensory information, in which seemingly disconnected details remain available to be subsequently retrieved, compared, and reinterpreted.
It is precisely this enormous quantity of captured information that provides the raw material for his hyper-interpretation.
Tsukumo’s interpretation, therefore, does not arise from a few carefully selected pieces of data, but from an extraordinarily large amount of information that can be cross-referenced. The greater the number of preserved details, the greater the number of potential relationships that can be established between them. Thus, what initially appears to be a secondary characteristic—such as a mark, a position, an expression, an environmental condition, or any other visual peculiarity—can later become a fundamental variable within a much more complex hypothesis.
This explains why Tsukumo is capable of producing interpretations that seem disproportionately deep relative to the initial stimulus. The stimulus he perceives is merely the starting point; the true complexity lies in the amount of information he is capable of associating with it.
Again, let us use the example of the envelope identified from the bloodstains. The initial piece of information is extremely small: a specific blood pattern near the corpse. However, Tsukumo is able to recognize its shape, associate it with an envelope, identify the type of envelope, and connect it to its use in hospitals. This information is then connected to the existence of Makoto’s sister, who is hospitalized, creating a new investigative lead.
The same principle appears when he notices that Makoto is not wet despite the heavy rain. The condition of his clothes, in isolation, is a mundane detail. Tsukumo, however, possesses enough contextual information to recognize the discrepancy between the observed state and the expected state. From there, he transforms a simple environmental observation into an inconsistency capable of challenging Makoto’s alibi.
These examples demonstrate that his perception operates in layers. First comes the raw capture of the stimulus; then its retention; subsequently, its comparison with other stored information; and finally, its interpretation within a broader structure.
Capture -> retention -> association -> contextualization -> interpretation -> hypothesis.
He preserves a much greater quantity of data and allows his interpretive ability to determine later which pieces possess value. This drastically reduces the risk of discarding relevant information simply because its importance is not yet apparent.
This characteristic also helps explain his ability to perceive patterns that other investigators fail to identify. If two people observe the same scene, but one records only the elements that are immediately relevant while the other records dozens of minor details, the latter possesses a much greater number of variables from which to establish relationships (aka a perfect methodology considering his approach). Tsukumo operates at this second level, but to an extraordinarily high degree.
For this reason, his Observation and Perception are practically inseparable. His exceptional observation provides an unusual quantity of data, while his exceptional perception transforms that data into meaning. One without the other would be insufficient. Capturing details without knowing how to interpret them would produce only a large collection of disconnected information; deeply interpreting information without possessing sufficient data, on the other hand, would increase the risk of speculation. Tsukumo demonstrates both abilities simultaneously.
His hyper-interpretive ability can therefore be understood as a direct consequence of his hyper-capture of sensory information. He does not merely see more; he apparently records more than should reasonably be necessary. And precisely because he does not immediately establish a rigid boundary between “useful information” and “useless information,” he can later retrieve details that others would have discarded and incorporate them into extremely complex reasoning structures.
This characteristic reaches its peak in the construction of broader hypotheses, such as his inference of the Time Loop. A conclusion of this magnitude cannot arise from a single extraordinary stimulus. It requires the accumulation of countless small inconsistencies, patterns, and seemingly independent pieces of information, followed by the ability to recognize that all of them may be explained by a common structure. The ability to “stretch the hippocampus” in this sense provides precisely the quantity of data necessary for Tsukumo to perform this type of synthesis.
Thus, the truly absurd aspect of his sensory ability is not simply that he perceives details others fail to notice, but that he preserves details others would not even consider worthy of noticing. His mind seems to operate under the premise that no information is truly useless until its context has been fully understood.
This ability transforms his sensory capture into an extraordinary investigative tool: Tsukumo accumulates an abnormal quantity of information, retains even those details that appear irrelevant, and subsequently uses his associative and interpretive abilities to uncover relationships that remained invisible to everyone else. The more fragments he possesses, the greater his ability to reconstruct the complete picture.
Ultimately, his sensory ability is not merely a matter of perceptual acuity, but of informational density. Tsukumo perceives more, retains more, and, most importantly, is capable of extracting meaning from a much greater proportion of what he perceives. It is precisely this combination of hyper-capture and hyper-interpretation that allows minimal stimuli to be transformed into investigative hypotheses of extraordinary scale.

RESISTANCE’s ANALYSIS [WIP]

COGNITIVE / AFFECTIVE / SHIELDING

* AFFECTIVE - The ability to resist internal or external emotional influences while maintaining emotional stability and control over feelings such as fear, anger, guilt, attachment, or despair. It reflects how difficult it is to emotionally destabilize the character or manipulate them through emotional bonds and psychological pressure.
* COGNITIVE - The ability to resist interference that affects thought, reasoning, or judgment. It includes resistance to logical manipulation, misinformation, mental confusion, induced biases, cognitive overload, and attempts to distort perception or decision-making.
* SHIELDING - The ability to protect internal information, intentions, vulnerabilities, or mental states from being read, profiled, manipulated, or exploited by others. It involves expressive self-control, concealment of thoughts, unpredictability, and the creation of barriers that make it difficult for others to “access” or use their mind against them.

COGNITIVE  RESIST:

Tsukumo’s cognitive resistance can be understood as a direct consequence of the combination of his Awareness, inferential ability, and flexibility of thought. It does not simply consist of being skeptical or suspicious of everything presented to him, but rather in the ability to prevent external information, evidence, or argumentative structures from automatically determining his interpretation of reality. Above all, Tsukumo demonstrates an exceptional ability to question the very premises he uses to interpret an event, reconstruct the situation from different perspectives, and replace an explanation when it is no longer capable of accommodating the observed facts.
This is particularly evident in the formulation of the hypothesis that the Time Loop exists. Initially, Tsukumo is faced with a situation that could be interpreted conventionally: several apparently related murders, Makoto directly or indirectly connected to the victims, and, at the same time, a sequence of alibis that seemingly placed him away from the crime scenes. A less cognitively resistant investigator might simply accept these alibis as evidence of innocence. Tsukumo, however, does not limit himself to observing what the evidence apparently demonstrates; he questions the very construction of the evidence. The fact that Makoto was not wet despite the heavy rain, the wallet conveniently appearing at a particular location, and the existence of a silhouette incapable of objectively identifying him are not treated as automatic confirmations of his innocence. On the contrary, Tsukumo begins to consider that these very circumstances may have been constructed to produce a specific conclusion. This approach represents an extremely relevant form of cognitive resistance because it prevents the individual from being passively guided by the superficial interpretation of information.
The same mechanism appears when Tsukumo begins connecting Makoto to Katagiri. The coinciding school absences, her exit from the hospital through the back, her attempt to avoid certain cameras, and the unusual behavior observed afterward cease to be isolated events and instead become parts of a larger causal structure. Tsukumo does not assume that every event necessarily has an independent explanation. He searches for a hypothesis capable of connecting the events. Thus, his resistance to manipulation does not depend merely on rejecting false information, but on possessing enough autonomy to reorganize the information he receives into a structure different from the one his adversary intended to produce.
This characteristic becomes even more evident when the evidence begins challenging conventional assumptions about the situation. Tsukumo observes Shiori’s changes around midnight, Makoto’s seemingly impossible movements, the disappearances and reappearances, and the repetition of events associated with the same temporal interval. Instead of simply classifying these phenomena as coincidences, observation errors, or insufficient information, he begins searching for a unifying explanation. The essential point is that Tsukumo does not treat the concept of “impossible” as a definitive conclusion; rather, he turns impossibility into something that needs to be investigated. If the conventional model of reality cannot explain the events, then perhaps the problem lies in the model rather than necessarily in the events themselves.
This is probably one of the strongest manifestations of his cognitive resistance. Tsukumo must overcome not only an external attempt at manipulation, but one of the most fundamental biases in human reasoning: the tendency to preserve assumptions about how reality should work. The existence of time travel directly contradicts his conventional understanding of the world. Nevertheless, when all conventional alternatives begin requiring exceptions, coincidences, or explanations that are excessively fragmented, he allows himself to consider the extraordinary hypothesis. This does not mean abandoning rational thought in favor of irrational belief. Quite the opposite: he accepts an extremely unlikely hypothesis because it possesses greater explanatory power than the alternatives available.
His Time Loop hypothesis, therefore, does not represent a simple conclusion; it is progressively constructed through the accumulation of inconsistencies. The murders display connections with one another; Makoto has relationships with the victims; his alibis are excessively convenient; there are objective inconsistencies within those alibis; Katagiri displays suspicious behavior temporally connected to Makoto; Shiori experiences abnormal changes at precisely certain moments; Makoto appears in locations without his approach being recorded; and seemingly impossible movements repeatedly occur within the same temporal intervals. Individually, each element could admit an alternative explanation. When considered simultaneously, however, they form a much more difficult structure to ignore. Tsukumo realizes that the Time Loop hypothesis does not merely explain one strange event: it allows all of these events to be understood as manifestations of the same phenomenon.
This is precisely where his THINKING ability becomes essential. Tsukumo demonstrates enough flexibility to alter the causal model being used throughout the investigation. Instead of remaining attached to the first available explanation, he continually reformulates his interpretation as new information emerges. His thinking possesses a strongly abductive character: he searches for the explanation that best accommodates the body of evidence. At the same time, he uses deductive reasoning to determine what consequences should exist if a given hypothesis were true.
The verification of the hypothesis that Makoto is the murderer demonstrates this characteristic. Starting from the premise that Makoto is the serial killer, Tsukumo asks what events should be expected if this hypothesis were true. Makoto is connected to the victims; he presents alibis precisely when his presence would be most problematic; he demonstrates an exceptionally strong emotional relationship with Shiori; events involving his sister coincide with important moments in the investigation; and individuals connected to the situation begin displaying behavior consistent with an attempt to protect or assist Makoto. The hypothesis is then tested not only by what it has already explained, but by the consequences it produces. This process functions as a form of self-correction and reduces Tsukumo’s vulnerability to arbitrary explanations.
His Awareness provides the necessary raw material for this process to be so effective. His ability to capture and preserve details allows Tsukumo not to depend exclusively on the most obvious information or on information that other people consider relevant. He demonstrates an explicit concern with recovering even seemingly insignificant details related to Makoto and Shiori, understanding that information may acquire importance only after new evidence is discovered. This is extremely relevant to his cognitive resistance because it reduces the possibility of third parties controlling his interpretation simply by controlling which information reaches him. The larger the body of data he is able to preserve, the greater his ability to compare versions, identify discrepancies, and perceive patterns that would not be visible from a reduced set of information.
His observational ability, therefore, does not operate independently of his interpretive ability. Tsukumo does not merely perceive more; he is able to use what he perceives to challenge seemingly established structures. A small detail can function as an anomaly capable of destabilizing an entire explanation. The state of Makoto’s clothes can challenge an alibi. A recently installed camera can alter the interpretation of Katagiri’s behavior. A physiological change in Shiori at a specific time can connect a medical phenomenon to an apparently criminal event. The value of this information does not necessarily lie in its individual importance, but in Tsukumo’s ability to integrate it into larger structures.
This also explains why his cognitive resistance possesses such a strong creative component. To resist logical manipulation, it is not enough to identify that an explanation is wrong; one must possess alternatives. An individual may recognize that something does not make sense and still remain trapped within the presented narrative because they cannot formulate another model. Tsukumo demonstrates precisely the opposite ability. When the conventional explanation fails, he expands the space of available hypotheses. The Time Loop is the ultimate example of this: he is capable of conceiving an explanatory structure that initially appears completely incompatible with common sense, but which later demonstrates the ability to unify what no conventional explanation could accommodate.
There is also a particularly important metacognitive dimension. Tsukumo does not merely question external information; he questions the very processes he uses to interpret it. During his mental reconstruction of the case, he realizes that certain conventional assumptions were functioning as barriers to his understanding. Instead of altering the events to preserve those assumptions, he alters the assumptions themselves. The ability to recognize “perhaps the problem lies in the way I am thinking about this” is one of the deepest forms of cognitive resistance. He does not become resistant simply because he possesses strong convictions; he becomes resistant because he can abandon his own convictions when they are no longer sustainable.
Consequently, Tsukumo’s cognitive resistance should not be understood merely as resistance to lies, persuasion, or misinformation. Its most important level is resistance to the imposition of interpretive models. An adversary may present false evidence, construct alibis, control information, or create circumstances designed to lead him toward a specific conclusion. Tsukumo, however, seeks to reconstruct the causality behind that information. He asks why a particular fact occurred, what other information is connected to it, what consequences should exist if a given hypothesis were true, and which elements remain unexplained.
In the case of the Time Loop, this reaches its extreme: the very phenomenon that initially should have been dismissed as impossible becomes, for Tsukumo, the most rational explanation available. His cognitive resistance manifests precisely in his ability not to allow common sense to determine in advance which hypotheses can or cannot be considered. He does not accept the Time Loop because he wants to believe in it; he accepts it because, after gathering, comparing, and reinterpreting an enormous amount of evidence, he realizes that rejecting it would require far more explanatory concessions than accepting it.
Thus, Tsukumo’s Cognitive Resistance is essentially a combination of autonomy, awareness of his own biases, flexibility of hypotheses, and the ability to reconstruct causality. His Awareness ensures that he possesses an exceptional amount of information; his Inference transforms that information into relationships and hypotheses; his Thinking allows him to explore alternative models; and his metacognition prevents him from becoming trapped by his own premises. The result is an investigator who is particularly difficult to manipulate intellectually, because an attempt to lead him toward a specific conclusion does not necessarily need to fail at the informational level: it can simply be dismantled when Tsukumo reorganizes the same information within a different causal model.
