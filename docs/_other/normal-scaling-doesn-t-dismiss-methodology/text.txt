Normal scaling doesn't dismiss methodology

I feel like I'm saying something obvious here, but I've been seeing a general attitude that seems to pit normal scaling and methodology as inherently mutually exclusive, when that just isn't the case.

First off, it's worth making some clarifications. When it comes to what I've seen referred to as "pure methodology", from my understanding it has the sole goal of scaling the methodological process and nothing else. It's a whole different exercise not what I will be talking about in this post. Normal scaling, on the other hand, seeks to assess the characters in the entirety of their abilities. So to distill the difference, it comes down to how much importance is given to certain feats over others. However, as I see it, the line between them is not as big as it's made out to be.

I'll explain myself, normal scaling decides that the pool of feats that should be considered for a character's scaling is equal to the entirety of their feats as shown, told, or implied in their story. This gives us what to work with. What's missing is the metric. Scaling, by definition, requires one. That metric, or the main one at least, is methodology.

Let me draw a comparison with another common metric, scale. Scale, generally speaking, can have a correlation with the impressiveness of a feat, but I think everyone will agree with the idea that just because a character operates at a larger scale it doesn't mean that they should scale higher than someone who doesn't (it'd be akin to saying that every president ever could outsmart anyone who hasn't actively ran a country, or that ad designers who can influence masses are inherently more impressive than someone influencing a single person, to matter the extent, which obviously isn't the case). It's a restrictive metric that doesn't take context into account. So let's take a step back, what causes higher operational scale to be more impressive, in the cases where it does? A number of things, such as the amount of variables or volatile elements, variety of agents, etc... and whenever these things factor into a feat, then the feat's methodology will have taken them into account, which makes using scale alone as a metric redundant.

The reason I believe methodology to be the best metric is because it's the closest we can get to a universal one. We can scale the processes underpinning the feats in a way that doesn't ignore the context surrounding them. It measures things like effectiveness, novelty, complexity, soundness, intricacy, and more, all pretty much universal concepts that allow to assess the practical cognitive demand and quality of a feat.

Where does this leave off-screen feats? Obviously that's the biggest point of contention between normal scaling and methodology users, because off-screen feats/statements don't have a traceable methodology and are for that reason impossible to accurately scale like other feats. So how can we maintain methodology as a metric with such feats? It's still possible. You need to draw the minimum/simplest logical implications for the feat to be possible as presented (a low end if you will), and in most cases run with that. If said implications exceed the level the character has proven to operate at, then the off-screen feat has solid grounds for being considered an upscale. However, if the high end would be a drastic upscale while the low end would be more consistent with what the character has shown, then it's more fair to consider the low end valid. What these minimum implications entail is obviously up to debate, but so are most aspects of scaling and that's the whole point of SCD. As for purely narrative implications (for example, a feat that may have a non-intelligence related explanation but is presented in the story as a feat of intelligence), here's what I think: I don't like using "narrative scaling" over "normal scaling" (if a distinction can be made) because it places the focus on authorial intent, which isn't always accessible or clear, but at the same time I think it's unfair to ignore it when it is.

It goes without saying, methodology isn't the only factor in normal scaling, stuff like formulation speed or the stress under which a feat is executed play a major role too, but they're more or less complementary.

The more I write this the more I think I'm saying stuff that will be obvious to many people, but at the same time I think it's worth making a post about it. I've seen many time normal scaling being misconstrued as "hype-scaling" that takes things only at face value, or using methodology as a metric leading to ignoring aspects of the characters/story, when the two things are fully compatible. I'm hoping that clarifying this can soften the recent beef a little bit.

I'm open to discussion of course, as long as it's respectful.
