note #3: how to notice whether something is deduction, induction or abduction

in deduction & abduction , i always leave one of the premises as a conditional proposition (categorical propositions are also acceptable)

that is necessary as deduction & abduction require general rules/laws/axioms in order to form a conclusion

examples of a general rule/law/axiom:

all men are mortal

if something is a dog, then it is a mammal

not all birds fly

it is christmas if and only if (iff) it is 25th december

all cats are mammals

if something is a cat, then it is a mammal

animals do not have beaks unless they are birds

in order to be born, you need a father

it's a right angled triangle iff pythagoras theorem applies

if i'm not in the world, then i'm not in antartica

^the underlined words are syncategorematic words which must be included in reasoning in order for it to be deduction or abduction

which is needed in order to deduce or abduce a conclusion

then in a deduction, not only do you have a rule; you also have the antecedent or a failed consequent in relation to the stated rule

example:

rule in conditional form: if daisie is a swan, daisie would be white (if S, then W)

positive case: daisie is a swan (S)

positive conclusion: so daisie is white (W)

OR

rule in conditional form: if daisie is a swan, daisie would be white (if S, then W)

negative case: daisie is not white(~W)

negative conclusion: so daisie is not a swan(~S)

both of these are valid, sound deductions as they have a rule, a case and a conclusion which follows automatically

in abduction, you only have a consequent (which is known by observation) in relation to the stated rule and have the conclusion in a likelihood as abduction doesn't guarantee the conclusion but rather concludes with the most likely hypothesis/cause (which is deductively fallacious)

example:

rule in conditional form: if daisie is a swan, daisie would be white (if S, then W)

positive observation: daisie is white(W)

positive conclusion: so it's likely daisie is a swan (S)
