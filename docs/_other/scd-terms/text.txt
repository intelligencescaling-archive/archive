Microleverage: Applying precise, localized actions to trigger disproportionate changes within systems through system analysis, leverage point identification, and precision action design.

Interoperability: Enabling separate, distinct systems to communicate and function together across boundaries. This evaluates whether independently designed components can operate as a unified whole.

Resourcefulness: The ability to identify and allocate resources across interconnected system nodes for maximum systemic output, differing from mere resource management by involving identification of resources others might overlook.

Systems Thinking: The ability to understand how different parts of a system interact and influence each other as a whole through boundary definition, component identification, relationship mapping, emergence recognition, and dynamic analysis.

Cascade Prediction: The ability to foresee how a single change triggers sequential consequences that propagate through a connected system over time through initial change identification, connection mapping, propagation prediction, and outcome assessment.

Interplay Recognition: The ability to identify system behaviors that arise from interactions rather than individual components through behavior observation, component analysis, interaction analysis, and emergence identification.

Archetype Recognition: The ability to identify recurring structural patterns across different complex systems through pattern extraction, abstraction, pattern matching, and prediction.

Systemics: The study of systems and their interconnected components to enable effective functioning and scalability. It focuses on understanding relationships, interactions, and dynamics to optimize complex structures over time.

First Principles: The process of breaking a problem down to its most basic, foundational truths through assumption identification, questioning, fundamental truth identification, solution building, and verification.

Analogical Bridging: Recognizing structural similarities between a current novel problem and previously encountered situations, then transferring proven solutions from familiar contexts into entirely new ones.

Convergence Testing: Systematically evaluating multiple candidate solutions against available evidence, constraints, and predicted outcomes to identify and commit to the most viable approach.

Directional Hypothesis: Forming an educated initial guess about which general approach will yield the best results when full testing of every option is impossible. Rather than exhaustively exploring all paths, a character with strong directional hypothesis identifies the most promising avenue to pursue first based on partial information.

Constraint Navigation: The ability to find clever solutions within tight limits, turning restrictions into advantages and highlighting adaptability when resources, rules, or time are strongly limiting.

Novel Variable Isolation: Identifying and separating critical, relevant factors from irrelevant noise in an unfamiliar problem to focus problem-solving efforts where they matter most.

Dynamic Recalibration: Adjusting problem-solving approaches in real time as the novel problem shifts, evolves, or introduces unexpected constraints mid-solution.

Edge Case Validation: Tests extreme, unlikely scenarios to confirm a solution works for all possible iterations of the novel problem, not just common use cases.

Solution Prototyping: Rapidly building and testing multiple incomplete, functional models of solutions to identify viability before committing resources to full implementation.

Pathway Generation: The ability to construct multiple independent solution frameworks for a novel problem when existing methodologies fail or are unavailable.

Boundary Testing: Proactively exploring and pushing scenario limits to expose vulnerabilities, find creative workarounds, and expand possibilities.

Experimental: The process of discovering solutions through rapid trial-and-error experimentation when existing knowledge or theoretical approaches are insufficient to solve a novel problem.

NCPS (Novel Complex Problem Solving): The ability to swiftly analyze and solve novel, intricate, and open-ended problems, demonstrating adaptability, creativity, and a deep understanding of the underlying principles involved. This involves applying and integrating knowledge from diverse domains to devise innovative and effective solutions.

Decisions: The systematic process of evaluating available information, identifying potential courses of action, and selecting the optimal choice aligned with objectives.

Awareness: The capacity to perceive, interpret, and anticipate environmental cues, social dynamics, and hidden variables in real time.

Metacognition: Awareness and understanding of one's own thought processes, involving the ability to control and reflect on how you learn, think, and solve problems.

Bayesian Update: A method of adjusting beliefs or predictions as new evidence becomes available, weighing new information against what you already know to update your understanding of a situation.

Chance Noticing: The ability to identify favorable circumstances or openings that others might overlook.

Methodology: The systematic framework used to gather relevant information, analyze options objectively, and apply consistent criteria when forming judgments—ensuring decisions are grounded in evidence rather than assumption.

Bias Control: Recognize, mitigate, and override cognitive biases—such as confirmation bias, overconfidence, or emotional interference—to ensure objective, rational decision-making.

Calibration: The precision in matching one's confidence to the actual strength of the available evidence. It evaluates how well a character avoids overconfidence in weak conclusions and undue doubt in strong ones.

Heuristics: The effective use of mental shortcuts and instinctive judgments under uncertainty or time pressure. It evaluates the practical wisdom and speed of a character's "good enough" reasoning.

Judgment: Making sound and informed decisions based on available information and analysis without biases, weighing the pros and cons of different options and making decisions that align with goals.

Critical: Analyzing, evaluating, and interpreting information objectively and rationally, considering multiple perspectives, arguments, and evidence while questioning assumptions, identifying biases, and making reasoned judgments.

Abstract: Identifying and manipulating intangible concepts, patterns, or relationships beyond concrete reality to enhance reasoning capabilities. It enables engagement with complex systems, hypotheticals, or universals inaccessible through sensory input alone.

Convergent: Using logic and information to arrive at specific, correct, and well-established solutions.

Divergent: Generating high-volume, varied solutions to open-ended problems—exploring all possible approaches without early judgment. Prioritizes idea diversity over immediate practicality.

Systematic: A methodical, stage-gated approach to problem-solving that relies on formalized frameworks (e.g. scientific method). It progresses through modular phases (hypothesis → testing → validation) while rigorously cross-checking internal consistency.

Counterfactual: Reasoning about alternative pasts or futures by mentally simulating "what if" scenarios that contradict established facts. Enables causal learning, contingency planning, and strategic refinement by exploring how outcomes shift when key variables are altered or reversed.

Comprehension: The ability to understand and make sense of information, ideas, or instructions, grasping meaning quickly without needing additional explanation or context.

Paraconsistent: The ability to reason with and operate within contradictions, inconsistencies, or conflicting information without logical breakdown or cognitive paralysis. It enables a character to hold opposing truths simultaneously and still function effectively.

Associative: The ability to link seemingly unrelated concepts or ideas through concept activation, remote association, novelty assessment, and creative application to generate novel connections or solutions.

Parallel: The ability to maintain awareness of multiple ongoing cognitive processes without actively processing them, allowing seamless re-engagement with any thread as needed.

Holistic: Considering systems or problems as whole rather than just as collections of parts, focusing on understanding how components interact within a larger context.

Lateral: Problem-solving through deliberate logic subversion—provocatively reframing constraints or assumptions to force unconventional solutions. Characterized by paradox acceptance and non-obvious connections that bypass traditional reasoning pathways.

Thinking: The ability to interpret, understand, and make sense of information, experiences, and perceptions through various thinking styles as appropriate for different situations.

Deductive: A form of logical reasoning that moves from a general principle or rule to a specific conclusion.

Inductive: A form of logical reasoning that moves from specific observations/facts to a broader general conclusion.

Abductive: A form of logical reasoning that starts with an incomplete observation(s) and then arrives at the most plausible explanation.

Probabilistic: Drawing conclusions based on likelihoods rather than certainties when evidence is incomplete through probability estimation, evidence weighting, Bayesian updating, and uncertainty management.

Backwards: A reasoning method where you start from a desired future outcome and work backward to map all required prior steps, helping plan effectively by reverse-engineering the path from an end goal to the current state.

Reasoning: The ability to utilize a set of facts to determine a conclusion by using logic through deductive, inductive, abductive, probabilistic, backwards, and analogical reasoning.

Shift Detection: Identifying subtle internal or external changes in conditions, including cognitive load, environmental shifts, or evolving stakeholder expectations, triggering the adaptive process before changes disrupt progress toward goals.

Routine Shedding: Discarding ingrained habits, automated workflows, and legacy processes that no longer serve goals amid shifting conditions, preventing wasted effort on methods that have become ineffective.

Adaptive Consistency: Balancing flexible adjustments with stable core objectives to avoid erratic, unmoored changes that derail progress, ensuring adaptations align with long-term goals rather than reactive whims.

Sustained Agile Capacity: Maintaining adaptive responsiveness over prolonged periods of change without cognitive fatigue or degradation of decision-making quality, preventing adaptive burnout during extended volatile scenarios.

Gradient Adaptation: Adjusting behavior and processes at a pace matching the speed of change, whether gradual shifts or sudden disruptions, avoiding over-correcting for minor changes or under-correcting for major ones.

Cognitive Pivoting: Reconfiguring internal thought processes, reasoning frameworks, and decision-making criteria to align with new conditions, avoiding over-reliance on outdated mental models.

Adaptability: The capacity to modify cognitive processes, behaviors, and strategies in real time to maintain progress toward goals amid shifting internal or external conditions. It prioritizes fluid responsiveness to change over static domain mastery or predefined plans.

Conflict Resolution:  Resolving interpersonal conflicts or disputes by balancing empathy, negotiation, and authority to find a common ground between parties or de-escalate or exploit conflicts.

Influence Building: The systematic construction of social capital through calculated reputation engineering, alliance networks, and incentive architectures, proactively designing ecosystems where others choose alignment with the influencer's goals.

Communication: Effectively convey ideas, information, and messages to others, both verbally and non-verbally, in a clear, concise, and persuasive manner.

Narrativization: Packaging information as vivid, relatable stories to direct social interpretations and reactions. This proactive storytelling molds group narratives, ensuring others align with their framed reality.

Stakeholding: Identifying, engaging and influencing key players (Allies and enemies), while balancing their interests and expectations to ensure they align with one's objectives.

Networking: Establishment and maintenance of interpersonal connections to expand spheres of influence to curating relationships for information access, resource exchange, or alliance potential.

Delegation: The ability to assign tasks to subordinates or allies that align with their strengths to maximize collective output.

Leadership: The ability to effectively guide, inspire, and direct others through influence building, communication, narrativization, stakeholding, networking, and delegation to align individual incentives with collective goals.

Foundation: Assessing the lay of the land, understanding fundamental aspects, and establishing a solid foundation within any new context.

Filtering: Selectively engaging with or rejecting incoming information, influences, and opportunities based on their relevance, credibility, and potential impact. This involves actively deciding what deserves attention and what should be discarded to protect one's focus, judgment, and integrity.

Positioning: Aligning oneself relative to key elements in an environment to optimize influence. It focuses on achieving advantageous placement to control outcomes.

Safeguarding: Protecting oneself and allies from potential threats. Includes taking steps to prevent potential threats or vulnerabilities and developing contingency plans in case of emergencies.

Prevention: Prevention leverages foundational insight to filter harmful connections, position advantageously, and implement safeguards, turning foresight into action that stops risks at their source.

Building: Constructing a strategic framework by assembling tactics, resources, and objectives into a coherent scheme while navigating constraints and limitations. It focuses on the initial creation phase—establishing a workable foundation before execution begins.

Balancing: Managing competing priorities, constraints, and trade-offs in real-time, while maintaining balance and stability.

Complexity: Measures how seamlessly layered, overlapping tactics are incorporated into a strategy to raise the difficulty of deciphering and countering it.

Flexibility: The ability of a strategic approach to withstand and adapt to disruptions, uncertainties, and challenges of other strategies while maintaining its core objectives and effectiveness.

Fortitude: The likelihood of achieving intended goals by focusing on the strength and resilience of the tactics included, without emphasizing the limitations the character faces.

Logistics: Orchestrating and allocating resources in real-time to bring the strategic vision to life during execution. It ensures the right assets are in the right place at the right time, aligning tactics precisely without gaps or errors.

Forecasting: To anticipate future developments and proactively refine tactics to align with the long-term strategic vision. Analyzing trends, predicting ripple effects, and embedding flexibility into the strategy before disruptions occur.

Coherent: Ensuring all tactics, actions, and decisions within a strategy align with each other and the overarching goals without contradiction. It measures internal consistency—eliminating wasted effort and conflicting priorities.

Guiding: A high-level principle or rule that directs strategic decisions and keeps actions aligned with long-term goals. It serves as a fixed compass when tactics must change, ensuring the core direction remains constant.

Vision: The core long-term aims and objectives a strategy is designed to achieve.

Length: Measures the total time a strategy spans from initial execution to final conclusion.

Strategy: The active, systematic implementation of multiple quality-executed tactics to achieve the underlying vision.

Coverage: The breadth of scenarios, contingencies, and variables a character accounts for when creating a plan—measuring how many potential outcomes they've prepared for.

Versatility: The range of different strategies and tactics a character can formulate before execution—measuring how many distinct approaches they can develop to address the same objective.

Intricacies: The extent to which a character considers the depth and interconnectedness of various aspects of a situation, measuring the complexity and relationships between different factors.

Risk Amst: Identifying, analyzing, and evaluating potential risks and uncertainties that could impact the success of a plan.

Acclimation: Modifying an existing plan's tactics in real-time during execution when faced with novel variables or disruptions—adjusting the blueprint without abandoning core objectives.

Applicability: Evaluates the contextual relevance and actionable precision of a plan’s design—ensuring strategies align with real-world constraints, resources, and goal feasibility.

Endgame: Devising and executing plans to steer a scenario towards a predetermined outcome, skillfully managing remaining challenges and optimizing overall performance in the final stages.

Process: The orchestration of tactics and strategies across the plan’s timeline—sequencing actions, allocating resources, and synchronizing dependencies to maintain goal coherence.

Planning: A flexible blueprint that compiles multiple strategies and tactics centered around clear goals, involving predetermined steps with contingencies for foreseeable circumstances.

Mental Fortitude: The psychological strength and resilience to face and overcome challenges, maintain composure under pressure, and persist in the face of adversity.

Impulse Control: Resisting immediate strong urges, delaying gratification, and making thoughtful decisions rather than acting on momentary impulses.

Inscrutability: The deliberate cultivation of unpredictability, ambiguity, or emotional opacity to conceal one's true intentions, vulnerabilities, or thought processes from others, thereby reducing susceptibility to manipulation or exploitation.

Autonomy: The inherent capacity to govern one's own thoughts, decisions, and actions according to personal values and principles, free from undue external coercion or undue influence from others' expectations.

Clarity: The active maintenance of cognitive, emotional, and communicative transparency to establish firm boundaries, counteract manipulation, and neutralize external pressure, ensuring that openness and directness function as defensive shields rather than vulnerabilities.

Resistance: The ability to resist, counteract, or push back against perceived threats, demands, or changes through mental fortitude, impulse control, inscrutability, autonomy, and clarity that infringe upon their autonomy, values, or sense of self.

Constraints: The inherent or imposed constraints that restrict a character’s ability to execute a feat consistently or optimally. These define the boundaries within which their demonstrated skills must operate.

Opposition: The active resistance or countermeasures characters face when attempting feats, which influence the credibility of their actions. This establishes a baseline of challenge severity for comparative evaluation.

Complexity: The degree of intricacy involved in executing a feat, determined by required coordination, knowledge, and adaptation, with higher complexity indicating greater cognitive or operational demand.

Efficiency: The ratio of output to input, measuring how little effort is wasted to achieve results and focusing on optimizing performance with minimal unnecessary effort.

Consistency: The statistical reliability with which a character can reproduce a demonstrated ability at the same performance level when executed repeatedly across varying contexts, conditions, and opposition levels.

Flexibility Demand: The extent to which a feat requires dynamic adjustments to external variables, testing a character’s situational adaptability. This distinguishes rigidly rehearsed actions from spontaneous ingenuity.

Contextual Adversity: The environmental, social, or logistical pressures that degrade a feat’s feasibility, requiring extra effort or superior skill to overcome. This highlights resilience under disadvantageous conditions.

Effectiveness: The definitive success of a feat in fulfilling its intended purpose, disregarding extraneous effort or minor setbacks and prioritizing functional victory over stylistic execution.

Framework: A structured method to assess a character's demonstrated abilities through constraints, opposition, complexity, efficiency, consistency, flexibility demand, contextual adversity, and effectiveness, contextualizing their actions within measurable criteria.

Fixed: The scenario operates under unchangeable rules, boundaries, or conditions, requiring adaptation within a rigid framework. Success depends on proficiency within static constraints rather than altering them.

Non-Fixed: The scenario is fluid, with ever-shifting rules or dynamics, demanding continuous re-evaluation and realignment of strategies. Success hinges on dynamic responsiveness beyond preset constraints.

Baseline: The stability or volatility of a scenario’s fundamental rules, boundaries, or conditions. Measures whether the framework is a canvas (shaped by the character) or a box (immovable from the outset).

Linear: The scenario unfolds in a straight progression, with cause-and-effect sequences that are predictable and minimally branching. Mastery requires precision in single-path optimization rather than multilateral analysis.

Multilayered: The scenario requires navigating multiple overlapping cause-effect chains, often with non-obvious interactions. Mastery depends on simultaneous comprehension of nested complexities.

Dimensionality: The scope or layers of reality a character must engage with—whether the scenario operates on a single plane of existence or across multiple interconnected dimensions (physical, temporal, conceptual, metaphysical).

Tangible: The scenario is governed by physically constrained, verifiable mechanics, where actions are measured empirically. Mastery requires direct engagement with material systems.

Abstract: The scenario revolves around intangible, conceptual, or hypothetical constructs, where reality is subjective. Mastery requires indirect engagement with metaphysical systems.

Approach: The angle or method a character uses to engage with a scenario—whether they confront it head-on, circumvent it, infiltrate it, or reshape it from within.

Direct Warfare: Conflicts are explicitly combative, involving overt confrontation between agents with relatively transparent objectives. Mastery relies on superior tactics in face-to-face competition.

Indirect Warfare: Conflicts operate through hidden, relational, or systemic forces, where adversaries may not directly interact. Mastery depends on manipulating unseen leverage points.

Engagement: The nature of interaction with forces in the scenario (conflict/cooperation, direct/indirect). Divides scenarios based on whether they highlight frontal competition or systemic manipulation.

Singular: The scenario is isolated, affecting only individual agents or sharply demarcated systems. Mastery requires optimizing for a constrained niche without broader externalities.

Collective: The scenario spans interconnected agents, systems, or social/institutional networks. Mastery requires horizontal influence to cascade effects across multiple nodes.

Reach: The scale of impact determining whether the scenario allows isolated or consequential effects. Defines if influence radiates to a single node or a cascade of systems.

Concentrated: The scenario’s critical elements are centralized in a single locus, allowing direct engagement to seize control. Mastery requires monopolizing a high-leverage point.

Distributed: The scenario’s key elements are dispersed across disconnected nodes, necessitating asymmetric engagement. Mastery requires parallel access to subsystems for isolated interventions.

Layout: The architecture of critical elements within the scenario (centralized or fragmented). Establishes whether control requires holding one throne or ten battlegrounds.

Reactive: The scenario provides minimal prior notice, requiring immediate adaptation to emergent threats. Mastery depends on instinctive adjustments with limited foresight.

Proactive: The scenario allows deliberate preparation, enabling preemptive structuring of defenses/resources. Mastery depends on preemptive planning to shape outcomes.

Prep Window: The advance time granted to prepare for scenario challenges (reaction vs. preparation). Quantifies whether characters must stitch wounds mid-fall or build a net beforehand.

Multi Agent: The degree of difficulty involved in managing scenarios with multiple independent actors, each with their own distinct goals. It measures how hard it is to coordinate with or outmaneuver many parties at once.

Scenario: The ability to analyze, adapt, and execute across varied operational frameworks through assessment across seven spectrum dimensions: Baseline, Dimensionality, Approach, Engagement, Reach, Layout, and Prep Window.

Field Dynamic: A character's innate or cultivated advantage in specific domains of knowledge, expertise, or environments determining how their specialized mastery amplifies or diminishes their effectiveness against opponents in contextually relevant scenarios.

Tempo Control: The degree to which a character dictates the rhythm, timing, and initiative of the engagement, forcing the opponent into reactive patterns rather than proactive execution.

Nash Equilibrium: A stable state in a competitive scenario where no participant can gain an advantage by changing their strategy alone. All parties are making the best possible choice given the actions of others.

Information Asymmetry: The disparity between what each competitor knows about the other’s capabilities, resources, and intentions. It dictates who operates with hidden knowledge and who is forced to act under uncertainty.

Methodological Dynamic: The alignment or opposition of characters’ strategic methodologies, determining how their approaches amplify, neutralize, or exploit each other’s strengths and weaknesses in direct competition.

Psychological Dynamic: The compatibility or dissonance between characters’ psychological profiles, communication styles, and decision-making habits determining how effectively they can predict, manipulate, or destabilize one another in strategic interactions.

Matchup: The dynamic interplay between competing characters' intelligence traits, skills, and situational factors that determines their relative advantage in a confrontation or strategic engagement—accounting for cognitive styles, adaptability, resource utilization, and contextual pressures.

Unpredictability: The ability to think and behave in ways that are unexpected and difficult to predict by others through expectation violation, pattern disruption, creative generation, and strategic coherence.

Foresight: The ability to foresee future developments and prepare for them through pattern recognition, causal reasoning, scenario planning, and preparation.

Cognition: The overarching ability to acquire, process, interpret, synthesize, and apply knowledge, skills, and information through basic processes, executive functions, and metacognition.

Psychology: The in-depth understanding of the mind, behaviors, thoughts, feelings, and social interactions through cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social understanding.

Counteraction: The ability to identify, predict, and respond effectively to opponents through opponent identification, prediction, response planning, rapid execution, and adaptation.

It measures how a character's expertise profile matches the demands of different scenarios and opponents.

Mentality: The overarching cognitive and psychological framework that dictates how a character perceives things, processes, makes decisions, and interacts with the world.

Insight: This category assesses the quality, depth, and adaptability of reasoning through impasse recognition, restructuring, sudden comprehension, deep understanding, and adaptive application.

Strategy: The active implementation of multiple tactics systematically to work towards or achieve the underlying vision, defining the overall goal and method of achieving it.

Planning: A flexible blueprint that compiles multiple strategies and tactics centered around clear goals, involving predetermined steps with contingencies for foreseeable circumstances.

Resistance: The ability to resist, counteract, or push back against perceived threats, demands, or changes through mental fortitude, impulse control, inscrutability, autonomy, and clarity that infringe upon their autonomy, values, or sense of self.

Framework: A structured method to assess a character's demonstrated abilities through constraints, opposition, complexity, efficiency, consistency, flexibility demand, contextual adversity, and effectiveness, contextualizing their actions within measurable criteria.

Scenario Proficiency: The ability to analyze, adapt, and execute across varied operational frameworks through assessment across seven spectrum dimensions: Baseline, Dimensionality, Approach, Engagement, Reach, Layout, and Prep Window.

Matchup: The dynamic interplay between competing characters' intelligence traits, skills, and situational factors that determines their relative advantage in a confrontation or strategic engagement—accounting for cognitive styles, adaptability, resource utilization, and contextual pressures.
