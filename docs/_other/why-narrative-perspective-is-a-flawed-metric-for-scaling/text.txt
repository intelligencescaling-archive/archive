Why is using Narrative’s perspective a Fundamentally Flawed Metric for Scaling Fictional Intelligence?

Narrative's perspective is being defined as: evaluating intelligence based on in-verse logic, while considering the story as a separate reality.

Human's perspective: evaluating intelligence based on real world logic, while considering the story a product of humanity.

Problems with the narrative's perspective:

1- Direct problem: the very moment you compare characters from a fictional world to another, you are breaking the very premise of using the narrative's perspective, since by comparing two fictional worlds, you are implicitly stating that neither of those universes ever was a reality.  Comparing fictional works only makes sense from a human POV.

2- Proportionality problems: given the premise of “Direct problem” now you are also breaking not only your own perspective, but also the very logic of the universes you are comparing. For example: an “omnicient” being in Universe A, could not make sense in a Universe where (let's say) knowledge is inherently subjective, depending solely on the individual.

3- Causality problem: once you establish that you have to have a certain degree at least of Human POV to compare fictional worlds (point 1) you must also have the doubt that everyone of these fictional worlds, and even within them, every fictional moment, operates on different causality systems. Now we are going to prove it’s not only virtually impossible, but also logically contradictory to say that this causality problem isn't a thing (given the premise of Human Pov that the stories are written from human authors):

* we lack knowledge on how causality operates within our world
* we lack a good understanding on how intelligence works. We cannot even evaluate it decently in reality, let alone simulate it
* even IF we had the previously mentioned knowledge, the authors would have to be extraordinarily smart in order to figure out how to build a coherent and logical story
* authors don't even write stories that have a causality that works the same as within our universe. Meaning they are inventing a causality system, without even knowing how ours works in the first place
* EVEN IF they had all those previous points fixed, the amount of screentime and direct explanations that they would have to get us understand how the causality operates in their universe, would be immense
* we, as humans, already are familiar with “plot armor” which is just a case of different causality. If you agree that plot armor exists, you are already proving my point
* even then, the job of authors is mainly to build entertaining/meaningful/good selling stories, not coherent and logical causality systems. They are not even incentivized in the first place

4- contingency argument: the understanding of information presented in fictional stories is inherently dependent on how our real world operates. The only reason we can even understand or evaluate the actions in these stories is because we interpret them through the lens of real-world knowledge and logic.

For example, if a character says, “I discovered nuclear physics alone,” we immediately recognize this as an impressive intellectual feat — not because the story proved it step by step, but because we know from real life how complex nuclear physics is. The story relies on our prior understanding to *imply* intelligence, without demonstrating the actual process.

This reveals that fictional narratives are necessarily incomplete: they don’t (and often can’t) fully explain every action or thought process. Instead, they depend on our intuition and real-world assumptions to fill in the gaps. Therefore, any evaluation of intelligence is *already* being filtered through a human, external perspective, regardless of whether the intent is to stay “in-universe.”

5- double standards problem: the evaluation of information presented in fictional stories is inherently dependent on how our real world operates. For example, when evaluating feats that involve some thought process (whether explicitly explained or only implied), there are many cases where something appears valuable only when compared to real-life outcomes or expectations — yet at the same time, that same feat becomes highly questionable if judged by real-life probabilistic reasoning.

This contradiction intensifies the more improbable or vague the feat is, and the more extreme the results appear to be.

Example: If a character acts confidently after making an extremely unlikely prediction (unlikely by our reality’s standards), and it turns out to be right — audiences often treat it as an act of genius. But this "genius" is only impressive if you import real-world logic into the analysis (since the story often gives no detailed justification for the feat).

* If you deny that it’s genius, you are contradicting the logic the story uses to portray the character as intelligent (and implicitly applying real-world doubt).
* If you accept that it’s genius, you're applying real-world standards of success (despite the feat lacking a realistic foundation).

In short:

Whichever standard you choose — in-universe logic or real-world logic — the moment you switch between them to justify or deny intelligence, you fall into contradiction. That’s a double standard.

In conclusion: we should shift from the perspective of the narrative, to the human perspective, which is authorial.
