HYAKKAOU PRIVATE ACADEMY

In chapter 1 we are introduced to the main setting of the story, the Hyakkaou Private Academy, a prestigious educational institution that was established 122 years ago. Throughout its history, the academy has maintained a reputation for teaching students from influential families in the areas of politics and finance. This selective student body has contributed to the academy's unique environment, which is where the next generation of leaders, policymakers, and financial magnates are made. Being associated with students from powerful backgrounds adds significance to the Hyakkaou Private Academy, as the networking and connections formed within the institution can have big implications in the political and financial worlds.

Furthermore, Hyakkaou Private Academy has earned a reputation as one of the best educational facilities nationally. This recognition is a display to the high level facilities and curriculum offered by the institution. The academy's commitment to providing a top-tier education that values not only studies, but also alternative abilities such as tactics, reading an opponent, and the strength to grasp victory over others, attracts students from the most powerful families. Although Hyakkaou evaluates its students through alternative skills as mentioned before, it does also have normal classes and a normal curriculum. There are people who say they don't, which is false. Mary is in the school via a scholarship because of her very high grades in the subjects, and we can see that students are graded in a range of different subjects in the Test Battle Game in Kakegurui Twin.
Overall, Hyakkaou Private Academy is known for producing graduates who go on to become influential figures in politics and finance on a national scale.
[Influence + Knowledge]

MOMOBAMI CLAN NARRATIVE
The Momobami Clan, also known as The Hundred Devouring Families, is a conglomerate of 100 branch families, all of which are very wealthy and hold influence over the politics of the entire nation. This includes the Jabami family.
There are panels from the manga that show that the Momobami Clan does have an educational curriculum for their kids (kid Kirari and Ririka having classes in the clan). Their education should clearly be by far superior to the Hyakkaou Academy level as their members demonstrate intellectual superiority to the students of the Academy on multiple occasions and are treated as such by the narrative of the story. Even very capable graduates from Hyakkaou are less successful than members of the Momobami clan, and are viewed as simply a means for the clan to establish control over the nation. As explained in the previous section, Hyakkaou is one of the best educational facilities in the nation (if not the best), which is attended by powerful and rich families from across the world, and creates the greatest CEOs, politicians, leaders, businessmen, entrepreneurs, etc.
Also, additional factors such as Yumeko/Souko being capable of Dice Control skill at a young age - which requires massive cognitive prowess and understanding of complex physics concepts - are further indicators that the Clan does have a high level of education. Another factor is that Yumeko demonstrates (especially in the game against Yuriko) to possess in-depth knowledge about grammar (Japanese linguistics and kanji deconstruction) and also knowledge in history (Historical and cultural literacy. Knowledge about specific Noble Houses of ancient Japan, meaning of old names, important and minor figures, etc).
[VCI + Influence + Knowledge]

VOTE ROCK PAPER SCISSORS:
Rules
On her first day, Yumeko accepts the “friendly” bet against the popular and skilled gambler, Mary Saotome. Yumeko begins with a small bet to gauge the situation, and while she wins that round, she ends up losing the next round where she bets a huge sum. Yumeko alternates between small and large bets for the next few rounds to detect anything suspicious, and she manages to spot two telling patterns: 1) Whenever the bet was high, Mary would manage to win the round, whereas whenever the bet was low, Mary would lose the round. 2) Whenever the bet was high and they drew, Mary would play the same card again, but if the bet was low and they drew, she would switch to a different card.
With this, Yumeko concluded that Mary is indeed cheating. She further concluded that the method would function in such a way that when the bet was low, the cards in the ballot were fairly mixed (a roughly equal amount of each card), in contrast to when the bet was high, where Mary’s pawns would place a larger amount of a specific card. On the uneven rounds, Mary would receive a signal indicating what card her pawns would be putting in. This allows Mary to pick her card according to what counters the most common card, so due to the uneven distribution, it’s an almost surefire win for Mary overall. This explains why Mary only repeated the same card twice on the high bet rounds, as she knew which card would be statistically strongest, therefore had no reason to switch to a different card.
Furthermore, Yumeko estimated the number of pawns voting in Mary’s favour to be around 20 out of 30 students, which turned out to be highly accurate, as the true number was 21. She did this through extrapolating from the hands she received and their respective probabilities of appearing naturally, and then determining how many controlled votes would be needed in order to get closest to a surefire win.
[Inductive/Abductive Reasoning]
Next she had to figure out the details of the cheating methodology, so Yumeko observed the game to understand how exactly Mary was cheating.
In summary, Yumeko’s process was:
P1: Mary is cheating somehow, by manipulating a certain amount of votes.
P2: The cheating requires a real-time method of communicating which votes to cast.
P3: Mary isn’t signaling to the players.
P4: There aren't any other means of real-time communication being used (e.g. auditory cues).
->
C1: Therefore, another player (Player X) must be providing the real-time signals.
P4: Mary would want to make her cheating unnoticeable to me.
P5: To receive Player X’s signals, Mary and the cooperating players must be able to see Player X.
P6: The cooperating players are already naturally looking toward me because I am the gambler.
P7: The only position from which Player X can be seen while those players continue appearing to look naturally toward me is directly behind me.
->
C2: Therefore, Player X is positioned behind me.

[Deductive Reasoning + Convergent Thinking]
From here, Yumeko used her hand mirror to estimate Ryota’s (Player X’s) hand movement, allowing her to counteract Mary’s cheating to an extent. However, in order to feel the true thrill of the gamble, she chooses to engage in a bet where neither winning or losing is guaranteed. Part of ensuring that the outcome is uncertain includes revealing to Mary that she had figured out how she is cheating.
The final round arrived, and Yumeko offered cash in order to gamble with 10 million Yen, showing no fear or hesitation despite the amount of money at stake. Mary refused due to the outrageous amount, so Yumeko provoked her by repeating the same words Mary used against her in a previous round (asking about her being too scared to continue). This pressured Mary into accepting, as she was obligated to do so in order to maintain her pride and reputation in the class.
[Emotional Management/Engagement + Observation + Direct/Emotional Manipulation + Trap Evasion]
From here, Yumeko would get her 3 cards. If she gets scissors, she’d play them and likely win, if not, she’d play her papers first, and if she has only rock, she’d likely lose. Yumeko has turned the rigged game into a perfect gamble, which she is thrilled with. Yumeko manages to get scissors and plays them on the first chance, as she predicted Mary would spend her paper cards first because it gives her the highest chance of winning. As a result, Yumeko wins the game.
[Foresight]
When Ryota reveals the horrors of the Housepet System to Yumeko, she shows no fear and instead is excited to gamble with such high stakes, immediately asking him a rapid-fire series of several insightful questions about the system.
[Emotional Management + Divergent Thinking]

DOUBLE MEMORY:
Rules
For this game, Itsuki designed special decks of cards that produce a tiny mark on the back that differs from the regular deck of cards her family's toy company produces. The cards are temperature sensitive and with the heat induction circuit in her bag, she can keep the cards warm and retain the marks on the back, each of which correspond to another card. When placed, the cards take two to three minutes to cool down and for the marks to disappear. Since Itsuki had already memorised what the marks corresponded to beforehand, she could abuse this to match cards against her opponent.
In around 1 minute (considering the marks would go away in 2-3 minutes), Yumeko uncovered the cheating method, identified all the marks, and memorized 52 different mark patterns (since there were 104 cards but she only needed to memorize one mark for each pair) and then connected those marks to the contents of the flipped cards as they were being flipped in the first round (which also means she should have memorized not only the marks, but also the two pieces of information - rank and symbol - that each card contains).
Also, the marks had already gone away shortly after the beginning of the second round, therefore, Yumeko should have remembered the exact position of each individual card of the two decks (104 cards) even after the marks had gone away.
Total amount of information processed/memorized: 52 mark patterns + 104 card positions + 104 * 2 pieces of information (each card has a rank and suit, and Yumeko is stated by Itsuki to have matched each mark to the contents of each card) = 364 pieces of information in an estimated time of one minute. And Yumeko should be mentally manipulating all that information at once when turning the cards up, so she’s actively maintaining and performing a cognitive task with 364 pieces of information that are being held in her short-term memory.
[Observation/Perception + PSI + WMI + VSI]
After the end of the first round, where Yumeko loses, she once again convinces her opponent to play another match. She acts distraught and leads Itsuki to believe she is just desperate and wants to retrieve her money. This convinces Itsuki to play again, since it would be an easy win and more money from a desperate person. This is especially successful because she sees Itsuki as a confident and somewhat sadistic person, so she would definitely bite at the chance of kicking Yumeko whilst she’s down.
[Emotional Understanding + Acting Skills + Bluffing + Direct Manipulation]
After Yumeko wins the match via exploiting her memorisation of all the card details, Yumeko engages in psychological warfare against Itsuki, revealing that she knew about the trick all along. She leads Itsuki to believe that her parent's company is in danger due to making cards used for cheating. This, alongside Yumeko’s verbal harassment and terrifying display of gambling thrill, leads Itsuki to have a psychological breakdown.
[Direct/Emotional Manipulation]

LIFE OR DEATH:
Rules
Yumeko immediately observes that the pot that is being used in the game has a slightly flared base, so she concludes that the chance of the numbers in the middle being hit is higher.
[Observation]
Yumeko belittled Yuriko's play for giving Mary hope and then crushing it, calling her the worst kind of human possible, which almost cracks Yuriko’s calm facade. Yumeko had also realized that Yuriko is a prideful person, especially when it comes to her name (a Japanese traditional one). Therefore, she taunts her with insulting jokes, using different arrangements and meanings of the Kanji in her name to mock her intelligence. This makes Yuriko angry, and she decides she wants to destroy and bury Yumeko. For this reason, Yuriko agrees to Yumeko’s high bet of 40, and distributes her own chips equally across 8 different positions, including position 24.
[VCI + Application Thinking + Direct/Emotional Manipulation + Emotional Perception/Understanding]
At the last second of the dealer’s countdown, Yumeko places all 40 votes on position 24. She apologises to Yuriko for her mockery, and says that the only reason she did that was so that she would bet on 24. Yuriko and the dealer become panicked, and Yuriko asks why Yumeko would need to do that. Yumeko reveals that she had observed from the beginning that the croupier’s tiny moles were actually hand piercings, and notices that the girl who showed her the place when she arrived also had these piercings. With this, Yumeko concludes that one of the swords must have a polarized magnet that is attracted to the metal hand piercings, enabling the croupier to manipulate the position of the said sword by sliding their hands around the cup, such that it could land anywhere on the board.
Therefore, when the croupier places the cup, Yuriko can observe the position of the dealer’s piercings in relation to the cup, and use that in combination with pre-made agreements on what the rigged spaces will be to place her bets. This is why, in response to Yumeko’s provocation, Yuriko placed her bets in such a way that conceals which position she is aiming for, whilst also betting enough on the cheated spot to land Yumeko in heavy debt. However, because Yumeko had also been observing the croupier’s hand, she also knew that the croupier had attempted to get the sword in somewhere around the 24 range (e.g. 22-26). Since Yuriko had been baited into using the cheat, Yumeko was able to determine the exact number was 24, as that was the only one of Yuriko’s bets that was within that range. Therefore, Yumeko bet all of her chips on 24, her bet on the position massively outweighing Yuriko’s. With the countdown over, Yuriko has no time to adjust her bets, and since Yumeko publicly announced how the croupier was cheating, she can’t manipulate the sword, as all attention is on her. This means that, if the croupier’s attempt was successful, Yuriko would be put in over 2 billion yen debt, invoking great fear in her.
[Observation/Perception + Abductive Reasoning + Foresight + Trap Evasion]
Yumeko also notes that the fact Yuriko hasn’t always won (she hasn’t bet even once on the right position for life this game) is because there is no 100% winning chance with this method, considering that other swords may get in the way or that it may already have a death sword at the desired number. She further infers that this is why Mary didn’t discover this cheating method, since the probabilities of winning with this method would be low if quantified, so it wasn’t a trick that would come to the mind of the opponent.
She also sees through Yuriko and notes that she was perfectly fine with the low chances since she is a student council member and has access to a lot of money, so it didn’t matter if she would lose sometimes as long as her chances were a little higher than her opponents, enabling Yuriko to make a slow but steady profit. This leads Yumeko to conclude that at the heart of the cheat lies not a trick, but Yuriko’s wealth. This is why Yumeko engineered a situation in which, if Yuriko loses, she would face such massive losses that she wouldn’t be able to recover financially, and would likely be removed from the Student Council, as this was the only way to get Yuriko to engage in a true gamble with stakes that mattered.
[Emotional Perception/Understanding + Inductive/Abductive Reasoning]
Yumeko ends up losing the game, and Yumeko supposes that this is due to Kirari’s interference. Yumeko recalls the building structure, immediately noting that if Kirari did intervene, she must have used the space beneath the Traditional Club to do such a thing. She then concludes that the sword would need to be dislodged, therefore, Kirari must have used a “badger”, an ancient trick where someone would move a dice by using a needle and a hole in the ground.  In this case, Kirari would need to move the sword using a strong magnet from beneath the floorboards to push it out of the slot. Although there is no hole in the floor, Yumeko speculates that Kirari had a crawl space built into the bottom of the room specifically for this gamble. Yumeko’s hypothesis is heavily suggested to be correct, due to how Kirari said (to the Student Council), just before Yuriko’s match, that she was going to toy with Yumeko.
[VSI + Abductive Reasoning + Application Thinking + Knowledge + Intuition]

He had failed to notice the possibility of this deception because it was derived from an unorthodox loophole in the rules, and their psychological warfare distracted him from examining the rules further. ESP GAME ARC

ESP GAME:
Rules
The game is played with Zener cards. The players have to guess the order of the cards chosen by the dealer, gaining one point for every correct card. It is played in two different rooms: a room where the dealer chooses the cards, and a second room where the players gamble. The rooms are soundproof and are only connected by cameras. A monitor in the dealer's room shows the players as they play the game, while the monitor in the other room only shows the dealer's hands and cards.
Pre-Game:
Not long after hearing Midari explain the ESP game, Yumeko had decided on her plan, and accepted the match. Yumeko’s goal is to complete the game without anyone getting shot, in other words, end the game in a tie. To achieve this, Yumeko said that she’d only accept on three conditions:
1. The game will last three rounds maximum.
2. The dealer is Ryota.
3. The loser pays the winner 1 billion yen.
Yumeko confidently gives these conditions despite Midari previously threatening them with a gun, because she had seen how badly Midari wanted to play the game with her, so she predicts that Midari would accept the conditions if it meant Yumeko would play4. Midari does accept these conditions, but by observing the conditions that were set, she suspects that Yumeko’s plan is this:
1. Make Ryota the dealer so he can cheat, allowing Yumeko to win all three games.
2. Intentionally miss her shots when she wins, causing no one to die.
Yumeko doesn’t deny Midari’s accusations, suspiciously saying “Who knows?”, convincing Midari that this was indeed Yumeko’s plan. In retaliation, Midari creates a rule that if you miss a shot, your opponent gets to shoot you instead. Believing she had countered Yumeko’s strategy, Midari lowered her guard and accepted the conditions. However, Yumeko had actually created the conditions in such a way that Midari would only notice this surface-level decoy strategy, luring her into a false sense of security and making Midari lower her guard once she prevented the decoy strategy from being executed. Yumeko had predicted Midari’s reasoning and knew what counter condition Midari would set, which is why Yumeko’s true plan actually operated within Midari’s extra condition, unbeknownst to Midari.
Also, condition 3, “The loser pays the winner 1 billion yen”, was mainly created to trick Midari into believing that Yumeko was willing to try to win the game if she was unable to make it a tie, since it would be pointless to set that rule if you weren’t open to winning. This was another deception by Yumeko though, as she never once considered winning the game. This condition was also the first of three traps Yumeko set to determine the answer to this important question:
‘Is Midari playing to win, or playing to lose?’
This question will be referred to as the key question.
After seeing Midari’s madness and desperation to play such a deadly game, Yumeko realised it was a possibility that Midari could be trying to lose, instead of win1. Yumeko created Condition 1 so she could limit the number of rounds and therefore tie the game, but depending on Midari’s motives, it would change the way Yumeko would need to play the game in order to cause a tie. So, she set multiple traps to reveal the answer as soon as possible. As stated earlier, the first trap was Condition 3, which she set at the very beginning. She observed Midari’s reaction to the condition, which was Midari saying, in a relaxed tone, “That’s fine. I don’t care about money. I don’t remember exactly, but I should have that much”. This shows that:
1. Midari doesn’t care about going into debt, not even bothering to check if she has enough to pay, indicating she doesn’t care about her own wellbeing2.
2. Midari immediately jumped to the conclusion of losing the money, rather than winning it, indicating she was prepared to lose the game2.
This gives Yumeko a hint that Midari may be aiming to lose, instead of win, but she needs further confirmation. So, before the game starts, she exploits Condition 2, which was that Ryota was the dealer, and says to him that “I will win, no matter what. We’ve got something special, don’t we? If you take “that” and turn it into some kind of signal, I’ll definitely win.” Of course, this is a total bluff, but Midari doesn’t know that.
Yumeko intentionally tells Ryota that she will “definitely win”, because it will indicate to Ryota alone that her claim was a lie, because Yumeko had previously told Ryota she hates matches where she’s guaranteed to win4. This is important because it helps Ryota realise that she is just bluffing, so he can go along with it and give a random signal over the camera, convincing Midari that they have a signal. So, Yumeko logically deduced that setting Ryota as dealer and bluffing about a definite win will signal Ryota to cooperate.
[Abductive1/Inductive2 Reasoning + Foresight4 + Indirect/Logical Manipulation + Misdirection + Fabrication + Concealment]
Round 1:
This is Yumeko’s second trap, because Midari’s response to this situation once again helps determine the answer to that key question. If Midari is playing to win, she would be forced to put 0 bullets in the gun until she can figure out the signal, because if they do have a signal, Midari would automatically lose and would be just increasing her odds of getting shot by putting in bullets.
Yumeko actually put in 0 bullets, so if this happened, it would force the first round to end in a tie, which would be good for Yumeko. However, if Midari still decided to load her gun, this indicates Midari isn’t even thinking about trying to win.
Midari puts in 6 bullets, so Yumeko is almost certain that Midari is trying to lose1, but she sets a third and final trap to confirm it. She secretly inserts lipstick into the barrel of the gun, so that when Midari matches more cards than Yumeko by chance, and aims the gun at Yumeko, she calmly tells Midari that she should not pull the trigger on the gun. She explains that she inserted lipstick into the barrel of the gun, which would cause the high pressure gas released by the gunpowder to have nowhere to go, causing the gun to misfire and inflicting a serious injury to Midari. She asks Midari to inspect the inside of the barrel as proof of the gun being jammed, and tells her that she inserted 3 bullets, so firing it would just be taking a 50% risk of blowing up her own hand for no reason. Of course, the idea that lipstick would cause the gun to misfire was just sophistry, though it was a half-truth based on how the mechanism of a gun actually works, making it sound like it was true.
Yumeko also never actually inserted any bullets. However, Midari didn’t know this and still pulled the trigger, which was enough proof to confirm that Midari was definitely trying to lose, since she was constantly doing things that were entirely self-destructive2. Since she had achieved her goal of answering that key question, Yumeko now had enough information to tie the game.
[Abductive1/Inductive2 Reasoning + Sophistry + Indirect/Logical Manipulation]
Round 2:
Part of the reason why Yumeko was so confident in this was because she had already seen through what Ryota’s thought process would be in the next round. DICE CONTROL FEAT:
Souko, Tsugiko and Yumeko use a special dice roll ability in chapters 100-104.5. This ability involves controlling the dice to land on the numbers you want when using a dice shaker.
However, to control the rolls of a non-loaded dice like this is virtually impossible. There have been many efforts made to try and control dice rolls, but they’re mostly inconclusive. In fact, a prominent study made in 2020 showed that a machine that was purpose-made for controlling dice throws was unable to achieve any meaningful results under a variety of conditions. Still, certain techniques are theorised to marginally increase the likelihood of rolling a certain number, however, in order to do this you must:
1.  Roll the dice by hand, not by dice shaker
2. Have a specialised environment and surface to roll the dice on
None of these apply to this situation, so controlling the dice using these techniques is impossible (it’d be impossible for these techniques to get such consistent results anyway). However, it’s definitely not down to luck or intuition, since it’s stated to be a skill. The fact that it is based on skill is further backed up by the fact that Souko fails to do it consistently once her focus is broken. It's also stated that there’s nothing supernatural about the technique, as Tsugiko mentions it’s not magic.
The manga also acknowledges its impossibility and that normal people wouldn’t be able to pull it off, so it’s not as if these things work differently in the Kakegurui universe.
So how would controlling the dice in this situation even be achieved? Well, according to chaos theory, the illusion of randomness in a dice roll is created from the fact that countless factors affect the roll, and even the tiniest change in any of them will have a butterfly effect that causes a major change in the results. This includes the exact force applied, shaking duration, shaking pattern, friction, air resistance, collision angles, etc. This makes it impossible for any normal human to control the outcome of a dice roll in a shaker cup, since dice are far too sensitive to changes in many different factors.
So, in order to control the results of the dice roll, then they’d need to constantly factor in all these aspects in real time, use countless calculations to predict and visualise the trajectory of the dice, determine whether it aligns with how they need the dice to be moving in order to get the desired end result, and adjust their shakes on a micro level to ensure it does align. What I’m describing here is a similar process an advanced physics engine would have to run to predict how the dice will land, but with the added step of having to manipulate the roll too. So this feat literally requires the mental modelling of a complex physics simulation in real time, hence insane CPI and VSI. And they can’t even see the dice while they’re doing it, so they’d have to be perceiving the specific information about the position, momentum, rotation, etc of the dice purely based on subtle vibrations and sounds alone, which is unrealistic levels of observation and perception. Oh, and Yumeko hadn’t even played dice before this, so she literally learnt how to do it on the spot from watching Tsugiko and Souko do it a few times at only 6 years old and then immediately implementing it just a few moments after witnessing it. Learning such a difficult skill so quickly is incredible learning ability, observation, perception, and inductive reasoning.
She also shows her dice roll control at other times, such as when she controlled the 10-sided die at Yumemi’s idol show, so we know it definitely wasn’t a fluke.
Rough Calculations:

Before we go through the process she would need to do in one timestep, we need to determine how long one timestep actually is, and therefore calculate the update rate. To accurately model the exact movements of the dice, including every factor, to the level of precision necessary to accurately predict the results, the update rate would have to be very high. The fastest and most chaotic part of the dice roll would be the collisions of the dice against each other or the shaker. Major changes can occur in the position and velocity of the dice in under a millisecond at these points, meaning the update rate would have to reach peaks of around 1000 Hz in order to track these changes accurately. However, since I’m making an underestimate here, and the dice spend a significant proportion of their time not colliding, the update rate can be lowered to a lower bound of 200 Hz, making the timestep 5 milliseconds. Now, here are the steps she would need to go through within one timestep:

Before calculations can be done, information must be purely gathered for a few moments in order to have something to base the calculations in step 1 on. This step happens once per complete dice roll and is not factored into the timestep.

1. Determine the current states of dice.

Position (3 axes) + sum of external forces + velocity (3 axes) + momentum + rotational position (3 axes) + angular displacement + angular velocity (3 axes) + energy loss ≈ 16 calculations per die
2 dice ≈ 32 calculations

2. Predict the trajectory of dice.
In order to know how exactly she needs to manipulate the dice, she needs to use the current state of the dice to predict how the states will change over time. This means doing the calculations in step 1 for each dice state in the future. First, we need to determine how far into the future she needs to predict. She needs to know where and how exactly the dice will collide with the walls of the container or each other beforehand to allow for adjustment. To predict an immediate bounce before it happens, perhaps she would need to predict around 50 ms ahead.

However, to perform larger physical adjustments in order to manoeuvre the dice towards a final result, predicting around 0.5 seconds ahead would be the bare minimum to allow for the time needed to move. Since one timestep is 5 ms, and 0.5 seconds is equivalent to 500 ms, that’s predicting 100 steps ahead per timestep. For every step, she’ll do the same number of calculations for step 1, so 16 x 100 = 1600 calcs per die.

3. Compare it to the desired trajectory.
She then must compare this prediction to what is needed to reach the desired results.

Essential values to be calculated:
Position deviation (3 axes), velocity deviation (3 axes), rotational position deviation (3 axes), angular velocity deviation (3 axes) ≈ 12 calculations per die.

FAQs:
Didn’t Yumeko cheat in the dice game?
Before playing the game against Tsugiko, Yumeko stuck some small grains of rice to the 6-face of both dice. This seems to have been an effort to load the dice to be more likely to land face down on 6, and therefore have the 1 facing up, by shifting the dice’s centre of mass. However, in order to have a noticeable effect on the results, a standard die would need to be loaded by about 4 grams. Grains of rice weigh around 0.03 grams, so over a hundred grains of rice would need to be stuck to the face of each die in order to have a noticeable effect, which is obviously not the case. It is also not strong enough as an adhesive to load the dice. It’s clear that Yumeko, who had never played dice before, just wanted a way to increase her odds of succeeding, even if only slightly, but due to how small the effect is, she would still need to do all the same calculations. Furthermore, the grains were removed by Tsugiko after the first two rolls and yet Yumeko was still able to control the dice. Similarly, Yumeko controlled the results of Yumemi’s 10-sided dice despite never having a chance to touch it before rolling it.

Why did Yumeko refer to the game as a gamble if she was controlling the dice?
It may seem like Yumeko mustn’t have been controlling the dice if she was talking about gambling and chance, but it’s important to remember that Yumeko couldn’t know for certain that she would be able to successfully control the dice. It was her first time playing dice, after all, and she just saw Souko - which is who she was learning dice control from - fail to control the dice after losing focus. There’s really no guarantee in her mind that she’ll succeed, no matter how flawlessly she calculates. Even after she succeeds twice, the grains of rice are then removed, so she’d be unsure if she’d succeed without the grains of rice.

Doesn’t this contradict Yumeko’s values, and therefore the narrative?
Not at all, this is pretty much explained by the answer to the previous question. Yumeko was taking what she would perceive as a risky gamble, betting on the extent of her own ability (which she has no idea, having never played dice), and the reliability of this skill (which she also has reason to doubt after watching Souko and Tsugiko fail while using it), while still putting her best effort into winning. One of Yumeko’s core values is that she wishes every party to try their best to win, while ensuring neither win nor loss is guaranteed. Some people mistakenly believe that Yumeko using her insane cognition to win is out of character, but this is not true. She demonstrates this behaviour in multiple gambles, like Double Memory and Tower of Doors. She also shows that she hates when people don’t try their best, like how she responds to Yumemi’s defeatist attitude in Active Station, and hates even more when people try to lose, shown in how she acts towards Midari compared with other cheaters. So this is very in character for Yumeko.

It is possible that each non-classroom facility is prescribed a “room number” of sorts, just like regular classrooms, and as such, within each building, the first 13 facilities are ranked from smallest to largest room number and assigned a number within the 1 to 13 range.
She inferred this correlation from the first explosion, as described earlier. From that information, she generalizes that card ranks map to particular rooms. In other words, card position tells us which building, and card number tells us which room within that building.
