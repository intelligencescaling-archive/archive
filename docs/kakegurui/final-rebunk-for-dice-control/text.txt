However, the main point of the debunk goes as such:

“Let's just say even with all the unknown micro factors she still has a 99% accuracy per calculation, that's pretty unrealistic but we will just use it as an example. It takes about 3 seconds to roll a dice in a cup which would make this 1956000 calculations. log10(P) = 1.956.000 * (-0,0043648) ≈ -1.956.000 * 0,0043648 ≈ -8.534”

This is an absolute abomination of an attempt to estimate the impact of error, it goes wrong multiple levels. Two incorrect assumptions are being made here:

1. All calculations are independent

2. Failing any of the calculations results in the total failure of the feat

Assumption 1 is made the moment he uses a simple compound probability calculation to try and determine the likelihood of success. It’s wrong to assume this, because I quite clearly laid out in the original doc that the calculations involve predicting trajectories, comparing actual trajectory to intended trajectory in real time, and calculating necessary adjustments to put it back on course, all centred around the same underlying physics. It’s easy to miss how dumb his method of figuring out the likelihood of success is, since it’s just a bunch of numbers to someone who doesn’t understand the full process, so let me give an analogy.

In Cris’ method, each calculation is treated as an independent probability, so like a spin of the wheel for instance. The accuracy is set to be a fixed amount like 99%, so that’d mean a 99/100 chance of success for each spin, hence on the wheel there would be 99 green spaces and 1 red space, all equal size. For every calculation, you spin the wheel. So he’s likening dice control to the process of spinning a 99% success chance wheel almost 2 million times and praying it doesn’t land on the one red space. This entirely ignores the process that Yumeko is actually doing and undermines the control she has, since she is predicting the trajectory of dice and making constant comparisons and adjustments to keep them on course. A more accurate analogy would be like trying to steer a bike down a lane, with every calculation being a tiny movement with an accuracy of 99%. The movements aren’t completely independent from each other, cuz you’re reacting to your previous movements and ensuring you stay on course. If you make one tiny error in your movements you don’t automatically crash, because your movements are INTERdependent and reactive, so if you mess up a movement, your next movements will be centred around adjusting yourself to make sure you stay on track. And now you can see how wrong assumption 2 is.

My explanation has always accounted for small errors in each calculation. That’s exactly why this section of the original doc entails recalculating the 100 states of the future every timestep instead of just reusing the old ones:

“She needs to know where and how exactly the dice will collide with the walls of the container or each other beforehand to allow for adjustment. To predict an immediate bounce before it happens, perhaps she would need to predict around 50 ms ahead.
However, to perform larger physical adjustments in order to manoeuvre the dice towards a final result, predicting around 0.5 seconds ahead would be the bare minimum to allow for the time needed to move. Since one timestep is 5 ms, and 0.5 seconds is equivalent to 500 ms, that’s predicting 100 steps ahead per timestep. For every step, she’ll do the same number of calculations for step 1, so 16 x 100 = 1600 calcs per die. 2 dice = 3200 calculations”

Notice how it says that she’s predicting 100 steps ahead PER TIMESTEP? That means she already has a prediction of the trajectory of the dice 100 time steps into the future, yet once the dice moves onto the next time step, she immediately calculates 100 steps ahead, which means she’s REcalculating 99 of the steps in order to correct errors. Ironically, I got asked by someone on my original Reddit post of my doc why she needs to recalculate the trajectory so much instead of just reusing it, and I explained that if she reused it, errors would compound, making it difficult to actually control the dice.
