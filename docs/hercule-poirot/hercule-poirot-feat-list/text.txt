\[Curtain Poirot's Last Case\] He murdered Stephen Norton and staged the scene to look like a suicide. Here was his entire plan: First, he pretended to be completely wheelchair-bound due to severe arthritis, even hiring a new valet to maintain and reinforce the deception. He then invited Norton to his room for hot chocolate and revealed his intention to execute him. Norton, in his arrogance, insisted on swapping cups—something Poirot had anticipated—so Poirot had drugged *both* cups with sleeping pills, knowing his higher tolerance would allow him to stay awake while Norton was rendered unconscious. Afterward, he carried Norton to Norton’s own room using the wheelchair. He disguised himself as Norton by removing his wig and false mustache, ruffling his gray hair, putting on Norton’s dressing gown, and adopting a limp. He then left Norton’s room and, posing as “Norton,” walked past Hastings, ensuring Hastings would later testify that Norton was alive after their supposed conversation. Once everyone was asleep, he returned to Norton’s room and shot the unconscious man in the center of the forehead with a Walther P38. He placed the pistol nearby and locked the room with a duplicate key he possessed—a key to a room he had previously moved out of, which Hastings mistakenly believed belonged to Poirot’s current room. Finally, he returned to his own room and deliberately stopped taking his amyl nitrite heart medication, allowing his heart condition to kill him. This ensured that if the crime were ever solved, he would not live to face justice.

\[*Murder on the Orient Express\]* He solved the *Murder of “Ratchett”* by deducing that all twelve suspects were involved in a collective revenge plot against Ratchett, who was actually Cassetti, the kidnapper from the Armstrong case. He began by examining “Ratchett’s” body and compartment and noted twelve stab wounds, an open window, a handkerchief with "H," a pipe cleaner, a flat match unlike Ratchett’s, and a charred paper reading “member little Daisy Armstrong.” He confirmed Cassetti was using a false identity because of the charred paper and because he had priorly spoken French to the conductor while posing as Ratchett, which Ratchett could not. He then interviewed the passengers and found inconsistencies: MacQueen knew about the Armstrong note, Hubbard falsely claimed a man entered her compartment, Schmidt saw someone in a Wagons-Lits uniform, Debenham lied about visiting America, Ohlsson reacted strongly to Daisy’s name, Arbuthnot remarked on Cassetti’s guilt, and Hardman admitted he was a private detective. Luggage inspection revealed further clues: Countess Andrenyi’s wet label and smudged passport, Schmidt’s uniform, the hidden red kimono, and Hubbard finding the murder weapon in her bag. Using these observations, he deduced that the passengers were all connected to the Armstrong family and had acted together to murder Cassetti as their own form of justice excluding Bouc and Dr Constantine. He then made up a simple stranger killed Cassetti theory for them to all use to get off the hook fooling the police.

\[The Murder of Roger Ackroyd\] He solved the murder of Roger Ackroyd by piecing together the crime through observation and questioning. He first noted the missing letter from Mrs Ferrars, the footprints leading in and out of the study, the moved chair, and the short window of opportunity after Dr Sheppard left. He then questioned the guests and staff, realised that Ralph Paton could not have been the murderer, and that many accounts of seeing Ackroyd alive were unreliable due to inconsistency. Using the evidence he priorly noted to deduce that Dr Sheppard himself was both Mrs Ferrars’s blackmailer and Ackroyd’s killer. Sheppard had staged the crime to make it appear as though an outsider committed it, using Ralph’s shoes to mislead the footprints, entering through the study window, setting a dictaphone to play Ackroyd’s voice, and manipulating the chair and door to create an alibi. From this he figures out Sheppard had committed the murder to hide his blackmail and that all the apparent clues had been deliberately arranged to mislead investigators.

\[Death on the Nile\] He solved the murder of Linnet Ridgeway Doyle by examining the crime scene, questioning passengers, and analyzing motives. He noted the gun found in Simon Doyle’s cabin, the scorched bullet hole, the lack of scorch marks on Linnet, the footprints near her room, the position of her body, and the missing pearl necklace. Witness statements revealed inconsistencies: several passengers gave conflicting accounts of where they were and what they saw, including suspicious behavior by Simon and Jacqueline Doyle, as well as others who appeared overly helpful or evasive. He also considered the timing of the murder and the opportunity each passenger had, including who had access to Linnet’s cabin. Using the evidence he had gathered, he deduced that Jacqueline Doyle had planned the murder with Simon in order to inherit Linnet’s fortune. The gun, planted clues, and falsified alibis were all part of their scheme. He revealed that Jacqueline had created a false appearance of innocence while manipulating Simon and the other passengers. The murder had been carefully staged to mislead everyone on the Nile cruise: Simon shot Linnet and staged the scene to make it appear as if Jacqueline was the killer, and then Jacqueline later killed Simon and herself. Their ultimate goal for the Inheritance was to make it look as though Jacqueline had been the sole perpetrator.
