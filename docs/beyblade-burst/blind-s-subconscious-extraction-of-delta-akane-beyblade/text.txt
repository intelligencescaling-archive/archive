Delta's narrative is shown on multiple occasions to draw comparisons between him and the Devil.

These comparisons are not limited to his personality, behavior, or the way other characters perceive him.

They extend to the symbolism surrounding his Bey, Diabolos, the imagery associated with his character, and the deeper implications of the relationship between a Blader and their Bey.

When all of these elements are examined together, a consistent pattern begins to emerge: Delta is repeatedly associated with the concept of Satan, or Lucifer, and the symbolism surrounding the Devil's fall from grace.

However, to properly understand the implications of this association, we first need to examine the symbolism behind Diabolos itself, the religious and mythological references connected to its design, and how these elements contribute to the characterization of Delta Akane.

After establishing this foundation, we can then explore the implications of Delta's conceptual identity for his cognitive abilities, particularly when considering his interactions with Blind, whose ability to perceive the darkness within Delta provides further evidence for the analysis I am about to present.

Delta's association with the Devil is established through multiple aspects of his character, with the most immediately apparent example being his Bey, Diabolos.

The name Diabolos itself is derived from the Greek word diábolos (διάβολος), a term associated with the Devil, an accuser, or a slanderer. It is also used in the Greek New Testament to refer to Satan.

The name of Delta's Bey already establishes a direct association with the Devil.

This is not simply a connection that needs to be inferred from an obscure detail in its design; the name itself carries the relevant meaning.

However, the symbolism becomes more interesting when we examine the other references associated with Delta and Diabolos.

Rather than relying exclusively on the name, the narrative repeatedly incorporates imagery associated with Satan, Lucifer, darkness, light, and the idea of obtaining greater power by consuming or surpassing others.

These details reinforce one another, creating a characterization that can be interpreted through the broader concept of the Devil.

One of the most important references to consider is Lucifer.

Although Lucifer is commonly treated as another name for Satan in later Christian tradition, the Latin word lucifer literally means "light-bringer" or "light-bearer." It is derived from lux, meaning "light," and ferre, meaning "to bring" or "to carry."

The word was also used in Latin to refer to the planet Venus, particularly when it appeared as the morning star.

Venus is exceptionally bright when viewed from Earth, making it one of the most conspicuous objects in the sky apart from the Sun and Moon.

Because it is frequently visible shortly before sunrise, it became associated with the morning star and, through its Latin name, with the concept of a celestial light-bringer.

This astronomical association is important because the imagery surrounding Lucifer's fall from Heaven developed into a powerful symbol of pride, ambition, and the loss of a formerly exalted position.

In later Christian interpretations, Lucifer is associated with an exceptionally glorious angel who fell from grace because of pride and the desire to exalt himself beyond his proper position.

Although the precise relationship between the biblical passages about the morning star and the later Satan tradition is more complicated than this popular account suggests, the symbolism itself is well established in religious and literary tradition.

The image of a brilliant celestial figure being cast down from Heaven is particularly relevant here.

Lucifer's light represents his former glory, his elevated status, and the extraordinary position he once occupied.

His fall, conversely, represents the consequences of his pride and his desire for greater power.

In this interpretation, the light that once distinguished Lucifer becomes inseparable from the story of his downfall.

The morning star, therefore, serves as a symbol of both extraordinary brilliance and the hubris that leads to a fall from grace.

This is significant when examining Delta because the symbolism surrounding Diabolos repeatedly returns to the concept of light.

The connection between Diabolos and Lucifer does not stop at the Devil's name or the imagery associated with celestial light.

Diabolos is also associated with the desire to consume the light of others in order to make itself shine more brightly.

This is a particularly important detail because it transforms light from a simple visual motif into something that represents power, superiority, and the ability to distinguish oneself from others.

Consider what this implies.

If light represents brilliance, glory, or a position of superiority, then the desire to devour another person's light suggests a willingness to obtain greater brilliance by taking it from someone else.

![img](5yb7q9mqv7wh1)

Rather than simply possessing its own light, Diabolos seeks to consume the light of others to increase its own radiance.

This creates an interesting parallel with the traditional characterization of Lucifer as a figure whose desire for greater power and exaltation ultimately leads to his downfall.

The important distinction is that the symbolism does not need to depict the two figures performing precisely the same actions.

Instead, the connection emerges through their shared conceptual themes: light, power, superiority, and the desire to rise above others.

Diabolos's desire to consume light can, therefore, be interpreted as an extension of the Luciferian symbolism surrounding Delta.

It presents the pursuit of greater power not merely as a desire to become stronger, but as a desire to elevate oneself by surpassing or consuming what others possess.

When this is considered alongside Diabolos's name, the association becomes considerably more compelling and further reinforces the narrative's recurring symbolism.

We have a Bey explicitly named after the Devil, a character repeatedly connected to satanic imagery, and a motif involving the consumption of light to increase one's own brilliance.

Taken together, these details establish a consistent thematic relationship between Delta and the traditional symbolism of Lucifer.

Another important detail is the name of Diabolos's primary frame: Venom.

At first glance, this may appear to be a relatively straightforward name intended to communicate danger, hostility, or a destructive nature.

However, when examined alongside the religious symbolism already associated with Diabolos, the name introduces another relevant connection.

That connection is Samael.

Samael is a figure appearing in various Jewish religious and mystical traditions, with different texts attributing different roles and characteristics to him.

Depending on the source, he may be associated with accusation, temptation, destruction, death, or the angelic and demonic realms.

In some later traditions and interpretations, Samael is identified with or associated with Satan, although these figures are not treated as identical in every religious source.

One particularly relevant aspect of this association is the interpretation of Samael's name.

The name is traditionally explained in some sources as meaning "Venom of God" or "Poison of God," although its etymology and translation are not entirely settled.

This creates a notable connection between Diabolos's Venom frame and the symbolism associated with Samael.

Consider the accumulation of these references:

1. Diabolos directly invokes the Devil through its name.
2. Lucifer introduces the symbolism of the light-bringer, the morning star, and the fall from grace.
3. Venom introduces a possible connection to Samael, a figure associated with Satan and destructive or adversarial roles in certain traditions.
4. The consumption of light reinforces the broader themes of power, superiority, and the desire to increase one's own brilliance at the expense of others.

Each individual reference can be examined independently, but their combined significance lies in how they reinforce one another.

Rather than being limited to one isolated name, the symbolism surrounding Diabolos draws upon multiple concepts associated with the Devil and figures connected to him in various religious traditions.

It is worth distinguishing between an established etymological fact and an interpretive connection: the meaning of Diabolos provides a direct linguistic association with the Devil, while the Venom–Samael connection is a more indirect symbolic parallel.

Nevertheless, within the context of this analysis, the latter remains relevant because it contributes to the larger pattern of imagery surrounding Delta.

The point is not that every reference independently proves Delta is Satan, but that the references collectively reinforce the same conceptual association.

"Satan Himself Masquerades as an Angel of Light"

Now that the association between Delta and Lucifer has been established, we can discuss the implications of the biblical material connected to this figure.

The first passage I want to discuss comes from 2 Corinthians 11:14–15:

"And no wonder, for Satan himself masquerades as an angel of light."

The passage continues by comparing Satan's servants to servants of righteousness, emphasizing the contrast between outward appearances and the nature of the person or entity beneath them.

This verse is relevant to the interpretation of Lucifer because it explicitly connects Satan with the imagery of an angel of light.

That imagery is particularly appropriate given the symbolism already discussed.

Lucifer is associated with the light-bringer, the morning star, and the figure whose former glory becomes part of the story of his fall.

The passage from 2 Corinthians adds another dimension to this symbolism by presenting the appearance of light as something that can conceal a darker reality.

In other words, light is not necessarily a guarantee of goodness or righteousness.

Something can appear radiant, glorious, or virtuous while concealing a fundamentally different nature.

When interpreted alongside Delta's characterization, this creates a connection that leads into the next part of this analysis.

Diabolos's relationship with light is not simply about possessing brilliance.

It involves the desire to consume the light of others, making the symbolism more complicated than a straightforward association with goodness or purity.

The same light that represents glory can also become associated with deception, pride, and the pursuit of superiority. This is consistent with the broader Luciferian interpretation of Delta.

The next passage comes from Psalm 8:4–5, which states, in a common English translation:

"What is mankind that you are mindful of them, human beings that you care for them? You have made them a little lower than the angels and crowned them with glory and honor."

The precise wording differs between Bible translations, with some translations rendering the relevant term as "angels" and others as "heavenly beings."

Regardless of the translation, the passage establishes a comparison between humanity and beings occupying a higher or more exalted position in the heavenly order.

The distinction is important because it provides the basis for the next part.

If angels occupy a position above humanity within the relevant religious framework, then they belong to a higher order of existence.

Since Delta is interpreted as embodying Lucifer, and Lucifer is understood through the tradition that identifies him as a fallen angel, this provides a basis for interpreting Delta's characterization as transcending ordinary humanity.
