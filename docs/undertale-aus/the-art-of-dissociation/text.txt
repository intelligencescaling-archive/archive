Phase 1: Project Genesis

During the 176th genocide run in a row, Chara attempts to convince Sans to kill everyone else alongside them, offering him the opportunity to experience something new.

However, this route ultimately ends the same way as the previous ones.

Sans refuses.

Despite everything he has experienced across the countless resets, he still refuses to become like Chara.

And this interaction reveals something important about his character: Sans wasn't always like this, and his smile wasn't always fake.

His behavior is not necessarily an indication that he was always emotionally detached or incapable of experiencing genuine happiness.

Rather, the repeated resets have gradually changed him, wearing down his mental state until he begins withdrawing from the world around him.

This distinction is important because it establishes that Sans's psychological deterioration is a process rather than an inherent characteristic that he naturally had beforehand.

He has been forced to experience the same events repeatedly, remember what happened, and watch everything return to the beginning, regardless of how much those experiences affect him.

So back when the first reset occurred, Sans attempted to rationalize Chara's actions.

He wanted to understand why they had done what they did.

There was presumably some explanation behind their behavior, some reason that could make the events understandable to him.

But as the resets continued, that initial attempt to rationalize the situation gradually deteriorated.

After experiencing the same cycle repeatedly, Sans began questioning whether he should tell someone what was happening.

* Should he tell someone about the resets?

* Should he try to explain what Chara was doing?

* Could anyone help him?

But those questions were met with the same problem every time.

They wouldn't remember at all.

No matter whom he told, the next reset would erase their memories of the previous timeline.

Sans would be left carrying the burden of those experiences alone, while everyone else returned to their ordinary lives without remembering what had happened.

Eventually, this resulted in the change of his attitude.

I don't care anymore.

This wasn't because the events had stopped affecting him.

Rather, It was because the repeated resets had affected him so severely that he had begun to lose the motivation to do anything about them.

He was trapped in a cycle where his experiences mattered to him, but apparently mattered to nobody else.

He could attempt to explain what was happening, but nobody would remember.

He could try to change the outcome, but another reset could undo it.

He could grow attached to someone, only to watch the relationship return to its original state that it was before the reset.

Over time, this would make it increasingly difficult for Sans to believe that anything he did could produce a lasting result.

This eventually led to Sans wanting one of two things to happen.

1. He wanted Chara to either continue.

2. Or he wanted them to stop.

Anything other than another reset.

Because at that point, the continuation of the timeline and its complete cessation would at least represent a definitive outcome.

Either something would finally change, or the cycle would finally end.

Another reset, however, would simply return him to the same situation.

The same knowledge that everyone else would forget what he had experienced.

This explains why Sans began closing himself off from the world around him.

By distancing himself emotionally, he could reduce the possibility of forming attachments that would only become another source of pain when the timeline reset.

If he stopped expecting anything from others, perhaps he would no longer be disappointed and the resets would hurt less.

And if he stopped allowing himself to become emotionally invested, perhaps he could endure the endless repetition without being continually wounded by it.

His detachment therefore becomes a defensive mechanism.

* Sans closes himself off because he wants to avoid experiencing the same emotional pain over and over again.

Yet this also creates an important contradiction: although he wants to avoid further suffering, he still wants something new to happen.

He doesn't necessarily want to remain trapped in the same emotional state forever.

He wants an experience that differs from everything he has already endured.

And Nightmare can exploit that desire.

So when Chara introduces something different, Sans finally begins experiencing something he has been deprived of throughout the resets: novelty.

The prospect of doing something new offers a temporary escape from the repetitive nature of his existence.

For someone who has experienced the same events over and over, novelty can become especially appealing because it breaks the pattern he had grown accustomed to.

In this situation, Sans begins to enjoy trying new things and experiencing different sensations.

He begins enjoying hurting chara, as well as the complicated sensations associated with hurting them and being affected by the consequences of his own actions.

This is significant because it demonstrates how far his psychological state has deteriorated.

The desire to experience something new has become entangled with the possibility of doing things he would previously have refused to do.

However, there is an important distinction here.

Wanting to experience something new does not automatically mean Sans is willing to abandon every principle he possesses.

That is precisely what makes his repeated refusals important.

Despite his deteriorating mental health, his isolation, and his growing desire for novelty, Sans continues to refuse Chara's proposal.

And, according to the story and information that is given to us, this refusal persists across the infinite timelines and possibilities in which Chara attempts to convince him.

As I've stated above in the context box, Nightmare is established as the root of all negative thoughts.

He influence's, creates, and manages the negative thoughts that emerge within a person's mind alongside the reasoning process that comes with said thoughts.

This naturally means Nightmare would have to be responsible for the negative thoughts driving his deteriorating psychological state.

The frustration caused by the resets, the growing emotional exhaustion, the desire to stop caring, and the longing to experience something different.

Nightmare would not merely need to implant these thoughts inside Sans's mind.

He would also need to make them appear to be Sans's own thoughts.

In other words, Nightmare would have to manipulate Sans internally while maintaining the illusion that Sans was independently arriving at these conclusions.

The reasoning would appear to belong to Sans himself.

And because Sans already has legitimate reasons to feel exhausted and emotionally detached, Nightmare could exploit those existing circumstances without making his influence obvious.

Nightmare builds upon Sans's genuine experiences while also steering his thoughts toward an outcome he would not otherwise accept beforehand.

The most important evidence in this—is the claim that Sans would always refuse Chara, regardless of how many timelines or possibilities they explored.

One AU alone contains an infinite amount of possibilities,

so that implies that attempting to persuade Sans through different approaches should, in principle, and logically, provide an enormous range of opportunities to find a successful method.

Chara could attempt to persuade him through friendship, curiosity, frustration, or the promise of new experiences.

Yet Sans will continue to say no.

Establishing a fundamental boundary in his characterization: even when Sans experiences negative thoughts and wants something new, he will not agree to killing his friends.

His psychological deterioration does not automatically erase this boundary.

This distinction between having a thought and acting upon it is essential in Nightmare's Manipulation right now.

Nightmare's influence may explain the negative thoughts Sans experiences, but the repeated refusals show that the negative thoughts that Sans feels alone are insufficient to make him agree to Chara's proposal.

His refusal remains consistent, even after the resets have taken a significant toll on his mental health.

This is because Sans's underlying code prevents him from crossing that particular boundary.

He may want to experience something new, but he will not kill his friends to accomplish it.

And that creates the obstacle Nightmare needs to overcome.

Because If Sans continues refusing Chara across all these attempts, Nightmare would need another way to make the desired outcome that he wants possible.

Instead of continuing to manipulate Sans (at the moment), he can exploit the person repeatedly trying to persuade him:

That person being the player.

After 176 unsuccessful attempts, the player has repeatedly encountered the same result.

Sans refuses.

Regardless of what they try, he continues to reject the proposal that they give him.

Eventually, the player begins to realize that ordinary persuasion is not going to work.

They want Sans to hurt his friends.

They want Sans to kill them.

They want him to say yes.

But he refuses to do so.

This creates a psychological opening for Nightmare that he himself set up.

The player's frustration grows because Sans continues to deny them the outcome they want.

The repeated failures can encourage increasingly desperate attempts to achieve their objective.

Rather than accepting Sans's refusal, the player begins looking for a way around it.

And so, exploiting the player's frustration, impatience, obsession, and dissatisfaction with the repeated outcomes.

He encourages the player to think that Sans's refusal is an obstacle that must be removed.

He makes the player increasingly focused on achieving the desired result, regardless of the consequences.

The player therefore considers an option that came from their negative thoughts that they previously would not have entertained:

Changing Sans himself so that he can finally say yes.

Rather than simply forcing Sans to agree, Nightmare manipulates the player into changing the conditions that prevent him from agreeing.

The player has already tried repeatedly to convince Sans.

They have already experienced the frustration of reaching the same outcome again and again.

Nightmare exploited that frustration until the player decided that persuasion was no longer enough.

If Sans will never agree under his existing conditions, then those conditions must be changed.

The player therefore modifies Sans's code, allowing him to respond differently and making the previously impossible answer possible.

Nightmare has successfully manipulated the player into doing what he wanted.

This explains the significance of the statement that Killer is here because of you.

The same general principle applies to other AU characters as well, with Dreamtale being the exception to this particular pattern. (Because it has no code to manipulate and doesn't follow the same rules as other AU's.)

This also connects directly to Killer's desire to experience something new.

Killer knows that he is different.

He knows that he is another AU version of Sans.

He is aware of his place within a larger collection of alternate universes rather than existing as the only possible version of himself.

This awareness contributes to his desire to experience something beyond the ordinary.

He wants to feel something new.

But the important question is where that desire ultimately leads.

Nightmare exploited these existing circumstances, guiding the negative thoughts surrounding them toward an outcome that Sans would otherwise continue to reject.

The player's intervention then changes the conditions under which Sans can act.

As a result, the desire to experience something new becomes part of the psychological foundation of Killer's identity.

Nightmare could have attempted different approaches to influencing Sans's thoughts.

But Nightmare's plan uses the player as the decisive variable—leading to the layers put behind this plan:

1. Nightmare exploits the repeated failure of persuasion itself.

2. Every refusal reinforces the player's frustration.

3. Every failed attempt makes the desired outcome seem further away.

And

4. the more determined the player becomes to obtain that outcome, the more tempting it becomes to alter the conditions preventing it.

5. Nightmare can therefore turn Sans's consistency against him.

6. Sans's refusal becomes the very thing that pushes the player toward changing his code.

Nightmare exploited the resulting frustration and directed it toward an intervention that allowed Killer to exist.

The method does not require Sans to independently abandon his mentality, but rather it relied on the player changing the conditions that maintained it.

The resulting chain can be represented as follows:

1. Repeated resets → psychological deterioration →

2.  desire for something new → continued refusal to kill his friends

3. player frustration → Nightmare's manipulation of the player →

4. modification of Sans's code → Killer's creation.

So when all these pieces are considered together, Phase 1 becomes the foundation for Phase 2.

Phase 2:  The Devil's Assistant

With Phase 1 now complete, Nightmare’s manipulation leads directly into the next stage of his plan.

Now that Sans has finally reached the point where he can say “yes” to Chara’s proposal, he becomes capable of joining them in the genocide of every monster in the Underground.

Chara has already given him the justification he needs to take that step: nobody will remember what happens after the reset, and he will have the opportunity to experience something new, to feel something different, and perhaps even to have fun.

And that is precisely what Nightmare wants.

By convincing Sans to participate in the genocide routes, Nightmare can push him further into a state of dissociation, gradually distancing him from his own actions, emotions, and sense of identity.

The more Sans participates in these killings, the easier it becomes for him to separate himself from the person committing them.

He can begin to treat the violence as something detached from who he is, even while he remains the one carrying it out.

However, the reason Sans finally agrees to Chara’s proposal is especially important.

He has given up on everyone.

As his dialogue says, Sans has reached a point where he no longer believes the people around him can understand what he has experienced.

They cannot remember the previous timelines, the resets, or the suffering he has endured throughout them.

From his perspective, every attempt to maintain a connection with these people becomes increasingly meaningless because the relationships he remembers are constantly being erased and restored without anyone else retaining the memories that gave them meaning.

He has spent countless timelines watching the same people live their lives, make the same decisions, and return to the same starting point.

Meanwhile, he is left carrying the accumulated memories of experiences that, to everyone else, never happened.

Eventually, his thoughts begin to reflect this hopelessness.

* They couldn't understand.

* They couldn't help him before.

* How could they help him now?

These thoughts represent and demonstrate how far Sans has withdrawn from the people around him.

The people he once cared about are no longer people he believes can help him, because, from his perspective, they cannot even recognize that he is suffering.

Consequently, when Chara offers him an opportunity to experience something new, Sans has less reason to resist than he once did.

If nobody remembers what happens, if every consequence can be erased, and if the people he cares about cannot understand what he is going through anyway, then what reason does he have to continue protecting the same world that has repeatedly left him alone with his memories?

This is where the previous genocide routes begin to reveal themselves through his actions.

Rather than being an entirely sudden change in behavior, Sans agreeing to participate in the killings is interpreted as the culmination of the psychological deterioration that occurred throughout the earlier resets.

His refusal to participate was one of the remaining boundaries separating him from the person Chara and The Player wanted him to become.

Now, after being worn down by repetition and encouraged by the promise of novelty, that boundary has finally been crossed.

And Nightmare has achieved exactly what he wanted: Sans is no longer simply experiencing negative thoughts. He is beginning to act upon them.

This leads directly into one of the most significant pieces for this next part: the first time Sans kills Papyrus during the genocide runs, specifically during his breakdown.

The dialogue in this moment reveals the resentment, jealousy, desperation, and emotional pain that Sans has carried for a long time through the resets.

Rather than presenting his actions as the result of a single emotion, his breakdown exposes several conflicting feelings that have accumulated within him.

He says:

"You do not know how much I hated you."

"You do not know how jealous I was of you."

"You don't remember."

"I get hurt, you are a failure brother."

"I needed you Papyrus, but you don't remember."

"You do not remember, and I get hurt for you."

"Why don't you remember?.."

"Why? Why? Why?"

"Apparently I do not need you."

These statements are important because they reveal that Sans's relationship with Papyrus is not as emotionally straightforward as it appears.

On one hand, Sans expresses hatred and jealousy toward his brother.

On the other hand, he admits that he needed Papyrus.

His resentment exists alongside an attachment that he has apparently been unable to abandon completely.

He is not expressing a desire to hurt someone he dislikes; he is actively confronting the painful contradiction of needing someone who, from his perspective, cannot remember the experiences that have damaged him.

The repeated emphasis on Papyrus not remembering is especially revealing.

Sans remembers what happened, but Papyrus does not.

That difference creates an emotional imbalance between them.

Sans has experienced countless timelines in which he watched Papyrus live, die, and become involved in events that were subsequently erased.

Yet whenever a reset occurs, Papyrus returns without the memories that would allow him to understand Sans's suffering.

To Papyrus, their relationship continues from whatever point the timeline establishes.

To Sans, however, that same relationship carries the accumulated weight of experiences Papyrus cannot even begin to recall.

This means that Sans is not angry because Papyrus fails to understand him.

He is angry because, in his eyes, Papyrus cannot understand him.

The memories that give Sans's pain its context are absent from Papyrus's mind, leaving Sans trapped in a cycle where his suffering remains real to him but effectively nonexistent to everyone else.

That is what makes the line "I needed you Papyrus, but you don't remember" so significant.

Beneath the resentment, Sans is admitting that he wanted his brother's support.

He needed someone who could recognize what he was going through, someone who could acknowledge that the repeated resets had affected him.

Yet Papyrus cannot provide that support because he has no memory of the events that caused Sans to need it in the first place.

And from Sans's perspective, that failure is devastating.

He needed Papyrus, but Papyrus could not remember why.

He was hurt by experiences that Papyrus could not remember sharing.

He wanted to be understood by someone who had no way of understanding him.

Consequently, the resentment grows until it begins to overshadow the attachment beneath it.

His jealousy and hatred become intertwined with his longing for his brother's support, creating an increasingly unstable emotional state.

This is precisely the kind of psychological vulnerability Nightmare can exploit—as he was the one to implant it inside of him.

Because as I've established plenty of times before, Nightmare is capable of influencing individuals by acting as an internal voice and making those thoughts feel as though they originate from the individual themselves.

Applied to Killer, this means Nightmare did not need to create every negative emotion from nothing.

Instead, he can take existing frustrations, insecurities, resentments, and unresolved emotional wounds and amplify them until they become increasingly difficult to resist.

Sans already has reasons to feel isolated. He already experiences the psychological consequences of remembering what others forget.

He already has a complicated relationship with his own suffering and the people he believes cannot understand it.

By acting as Sans's inner voice, Nightmare can reinforce his resentment toward Papyrus, deepen his jealousy, and repeatedly draw his attention back to the pain associated with the resets.

Thoughts that might otherwise remain fleeting or manageable can become increasingly persistent, intrusive, and emotionally overwhelming.

Instead of allowing Sans to process his conflicting feelings, this influence pushes him toward interpreting them in the most destructive possible way.

His need for support becomes another reason to feel betrayed.

And his inability to make Papyrus understand becomes further evidence, in his mind, that relying on his brother was pointless.

The result of this is a feedback loop in which Sans's existing pain fuels his negative thoughts, while Nightmare's influence makes those thoughts more intense and difficult to escape.

The more Sans dwells on what he has lost, the more material Nightmare has to work with.

And when Sans finally kills Papyrus, all of these conflicting emotions collide.

The act itself does demonstrate that Sans has become willing to kill.

But His subsequent breakdown reveals that killing Papyrus has affected him far more deeply than he might have anticipated.

Despite everything he has said about his brother, the act forces him to confront the emotional significance of what he has done.

He has killed someone he resented.

But also someone he needed.

That contradiction is what makes the moment so important.

If Sans had completely stopped caring about Papyrus, there would be little reason for the killing to provoke such an intense emotional response.

Instead, his breakdown shows that his attachment has not simply disappeared.

It has become entangled with his resentment, and the act of killing Papyrus forces those opposing feelings to surface together.

Nightmare's influence, as I explained, shows how Sans could reach the point of committing such an act while still being emotionally affected by it afterward.

He has been pushed toward doing something destructive, but that does not mean every part of him has accepted what he has done.

Another important detail is the contradiction in Sans's own mind following the killing of Papyrus.

He expresses that he does not want this anymore. Yet, at the same time, another part of his internal dialogue expresses the opposite sentiment: "Yes, this is what I want."

This is a sign of Sans actively attempting to dissociate himself from the situation.

On one side, he recognizes the emotional reality of what has happened.

He has killed Papyrus, and the act has affected him.

There is still a part of him that does not want to accept the violence, the consequences, or what the killing might mean about the person he has become.

On the other side, he has already participated in the genocide.

He has agreed to Chara's proposal, crossed a boundary he previously refused to cross, and entered a situation in which killing has become something he is willing to do.

The conflict between these two responses creates a psychological divide.

One part of Sans protests against what is happening, while another attempts to justify or embrace it.

Rather than allowing these conflicting feelings to coexist and confronting what they mean, he separates himself from the experience by leaning into the idea that this is what he wants.

In other words, the thought "This is what I want" functions as a means of distancing himself from the emotional consequences of his actions.

If he convinces himself that he wants to do this, then he does not have to confront the possibility that he is doing something he cannot emotionally accept.

He can attempt to turn a painful, conflicting experience into a choice he has supposedly made willingly.

However, that does not necessarily mean the conflict disappears.

His breakdown shows the opposite: the feelings he is trying to suppress continue to surface, forcing him to confront the emotional consequences despite his attempts to distance himself from them.

By reinforcing the thoughts that encourage Sans to continue while simultaneously exploiting the pain that makes him want to reject what he has done, Nightmare can push him further away from a stable understanding of his own feelings.

Sans becomes caught between the part of himself that recognizes the horror of his actions and the part that attempts to convince him that those actions are desirable.

The longer this conflict continues, the easier it becomes for him to detach himself from the present moment rather than process it—and the more LOVE he gains, the easier it becomes to distance himself as well.

This is where dissociation becomes central.

Sans is not necessarily becoming free of his emotions.

He is becoming increasingly disconnected from how he experiences and interprets them.

His contradictory thoughts allow him to move between rejecting his actions and justifying them, creating distance between himself and the reality of what he is doing.

Instead of fully confronting his guilt, grief, resentment, and attachment, he can attempt to bury those feelings beneath the belief that he wants what is happening.

Nightmare benefits from that separation because it makes Sans increasingly vulnerable to further manipulation.

It becomes even more significant when Chara reminds Sans that none of this ultimately matters because they can simply reset.

This statement provides Sans with another justification for distancing himself from the consequences of his actions.

1. If everything can be reset, then the killing can be treated as temporary.

2. If the timeline can be erased, then the suffering associated with it can supposedly be erased as well.

3. If nobody remembers what happened, then Sans can tell himself that the events do not need to carry the same weight they otherwise would.

However, this reasoning overlooks the central problem that has defined Sans's experiences from the beginning.

1. The others may forget.

2. But sans remembers, The reset does not erase his memories.

It does not undo the emotional experiences he has accumulated, nor does it eliminate the psychological effects of repeatedly living through events that everyone else forgets.

Consequently, Chara's promise is particularly damaging in the context of Sans's existing mental state.

1. It encourages him to treat the consequences of his actions as meaningless

2. while leaving him to retain the memories that make those consequences emotionally significant.

The promise of a reset gives him permission to continue, but it does not provide him with genuine relief from what he experiences.

Because Nightmare is acting as Killer's inner voice, he can amplify the reasoning that makes the reset seem like a justification for continuing the genocide.

The idea that nobody will remember can become more than a practical explanation for why the killings do not have lasting consequences for everyone else.

It can become a thought Sans uses to distance himself from the reality of what he is doing.

He can tell himself that he will finally be able to experience something new.

And, whenever the emotional consequences threaten to break through those justifications, Nightmare can reinforce the same reasoning again.

This creates another cycle of dissociation.

1. Sans participates in the killings, experiences the emotional consequences

2. and then relies on the promise of a reset to distance himself from those consequences.

Rather than resolving his distress, the justification allows him to continue acting in ways that may generate even more Dissociation.

1. The reset becomes an excuse to avoid confronting what he has done,

2. even though it cannot erase the memories he carries.

Furthermore, Chara's insistence that the events do not matter because they can reset directly reinforces the mindset that Nightmare has been cultivating.

* The more Sans accepts this reasoning, the easier it becomes for him to treat his actions as disconnected from his identity and emotional reality.

This is exactly the reaction that Nightmare wants.

He did this in order to establish a psychological state in which Sans can continue committing those acts while becoming increasingly detached from their significance.

1. The killings become easier to justify.

2. The emotional consequences become harder to process.

3. And the distinction between what Sans genuinely wants and what he has convinced himself he wants becomes increasingly blurred.

Phase 1 established the conditions that allowed Sans to become willing to say "yes."

Through the repeated resets, the deterioration of his emotional state, and the manipulation of both Sans and the player, Nightmare helped create the circumstances that led to the modification of Sans's code.

Phase 2 then explores what happens after that boundary has been crossed.

1. Sans begins participating in the genocide routes, believing that doing so will allow him to experience something new.

2. His accumulated resentment toward the people who cannot remember his suffering becomes increasingly prominent, while his relationship with Papyrus exposes the unresolved attachment and emotional pain beneath that resentment.

3. When Sans finally kills Papyrus, those conflicting emotions erupt. His breakdown reveals that, despite his willingness to commit the act, he has not simply become indifferent to its consequences.

4. Instead, he struggles between rejecting what he has done and convincing himself that it is what he wants.

5. Nightmare's influence, acting through Sans's inner voice, amplifies the negative thoughts that sustain this conflict.

6. Chara's promise that everything can be reset then provides another justification for continuing, encouraging Sans to distance himself even further from the emotional reality of his actions.

7. Nightmare has already succeeded in helping create the conditions under which Sans can participate in the genocide.

8. Now, he can use Sans's actions and their emotional consequences to push him further away from himself.

Each of the layers behind phase 1 and 2 makes his justification easier to believe and continue doing, while each new experience provides further material for his negative thoughts to feed upon.

And that leads directly into Phase 3.

Phase 3: The Souls Change

This is where Phase 3 of Nightmare's manipulation comes into play.

By this point, Sans has already been pushed through repeated resets, psychological deterioration, and the gradual breakdown of the emotional boundaries that once prevented him from agreeing to Chara's proposal.

Nightmare has spent the previous phases exploiting Sans's accumulated pain, reinforcing his negative thoughts, and encouraging him to view the people around him as incapable of understanding what he has endured.

Now, however, the consequences of that manipulation become much more apparent.

Sans has dissociated to the point where he is capable of killing everyone in the Underground, even before Chara has personally reached the point.

This is an important distinction because it demonstrates that Sans's behavior is no longer entirely dependent on Chara leading the way.

Previously, Chara's proposal served as the catalyst that allowed him to cross the boundary he had spent so long refusing to cross.

Now, Sans is capable of continuing the genocide independently, acting before Chara has even reached the point where they would ordinarily encounter these characters.

In other words, he is no longer simply following Chara's lead.

He is beginning to carry out the killings on his own.

This reflects how far Nightmare's manipulation has progressed.

By repeatedly reinforcing Sans's negative thoughts and encouraging him to distance himself from the emotional consequences of his actions, Nightmare has created a state in which Sans can continue killing despite the internal conflict those actions produce.

The person who once refused to kill his friends has now become someone capable of killing them without waiting for Chara to do so first.

And yet, this does not necessarily mean that Sans has stopped feeling anything altogether.

In fact, the dialogue reveals exactly how far his dissociation has progressed

One of the clearest indications of this change is Sans's behavior during the genocide route itself.

Despite the things he has done and the emotional turmoil he continues to experience, Sans is still capable of presenting himself with the same familiar demeanor.

He smiles, maintains an outward appearance resembling his usual self, and continues acting in a manner that does not immediately reveal the extent of his internal deterioration.

He even has a gift for Chara: the knife they use to carry out the killings.

This shows that Sans is no longer just tolerating Chara's actions or reluctantly participating in their plans.

He is actively contributing to the genocide, providing Chara with the very weapon they will use to continue killing.

Previously, Sans repeatedly refused Chara's attempts to persuade him to join them.

He maintained that boundary even when confronted with the possibility of experiencing something new.

Now, however, he is not only willing to participate in the killings but is also willing to facilitate them.

The gift demonstrates that the psychological distance between Sans and his actions has grown considerably.

He can perform an act that directly enables further violence while maintaining the outward appearance of someone who is still behaving normally.

But the most important part is that his outward demeanor does not accurately reflect his internal state.

Although Sans continues to smile and act like himself, his internal dialogue reveals that he is disgusted with his own behavior.

He recognizes that something about what he is doing is wrong, and he is disturbed by the fact that he continues to do it despite that awareness.

His actions and his internal reactions are moving in opposite directions: outwardly, he appears composed and even willing to participate, while inwardly, he is struggling with what he has become.

This contradiction is essential to understanding how far his dissociation has gotten.

Sans can recognize the horror of his actions without allowing that recognition to stop him from carrying them out.

His awareness has not disappeared.

Instead, there is an increasing separation between that awareness and his behavior.

He can acknowledge that he feels disgusted with himself while continuing to kill, as though the part of him that recognizes the wrongdoing is no longer capable of exerting the same influence over his actions.

The smile, therefore, is not evidence that he is genuinely enjoying everything he does.

It highlights the contrast between the person he presents to the outside world and the turmoil he is experiencing internally.
