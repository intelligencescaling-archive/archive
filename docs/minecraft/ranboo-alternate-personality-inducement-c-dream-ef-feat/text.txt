Now, to fully appreciate the depth of this EF, we first need to understand the psychology behind dissociative identity disorder. Dissociative identity disorder is a neurological disorder that impacts several parts of the human brain, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex. Under extreme pressure, stress, or fear, the amygdala can enter a defensive state, which may lead to the formation of an alternate identity as a defensive mechanism for the brain.

Trauma sufficient to cause this fracturing of identity also disrupts the hippocampus’s ability to integrate memories cohesively. As a result, individuals with DID often experience memory fragmentation or amnesia between identity states.

It is alo important to note that traits and the ability to retrieve memories are determined by the specific traumas and experiences that led to the formation of the identity. It is also worth noting that DID is more likely to manifest in younger children, as their brains have not fully developed. In contrast, adults have more complete brain development, meaning drastic shifts in major centers such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex are significantly harder to induce.
