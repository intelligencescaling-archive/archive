Introduction
        «The Idiot» («Идиот») is a novel by Fyodor Dostoevsky that was first published in serial form between 1868 and 1869. It tells the story of Prince Myshkin, a gentle and kind man who has recently returned to Russia after years spent in a sanatorium in Switzerland due to an illness. As he re-enters society, his innocence and good nature are tested as he becomes entangled in various dramas involving love, jealousy, and deceit.
        Dostoevsky was born in Moscow in 1821 and was a prominent Russian writer and philosopher. He led a tumultuous life, including several years in prison and exile in Siberia for alleged political activities. His works often explore themes such as morality, religion, and the human condition, with characters struggling against social pressures and their own inner demons.

Part I
Chapter I
Introduction (train station scene; novel introduction)
At the beginning of Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novel «The Idiot», we encounter a portrayal that reflects an atmosphere and environment permeated by fog and cold, which is symbolic of many aspects of the novel. The opening of the work introduces us to the atmosphere of a winter morning, describing the approach of the train to Petersburg in damp and foggy weather: «At the end of November, during a thaw, at nine o’clock in the morning, a train on the Petersburg-Warsaw railway line was approaching Petersburg. It was so damp and foggy that it was difficult to see anything out of the carriage windows ten paces away, to right and left». This fog can be viewed as a metaphor for the general incomprehension and uncertainty that pervade the world of the novel, as well as a symbolic reflection of the fog obscuring the view of the truth or true intentions of the characters.
The description of the passengers and their conditions is also significant. Returning from abroad and third-class passengers create a contrast that may indicate social differences and contradictions that will play an important role in the plot’s development. Tired faces and weary eyes emphasize the overall state of exhaustion and disorientation, which may foreshadow further psychological development of the characters (possibly symbolically indicating Prince Myshkin’s epilepsy, as well as social injustice, highlighted by the contrast between «people returning from abroad» and «petty and businesslike people»).
This introduction immerses us into the world of the novel, anticipating the complex relationships and internal conflicts that will unfold further. The fog and dampness create an atmosphere of mystery and uncertainty. Thus, these opening lines not only describe the physical environment but also anticipate the thematic and symbolic aspects of the novel, creating an atmosphere of mystery and intrigue that will unfold as the story progresses.
From the crack of dawn, two young men sit opposite each other, both lightly packed and modestly dressed. Dostoevsky masterfully uses their external descriptions to convey their inner states and characters.
The first of them is Parfyon Rogozhin. He is described as a man of small stature with curly black hair and fiery gray eyes. His face is characterized by a broad and flattened nose, high cheekbones, and thin lips that form a mocking and malicious smile. Notably, he has a high and well-formed forehead, which somewhat compensates for his ignoble facial features. A significant detail of his appearance is his deathly pallor, which, combined with his sturdy build, gives him a passionate and emaciated look that does not harmonize with his rude smile and self-satisfied gaze. Rogozhin is warmly dressed in a wide lambskin coat, allowing him to withstand the cold night.
The second character is Prince Myshkin. He is described as a young man also around twenty-six or twenty-seven years old, slightly above average height, with very fair hair, sunken cheeks, and an almost completely white beard. His eyes are large, blue, and intense, with a quiet but heavy gaze that can reveal the presence of epilepsy. The prince’s face is pleasant, thin, and dry, but colorless, now even blue from the cold. He is wearing a cloak with a hood, which turned out to be insufficiently warm for the Russian winter. In his hands, he holds a thin bundle made of old, faded fabric, hinting at his modest circumstances. On his feet are thick-soled shoes with gaiters, also marking him as a foreigner.
This contrast between Rogozhin and Myshkin highlights their different social and emotional states. The description of their appearance serves to create initial impressions of their characters and future interactions.
Rogozhin is brazen and self-satisfied, finding pleasure in others’ misfortunes, while Myshkin is quiet and suffering, with a heavy, intense gaze that makes him vulnerable and evokes sympathy. The interaction between these characters, starting with Rogozhin’s indelicate smirk, will become one of the novel’s central themes, exploring their contrasting traits and their impact on each other.
Dialogue (Myshkin, Rogozhin, Lebedev; Myshkin’s introduction; overall characterization)
The conversation begins with a simple exchange of phrases about the cold and Prince Myshkin’s stay abroad. The fair-haired young man, Prince Myshkin, shows remarkable readiness to answer the questions of his dark-skinned neighbor, Rogozhin, who immediately stands out with his caustic and mocking tone. We learn that Prince Myshkin had been treated for a strange nervous illness in Switzerland for a long time, but the treatment did not help. His fellow travelers quickly begin to discuss his plight, mockingly suggesting that all his luggage is limited to one small bundle, which turns out to be true.
The dialogue highlights Prince Myshkin’s naivety and simplicity, as he sincerely and without suspicion answers all the questions. At the same time, Parfen Rogozhin, a vivid and coarse character, reveals himself as a person in a state of intense emotional turmoil. He talks about his difficult situation and the recent death of his father, who left him a large inheritance that he has not yet received due to his family’s intrigues.
A significant moment is the revelation of Myshkin’s identity as a prince, which evokes mixed feelings of surprise and mockery from his interlocutors. This episode illustrates the difference in social status and perception between the characters, as well as outlines the main theme of the novel — the clash between Myshkin’s sincerity and purity and the cruelty and selfishness of the people around him (Lev Myshkin claims he is «the last of the Myshkins», which elicits laughter from his fellow travelers. There is a reference to Karamzin’s «History»: «In Karamzin’s «History», one can and should find references to the individual». Myshkin states he «doesn’t know where to stop», which also provokes laughter from Rogozhin and his lackeys. Myshkin further asserts that «due to my inherent illness, I don’t even know any women», and towards the end of the dialogue, having become interested in the prince, Rogozhin claims that «even people like you, God loves» (which alludes to religious parallels where Lev Myshkin is likened to Jesus and Rogozhin to Judas), with Lebedev agreeing, indicating his dependence and ability to flatter).
Rogozhin, in his story about meeting Nastasya Filippovna, reveals himself as a passionate and impulsive person. His story shows how strong feelings and inner struggle can influence a person’s actions and fate. His relationship with Nastasya Filippovna, among other things, plays an important role in the development of the plot and the further interactions between the characters.
Also, there is a third interlocutor, a pockmarked-face official, who plays the role of a know-it-all, trying to show his knowledge of all the circumstances and people mentioned in the conversation. This character adds a comic element to the scene and emphasizes the peculiar atmosphere of public transport, where people from different social strata are forced to interact closely.
Thus, in this scene, Dostoevsky masterfully portrays his characters, creating intrigue and preparing the reader for the complex entanglements of their fates. From the first glance, one can notice the contrasts between the characters, which will develop and deepen throughout the novel.
Lebedev
Lebedev is portrayed as a typical representative of his social class, a low-ranking bureaucrat with grand claims to knowledge and influence. He exhibits several key characteristics that make him an interesting character in this episode:
Know-it-all Image
Lebedev is depicted as a «know-it-all», a person who knows everything and everyone in his surroundings. His knowledge does not pertain to important or profound matters but rather to personal information about people, their connections, and conditions:
«They know everything, their restless curiosity and abilities are irresistibly drawn in one direction, of course due to the lack of more important life interests and views».
This knowledge is a source of self-respect and social status for Lebedev, despite his real poverty and insignificance:
«The people about whom they know all the ins and outs would certainly not have imagined what interests guide them, and yet many of them are positively consoled by this knowledge, which is akin to a whole science, achieving self-respect, and even the highest spiritual satisfaction».
Social Status and Ambitions
Lebedev is a typical minor official with low status, which is emphasized by his appearance and manners. He is poorly dressed and receives a modest salary, reflecting his low social standing:
«For the most part, these know-it-alls walk around with worn-out elbows and earn seventeen rubles a month in salary».
However, his ambitions and desire to appear important push him to interfere in conversations and show off his knowledge, allowing him to feel significant. For example, he actively joins the conversation about Pavlishchev and Epanchina to demonstrate his knowledge:
«— We know General Epanchin, in fact, because he is a public figure; and we also knew the late Mr. Pavlishchev, who supported you in Switzerland».
Parasitic Behavior
Lebedev demonstrates typical parasitic behavior, trying to gain benefits from his knowledge and connections. He is ready to ingratiate himself with wealthier and more influential people, such as Rogozhin, hoping to gain some advantage from it:
«And now a little more than a million has to be received at once, and at least, oh Lord! — the official exclaimed, throwing up his hands».
His willingness to humiliate himself and fawn for potential benefit is evident in his reaction to Rogozhin:
«Well, what does he need, tell me, please! — Rogozhin irritably and angrily nodded at him again, — I won’t give you a penny, even if you walk upside down in front of me. — And I will, and I will walk».
Manipulation and Flattery
Lebedev skillfully manipulates people using flattery and sycophancy. He knows what to say and how to behave to ingratiate himself with those above him in status. This is evident in his interaction with Rogozhin when he emphasizes his knowledge and willingness to please:
«Pah, damn it, he really knows. — Knows everything! Lebedev knows everything! Your Excellency, I also traveled with Alexashka Likhachev for two months, and also after the death of his parent, and I know all, that is, all the nooks and crannies, and without Lebedev, it came to the point that not a step».
Conclusion
Lebedev is presented as a character embodying certain traits and social phenomena in 19th-century Russian society. His pettiness, desire for self-elevation through the knowledge of trivial details about others’ lives, and readiness to humiliate himself before the powerful make him an interesting and typical character in Dostoevsky’s works. Lebedev not only highlights social stratification and moral degradation but also serves as an example of that part of society that lives in the shadows of great people, trying to catch even a small ray of their glory and wealth.
Rogozhin
Rogozhin appears as a vivid and complex character with pronounced contrasts in his behavior and emotional state:
Acerbity and Mockery
Rogozhin immediately stands out with his acerbic and mocking tone. This demonstrates his inclination towards sarcasm and possibly a cynical view of the world. His mockery might be an attempt to hide his own vulnerability and emotional turmoil.
Strong Emotional Agitation
Rogozhin’s inner state is characterized by strong emotional agitation. His emotions, triggered by the recent death of his father and the struggle for inheritance, come to the surface, giving his words and actions a special tension. This also indicates his impulsiveness and passion, which will play a significant role in his future actions.
Complicated Family Relationships
Rogozhin mentions the family intrigues related to the inheritance. This adds depth to his character, showing him as a person involved in family conflicts and the struggle for material values. Such a situation intensifies his inner contradictions and sense of injustice.
Openness in Conversations
Despite his acerbity, Rogozhin speaks quite openly about his personal problems and feelings. This might be a sign of his trust in his companions or simply a need to unburden himself and share his grief.
Attitude Towards Prince Myshkin
Rogozhin shows interest in Prince Myshkin, asking him questions and listening to his answers. His reaction to Myshkin’s openness and simplicity, possibly mixed with a sense of wonder and even envy, highlights the contrast between the two characters: one is naive and sincere, the other cynical and emotionally wounded.
Story about Nastasya Filippovna
The story about Nastasya Filippovna reveals Rogozhin as a passionate and impulsive person, ready to take risks for his feelings. His attitude towards her is a reflection of his internal struggle and readiness for extreme actions in the name of love.
Conclusion
Rogozhin appears as a multi-layered character with contradictory traits. His acerbity and mockery serve as a mask to hide deep emotional experiences, while his passion and impulsiveness foreshadow the dramatic development of events in the novel. This contrast between his outward behavior and inner state makes his character complex and intriguing for the reader.
Myshkin
Prince Myshkin is portrayed as a character with distinctive traits that set him apart from others:
Naivety and Simplicity
Myshkin’s naivety and simplicity are immediately apparent. He engages in a straightforward exchange of phrases about the cold and his time abroad without any hidden motives or pretenses. This contrasts sharply with the more cynical and complex nature of Rogozhin and other characters, highlighting Myshkin’s childlike innocence and lack of guile.
Openness and Sincerity
Myshkin displays remarkable openness and willingness to answer Rogozhin’s questions. His sincerity is evident in the way he talks about his illness and treatment in Switzerland. Unlike Rogozhin, who uses sarcasm as a defense mechanism, Myshkin speaks plainly and honestly, without any attempt to hide his vulnerabilities.
Social Vulnerability
The conversation quickly turns to Myshkin’s dire circumstances, with his companions mocking him for his meager possessions, which indeed consist of just a small bundle. This interaction underscores Myshkin’s social vulnerability and the precariousness of his situation, evoking a sense of pity and empathy from the reader.
Unassuming Demeanor
Despite being a prince, Myshkin does not exhibit any signs of pride or superiority. His unassuming demeanor and readiness to disclose personal details about his health and financial situation suggest a profound humility. This further differentiates him from the other characters who are more preoccupied with social status and material wealth.
Contrast with Rogozhin
Myshkin’s interaction with Rogozhin highlights the stark contrast between their characters. While Rogozhin is passionate, impulsive, and emotionally turbulent, Myshkin remains calm, composed, and gentle. This juxtaposition not only underscores Myshkin’s unique qualities but also sets the stage for the dynamic relationship that will develop between them.
Symbol of Purity and Ideal Person
Myshkin’s simplicity and sincerity make him a symbol of purity in a world filled with intrigue, cynicism, and selfishness. His reactions and behavior serve as a foil to the more complex and morally ambiguous characters around him, emphasizing his role as a moral compass in the narrative. Myshkin is contrasted with Rogozhin in this context. This refers to the religious parallel that will play an important role later on: Rogozhin is Judas (greedy for money), Myshkin is Jesus (forgiving all offenses). Myshkin is the antithesis of Rogozhin, and this is not only reduced to religious parallels but also to the social and everyday levels of the characters, which clearly correlates with the previous conclusion. Dualism is also emphasized: the material (Rogozhin) and the spiritual (Myshkin) (this is explored in Hegel’s works of the time). It is also clear from the first pages that Myshkin is an outsider in the society of these two people; he finds no place for himself in the dialogue (a logical conclusion based on the provided facts). This means Dostoevsky wants to show the impossibility of an ideal person existing in this imperfect world from the very beginning. Therefore, the first interaction of Prince Myshkin — the atmosphere of the described place (class, socio-political contrasts, the description of different people’s faces, which defines them), the fog (creating a certain atmosphere and unpredictability), the overall gloomy writing style, the train symbolizing arrival in a new place from the old, and the dialogue in which Myshkin is completely alien — sets an incredible intrigue: what will happen next with this «pure» person? It also fully reveals the beginning of the main dynamic and the central theme of the work, which is the impossibility of an ideal person existing in an imperfect world (an idea later explored by Camus and Nietzsche as well).
Initial Reception
The mixed feelings of surprise and mockery from his companions upon learning about Myshkin’s princely status illustrate the social prejudices and superficial judgments prevalent in the society depicted in the novel. This episode foreshadows the challenges Myshkin will face as he navigates a world that often fails to appreciate or understand his true worth.
Symbolism of the Surname «Myshkin»
Simplicity and Modesty: The surname «Myshkin» sounds simple and unpretentious. This aligns with the character of Prince Lev Nikolaevich Myshkin, who embodies modesty and humility. His surname immediately distinguishes him from other characters, many of whom have more grandiose and imposing names.
Associations with a Mouse: A mouse is traditionally associated with small, modest, and inconspicuous creatures. This association extends to Prince Myshkin (because «Myshkin» in Russian, it is a diminutive form of the word «mouse» transformed into a surname), who, despite his princely title, behaves very simply and even naively. He often finds himself in situations where his kindness and openness are perceived as weakness or oddity.
Parallel with Myshkin’s Behavior and Perception
Simplicity as Virtue and Vulnerability: Myshkin often behaves simply and sincerely, eliciting various reactions from other characters. Some see his spiritual greatness in this, while others perceive it as naivety and weakness. The surname «Myshkin» emphasizes his vulnerability and openness in a world dominated by more cunning and calculating individuals.
Misunderstanding and Condemnation: Like a mouse, Myshkin often finds himself in the position of a «secondary» and misunderstood figure. His altruism and lack of selfishness often provoke confusion and even contempt from others. The surname here acts as a metaphor, highlighting his insignificance in the eyes of society, despite his inner strength and moral superiority.
Conclusion
Prince Myshkin is characterized by his naivety, openness, and unassuming nature. His simplicity and sincerity stand in stark contrast to the cynicism and complexity of the other characters, making him a beacon of purity and humility. This portrayal sets the foundation for his role in the novel, where his interactions with others will reveal the profound impact of his innocence and moral integrity on those around him.
Conclusion
Rogozhin emerges as a complex and emotionally intense character, characterized by his sarcasm, cynicism, and deep inner turmoil. His sharp wit and sarcastic tone serve as a defense mechanism against his vulnerabilities and the tumultuous emotions stemming from his family dynamics and personal struggles. His impulsive nature and passionate disposition foreshadow the dramatic events that will unfold as the novel progresses, particularly in relation to his intense feelings for Nastasya Filippovna.
On the other hand, Prince Myshkin is portrayed as a character of profound simplicity, sincerity, and naivety. His openness in discussing personal matters such as his health and financial situation contrasts sharply with the guardedness and cynicism of those around him. Myshkin’s humility and lack of pretense make him a symbol of purity amidst a society marked by deceit and ambition. His status as a prince, despite his modest appearance and possessions, underscores his detachment from worldly concerns and his focus on moral principles and genuine human connection.
The interaction between Rogozhin and Myshkin in this excerpt serves to highlight not only their individual qualities but also the broader themes of the novel. The contrast between Rogozhin’s turbulent emotional world and Myshkin’s serene innocence sets the stage for the exploration of deeper philosophical questions about human nature, morality, and the search for meaning in life.
Overall, Dostoevsky skillfully portrays these characters as embodiments of conflicting forces within society: Rogozhin representing the complexities and darker aspects of human psyche, and Myshkin embodying an idealistic purity that challenges societal norms and expectations. Their interactions and evolving relationships throughout the novel promise to unravel profound insights into the human condition, making «The Idiot» a compelling exploration of morality, authenticity, and the pursuit of spiritual redemption.
Chapter II
Introduction (Epanchin’s introduction)
In the second chapter of Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novel «The Idiot», General Ivan Fyodorovich Epanchin is described. He lives in his own house on Liteynaya Street and has a substantial income from renting out several of his properties, including a large house on Sadovaya Street and a significant estate near St. Petersburg. Additionally, he is involved in various joint-stock companies, which bring him considerable profits. The General is regarded as a man with extensive connections and considerable influence.
Despite his origins as the child of soldiers, the General managed to earn respect and status in society. He is intelligent, shrewd, and skilled at presenting himself as a humble and simple man, for which he is valued by those around him. However, behind this outward simplicity lie personal ambitions and cunning.
Epanchin is married to a woman from the princely Myshkin family, which also contributes to his social standing. He has three adult daughters: Alexandra, Adelaida, and Aglaya, who are beautiful, intelligent, and well-educated. They do not seek arrogance but are proud of their heritage and status in society.
Dostoevsky then describes another encounter of the ideal man with the imperfect world.
Dialogue (Myshkin, Epanchin’s secretary; more of Myshkin’s profiling)
The dialogue begins with General Epanchin’s secretary trying to find out who Lev Myshkin is and what his intentions are.
Myshkin is not shy about talking about himself, his illness, and the reasons for his arrival. This shows that Myshkin is not prone to deceit and deception; he is open and simple in his intentions.
Myshkin does not avoid questions about his dire situation; on the contrary, he openly talks about his poverty and lack of means: «And as for my appearance and the bundle, there is nothing surprising about it: at present, my circumstances are rather unfortunate». This emphasizes his humility and lack of vanity. He does not try to hide his shortcomings or present himself in a better light, which contrasts with the usual behavior of people in society.
The secretary initially treats Myshkin with suspicion and skepticism. His questions are full of distrust and criticism. However, gradually, as the conversation progresses, he begins to understand that he is dealing with an unusual person. His attitude changes, and he starts to show more respect and interest in Myshkin.
The dialogue between Myshkin and the secretary can be seen as a symbol of the confrontation between simplicity and sincerity on one side and suspicion and distrust on the other. Myshkin represents the ideal of human kindness and honesty, while the secretary embodies more common traits in society — skepticism and distrust.
It turns out that Lev is not the last of the Myshkins: «Oh, almost not for the matter at hand! That is, if you like, there is one matter, just to ask for advice, but mainly, I am here to introduce myself, because I am Prince Myshkin, and General Epanchina is also the last of the Myshkin princesses, and besides me and her, there are no more Myshkins».
An additional detail is added to Prince Myshkin’s image: «The prince stood up, hurriedly took off his cloak, and remained in a rather decent and neatly sewn, though already worn jacket. A steel chain ran across his vest, on which were Geneva silver watches».
Myshkin’s speech is simple and direct. He speaks slowly, considering every word, which shows his thoughtfulness and attentiveness to his interlocutor. He does not try to impress the secretary; his words are sincere and straightforward, making his image even more attractive.
What follows is one of the deepest monologues ever written in a dialogue in the history of literature and the entire fictional world as a whole.
Monologue (France guillotine; capital punishment)
Lev Myshkin’s monologue touches on the theme of capital punishment, which is an important motif in the novel. Myshkin describes his encounter with the guillotine in France and shares his thoughts on the moral aspect of this phenomenon. This theme is raised between the servant and Myshkin when the former asks about the prince’s stay in Switzerland and his arrival in the cultural capital, asking Myshkin, «Have you ever lived in Petersburg before?» Myshkin replies that he has only passed through and «nowadays there’s a lot of talk about the courts here». The servant is interested in the fairness of the tribunal abroad; Lev says that «we have no death penalty», and to the question: «And there they execute?» — he responds that he saw an execution in France by guillotine (he was taken there by Schneider — his doctor).
Myshkin emphasizes that even a strong and fearless person, condemned to death, experiences horror before the execution. He describes how the condemned man cried and was as white as a sheet, indicating Myshkin’s deep understanding of human emotions and suffering. This moment reveals his empathy and ability to sympathize with other people.
Myshkin criticizes the death penalty as a phenomenon. He stresses that the state’s killing of a person in response to their crime violates the moral principle «thou shalt not kill». Myshkin’s view reflects his humanistic ideals and deep sense of justice.
Myshkin considers whether the greater suffering is physical pain from torture or the psychological horror of inevitable death. He asserts that the greatest suffering lies in the awareness of impending death, especially in the final moments before execution: «As you lay your head under the knife and hear it sliding above you, those quarter-seconds are the most terrifying of all». Myshkin compares «bandit murder» and «murder by sentence», arguing that in the first case, the victim has hope for salvation, while the condemned man is doomed (indicating the complete power of the state over an individual). He also provides an example with a soldier: «Bring and place a soldier against a cannon in battle and shoot at him; he will still hope, but read this very soldier a sure sentence, and he will go mad or cry». These reflections highlight the depth of his philosophical views and his ability for abstract thinking (This topic will also be addressed later in the works of Albert Camus, especially in his essay «Reflections on the Guillotine». Like Myshkin, Camus deeply contemplated the moral and philosophical aspects of the death penalty. He criticized it as an inhumane and unjust measure that violates the principle of respect for human life. Camus argued that even the most heinous crimes do not justify state-sanctioned killing, as this action undermines the foundations of humanism and justice).
Myshkin’s monologue is filled with details and emotional nuances. He speaks quietly but with animation, showing his deep involvement in the topic under discussion. His speech style is simple but filled with sincerity and seriousness.
The description of the guillotine and the execution process serves as a symbol of the cruelty and heartlessness of the justice system. The contrast between Myshkin’s outward calm and his inner philosophical reflections emphasizes the complexity of his character and the multifaceted nature of his personality.
Lev Myshkin’s monologue in this chapter is a powerful philosophical statement on the nature of human suffering, the fear of death, and the moral aspects of the death penalty. His reflections reveal the depth of his character, his empathy, and his humanism, while also emphasizing his role in the novel as the moral and spiritual center. Truly one of the most characterizing, deep, and complexly philosophical monologues in the history of literature!
Conclusion
The chapter provides a deep insight into Prince Lev Myshkin’s character, highlighting his sincerity, humility, and philosophical depth. Through his interactions with General Epanchin’s secretary, we see Myshkin’s openness about his circumstances and his refusal to disguise his poverty or present himself falsely. This contrasts sharply with the usual societal behavior, emphasizing Myshkin’s unique moral and ethical standpoint.
The evolving dynamic between Myshkin and the secretary illustrates a broader theme: the conflict between innocence and suspicion. While initially met with skepticism, Myshkin’s honesty and simplicity gradually win over the secretary, reflecting the transformative power of genuine human goodness.
Myshkin’s monologue on capital punishment is a centerpiece of the chapter, revealing his deep empathy and humanistic ideals. His detailed and emotionally charged account of witnessing a guillotine execution in France underscores the inhumane nature of the death penalty. Myshkin’s reflections on the psychological torment experienced by those condemned to death highlight his profound understanding of human suffering and his unwavering belief in the sanctity of life.
This chapter serves to establish Myshkin not just as a character, but as a moral beacon within the narrative, embodying principles of compassion, honesty, and deep philosophical insight. His interactions and reflections set the stage for the central conflicts and themes of the novel, making this chapter a critical foundation for understanding Myshkin’s role and the broader societal critiques Dostoevsky aims to convey.
Chapter III
Introduction
Further characterization of Prince Lev Myshkin reveals that he is twenty-six years old and is proficient in calligraphy. This aspect of his character leads to a monologue closely related to the philosophical aesthetics and symbolism of the social classes of that time.
Monologue (scripts monologue)
Myshkin describes different scripts with incredible passion and attention to detail. This monologue reveals him as a person capable of seeing beauty in small things and understanding deep symbols hidden in seemingly simple details.
The Signature of Abbot Pafnutiy: Here, Myshkin admires the ancient style of signatures, noting the taste and effort that went into them. This shows his respect for traditions and history, as well as his deep knowledge of calligraphy.
The Large Round French Script: Moving to another script, Prince Myshkin demonstrates his ability to see the merits in different styles, highlighting the unique features of French writing.
The Russian Military Clerk Script: In describing this script, Myshkin sees the character and soul of the clerk, constrained by military discipline but expressing talent. This symbolizes the struggle between individuality and social structure.
The English Script and French Variation: Myshkin meticulously analyzes the differences between English and French styles of writing, showing his deep understanding of aesthetics and pursuit of perfection. His admiration for minor changes and flourishes reflects his ability to see beauty in details and nuances.
These descriptions of scripts symbolize the contrast between different social classes and their internal conflicts, where each style represents a particular era and social structure.
At the end of this chapter, Dostoevsky includes an interesting dialogue that adds depth to the main character.
Dialogue (prediction about Rogozhin)
In this dialogue, Myshkin’s ability for deep psychological analysis is revealed. His words about the woman they are discussing show his skill in seeing the inner essence of people.
«A remarkable face!» — Myshkin sees the woman’s inner struggle and suffering behind her outward beauty. He analyzes her features to understand her fate and character.
«This proud face, terribly proud, and I don’t know if she is good?» — Here, Myshkin expresses his doubt and hope that the woman is good, which underscores his desire for goodness and harmony.
Prediction about Rogozhin: The phrase «he would marry her, and a week later, he might kill her» reveals Myshkin’s insight and ability to predict possible tragic outcomes. This indicates his deep understanding of human psychology and internal conflicts, making his character more multifaceted and complex.
Conclusion
Myshkin is not just an observer; he possesses deep empathy and understanding of human nature. His ability to predict tragedies and see the hidden traits of people makes him not only intelligent but also very sensitive to the suffering and internal conflicts of others. These qualities make him a unique and complex character who, despite his outward simplicity and naivety, possesses profound wisdom and insight.
Chapter IV
Introduction
In the fourth chapter of Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novel «The Idiot», the reader is introduced to General Epanchin’s family. Dostoevsky provides detailed descriptions of the general’s three daughters and their mother, Lizaveta Prokofyevna.
Physical and Emotional Description of the daughters: The three Epanchin daughters are depicted as healthy, blooming, and robust women with strong physiques. Their strength and health are also expressed in their open enjoyment of food, which slightly worries their mother. However, despite their outward respectfulness, Lizaveta Prokofyevna’s opinion has lost its authority in the family. This illustrates how family dynamics change as children grow up and begin asserting their own opinions.
Character of Lizaveta Prokofyevna
General’s wife, Lizaveta Prokofyevna, is described as capricious and impatient, becoming more eccentric with age. However, she still has a submissive husband on whom she can vent her accumulated emotions, thereby restoring harmony in the family. This description highlights the tension in family relationships and underscores Lizaveta Prokofyevna’s role as the emotional center of the family.
Family Customs and Habits
Dostoevsky details the morning and dining habits of the Epanchin family, creating a vivid picture of their daily life and traditions. For example, their daily hearty breakfasts, almost resembling a lunch, and the habit of drinking coffee in bed precisely at ten o’clock in the morning underscore their bourgeois lifestyle and adherence to certain rituals.
General Epanchin
The general is portrayed as an experienced husband and a clever father who has chosen an atypical strategy regarding his daughters’ marriages, preferring not to rush them. His arguments are based on facts and common sense, highlighting his practicality and foresight. He has also managed to convince his wife of the correctness of this approach, demonstrating his influence in the family. However, the appearance of Totsky and his intention to marry one of the daughters disrupts this calm family life, creating conflict and intrigue.
Totsky’s Proposal
The appearance of Afanasy Ivanovich Totsky, who wants to marry one of the Epanchin daughters, becomes an important turning point. Totsky, a high-society man with significant connections and wealth, makes a very attractive proposal. Although Totsky does not consider the youngest daughter, Aglaya, as a bride, his proposal still causes significant upheaval in the family. The eldest daughter, Alexandra, is seen as the most suitable candidate, and her potential marriage to Totsky is viewed positively due to her kindness, intelligence, and ability to get along with people.
Hidden Tension and Uncertainty: Despite Totsky and General Epanchin avoiding formal steps, it is evident that the atmosphere is heating up. This is especially apparent through the discontent of General’s wife, Epanchina, indicating internal conflicts and dissonance.
The Barashkov Family’s Story
The story of the dire situation of Philip Barashkov, his tragic fate, and Totsky’s guardianship over his daughters, especially Nastasya, adds depth and context to Totsky’s character. It shows his generosity but also hints at possible hidden motives.
Transformation of Nastasya Filippovna
Nastasya Filippovna, raised in the best conditions thanks to Totsky, unexpectedly gains independence and a character markedly different from before. Her sudden appearance in St. Petersburg and bold behavior indicates profound internal changes and a strong sense of disdain for Totsky.
Determination and Unexpected Behavior
Totsky faces the unexpected determination and independence of Nastasya Filippovna, forcing him to reconsider his methods and approaches. He realizes that he is dealing with a new, strong personality who will stop at nothing to achieve her goals.
Totsky’s Position and Fears
Totsky feels his vulnerability and inability to handle the new Nastasya Filippovna, leading to his decision to yield to her and try to free himself from her influence by any means.
Nastasya Filippovna’s Attitude Towards Totsky
Nastasya Filippovna clearly despises Totsky and does not value her position or herself. This makes her particularly dangerous in Totsky’s eyes, as he fears her unpredictability and determination.
Nastasya Filippovna’s Life in St. Petersburg
In St. Petersburg, Nastasya Filippovna lives a secluded life, continuing her self-education and maintaining a limited circle of acquaintances. This underscores her independence and unwillingness to conform to societal expectations.
Totsky’s Consultation with General Epanchin
Totsky seeks help from General Epanchin, openly explaining his situation and his determination to gain freedom. The general also becomes involved in this situation because of his family and daughters.
Conclusion
This chapter provides a detailed description of General Epanchin’s family and their internal dynamics. Dostoevsky skillfully paints the portraits of the family members, emphasizing their characteristic traits and habits, while also laying the groundwork for future conflicts and events in the novel. He reveals the complex relationship between Totsky and Nastasya Filippovna, highlighting the latter’s transformation from a submissive girl into an independent and determined woman. Despite Totsky’s attempts to maintain control and influence, he faces Nastasya Filippovna’s unpredictability and inner strength, causing him fear and anxiety. Their relationship is characterized by deep conflict, with each side pursuing their own goals, and this confrontation sets the stage for the further development of events in the novel.

Asinus
        The Story of the Donkey and Its Significance
When Prince Myshkin talks about his trip to Switzerland, he mentions a donkey whose braying woke him from a painful state and made him pay attention to the surrounding nature. This episode has several layers of meaning:
Simplicity and Humility
The donkey symbolizes simplicity and humility, important traits that Prince Myshkin values and embodies himself. While many characters in the novel pursue material wealth and social status, the prince finds comfort and joy in simple things.
The donkey also represents patience and hard work, reflecting the prince’s moral and spiritual values.
Reaction of the Epanchin Family:
General Epanchina
General Epanchina, Elizaveta Prokofyevna, initially reacts to the prince’s story about the donkey with surprise and skepticism, highlighting her pragmatism and critical nature. She even jokingly remarks that some women might fall in love with a donkey, referencing mythological themes.
Epanchin’s Daughters
The Epanchin daughters laugh at this episode, showing their frivolity and immaturity, but also contrasting with the prince’s seriousness. Their reaction underscores the difference in worldviews and values between them and the prince.
Prince’s Emotional Awakening
Catalyst for Renewal
The donkey’s braying serves as a catalyst for the prince, awakening him from darkness and alienation, symbolizing the possibility of rebirth and renewal through contact with nature and simple joys of life.
Role of This Episode in Plot and Character Development
Character Revelation
This episode further reveals Prince Myshkin’s character as someone capable of seeing beauty and value in the most ordinary and simple things. His reaction to the donkey and subsequent affection for the animal show his spiritual depth and ability to find comfort and joy outside material values.
Contrast with Epanchins
The Epanchin family’s reaction to the prince’s story emphasizes their material and social ambitions, creating a contrast and conflict between their values and those of the prince. This interaction also lays the groundwork for future plotlines and relationships between characters, reflecting their inner conflicts and aspirations.
Thus, the episode with the donkey not only illustrates Prince Myshkin’s character but also helps reveal the dynamics of the Epanchin family and their attitude towards him, playing a crucial role in the development of the novel’s plot.
Tristitia et mors
The Prince’s Reflections on Time and Life
Prince Myshkin’s Experience in Switzerland
Prince Myshkin recounts his time in Switzerland, where he found peace and happiness. He describes his life there, where each day became increasingly precious to him.
Happiness and Peace
Prince Myshkin starts by mentioning that he found tranquility and began recovering in this village. Gradually, each day became special and brought him joy, highlighting his ability to find happiness in simple, everyday things.
Nature and silence became sources of peace for him. His nightly reflections under the sound of a waterfall and afternoon walks in the mountains, surrounded by pines and medieval castles, create an atmosphere of calm and contemplation.
The Prince’s Philosophy and Reaction to It
Philosophical Reflections
The prince talks about sometimes feeling restless and wanting to understand his destiny. This is evident in his quest to find the meaning of life beyond the mundane.
He imagines a big city where life is much more intense and noisier, symbolizing his desire for knowledge and experience.
Reaction of the Epanchin Daughters
Alexandra asks if he ever felt a pull to go somewhere, indicating her interest in travel and discoveries.
Aglaya sarcastically remarks that his thoughts on life in prison remind her of textbooks, highlighting her skepticism and ironic attitude toward his philosophy.
Adelaide calls him a philosopher and suggests he came to teach them, underscoring her view of him as a dreamy and somewhat naive person.
The Story of a Man Who Survived Execution
Prince Myshkin’s Encounter: Prince Myshkin tells of his meeting with a man who survived a death sentence. This story carries deep philosophical and emotional significance:
Time Before Execution
The man, who was read the sentence but later pardoned, lived through 20 minutes awaiting death. These minutes became the most important and longest of his life.
He planned how he would spend these last minutes: saying goodbye to friends, thinking about himself, and looking around. These actions emphasize his desire to use every remaining second.
Emotional Experiences
He remembered every detail of this time with extraordinary clarity. He was particularly struck by how much he could have done if given his life back.
The realization of the value of time and the ability to live each moment to the fullest became a revelation for him. However, when his life was spared, he couldn’t live as he had envisioned.
Abruptness and the Prince’s Philosophy
Interruption and Question
Prince Myshkin’s story is interrupted, and Aglaya asks about the purpose of his story. The prince explains that he wanted to show how important it is to cherish every minute.
Philosophical Conclusion
Alexandra concludes that five minutes can be more valuable than treasures, but living while counting every minute is impossible.
The prince agrees that it is difficult but maintains that the awareness of the value of time is still important.
The Prince’s Humility and Conviction
Aglaya’s Observation
Aglaya remarks that the prince thinks he can live better than others by appreciating the value of time. The prince admits that sometimes he feels this way, reflecting his humility and confidence in his philosophy.
This dialogue reveals his desire to live differently and his philosophical reflections on time and life, which others might not share, but which he considers important.
This passage helps to better understand Prince Myshkin’s philosophy and his striving to appreciate the value of every moment in life. At the same time, it shows the contrast between his views and the skepticism of those around him, highlighting the uniqueness of his character and his quest for spiritual and philosophical insights.
Pudor et idea
The Prince’s Inner Turmoil
The prince tries to understand if the women are offended by his words:
«Are you angry with me for something?» he suddenly asked, seemingly confused, but still looking everyone straight in the eye.
This phrase reveals his sensitivity and insecurity. He genuinely cares about how others perceive him.
Aglaya’s Sarcasm and Criticism
Aglaya, one of the young women, behaves sternly and critically towards the prince:
«If you say you were happy, then you lived not less, but more; so why do you squirm and apologize?»
Aglaya highlights the contradiction in the prince’s words. She questions his experience and his right to lecture others about life.
The Prince’s Defensive Reaction
The prince tries to explain his position and admit his insecurity:
«If you’re angry, don’t be», he said, «I know myself that I’ve lived less than others and understand life the least».
These words show that the prince is aware of his inexperience and tries not to come across as a mentor, even though he doesn’t always succeed.
Capital Punishment and Its Perception
An important topic raised in the conversation is the prince’s perception of capital punishment:
«It’s a pity, prince, that you haven’t seen an execution, I would have asked you something». «I have seen an execution», the prince replied.
Here, the prince shares his profound and traumatic experience of witnessing an execution. This event significantly influenced his worldview and left an indelible impression.
Aglaya’s Criticism and Discussion of Moral Aspects
Aglaya continues to criticize the prince, implying that he might not take his experiences seriously enough:
«I should have guessed! That tops it all. If you’ve seen it, how can you say that you’ve lived happily all this time?»
These words underline her skepticism and attempt to expose possible contradictions in the prince’s statements.
The Prince’s Desire to Share His Experience
The prince talks about his intention to share his impressions of the execution and the painting in Basel that struck him:
«I recently saw a painting in Basel. I really want to tell you about it. I’ll tell you someday — it impressed me greatly».
This shows that the prince strives to share his profound thoughts and experiences, despite criticism and misunderstanding.
Conclusion
This passage demonstrates the complexity and ambiguity of the prince’s character, his inner conflicts, and his desire to be understood. He wants to share his experiences and thoughts but faces distrust and criticism. The dialogue highlights his insecurity, quest for self-awareness, and desire to be accepted and understood by others.
Monologue (face before execution)
The Beginning of the Description: Immersion in Memories
The prince begins his monologue with full readiness, immediately forgetting about everything else:
«Just one minute before death», the prince began with full readiness, becoming engrossed in his memory and seemingly immediately forgetting about everything else»....
This moment shows how deeply and emotionally the prince is involved in his memories. He strives to convey the exact feeling he experienced while observing the execution.
The Psychological State of the Condemned
The prince describes in detail the psychological state of the condemned person when he learned about his impending execution:
«He didn’t believe it at first, began to argue that the paper would come out in a week, but when he fully woke up, he stopped arguing and fell silent…».
This fragment shows the shock and confusion of a person suddenly confronted with the inevitability of his fate. The transition from disbelief and argument to silent acceptance is important here.
The Path to the Scaffold: Thoughts and Feelings
The prince describes in detail the path of the condemned to the place of execution:
«I think that here too, it seems that there is still endless time to live while being driven…».
These words emphasize the illusion of time, where every moment seems long and filled with the anticipation of the inevitable. The prince emphasizes that the condemned perceives each street as yet another postponed moment of life.
Physical and Emotional Reactions
The prince describes the physical and emotional reactions of the person before the execution:
«As he climbed and stood on the scaffold, he suddenly turned as white as paper, completely like white writing paper».
Here we see the physiological manifestations of fear and horror: pallor, weakness in the legs, nausea. These descriptions help the reader feel the full horror of what is happening.
The Role of the Priest and Religious Aspects
The prince mentions the priest who accompanied the condemned:
«The priest, probably a smart man, stopped talking, and kept giving him the cross to kiss»...
The role of the priest at this moment is support and consolation, but the prince doubts that the condemned fully realized the religious meaning of what was happening. He kissed the cross eagerly, more as a last act of desperation.
Mental Activity in the Last Moment
The prince describes the intense mental activity of the condemned in the last moments of life:
«The head lives and works terribly, must be, strongly, strongly, strongly, like a machine in motion»...
This fragment emphasizes that even in the last moment, consciousness remains active. The prince imagines how the condemned fixates on the smallest details of the surrounding world, including the rusty button on the executioner’s clothing.
Inevitability and Realization
The climax of the monologue is the description of the moment when the condemned is already lying on the block:
«And suddenly he hears above him the slide of the iron! You will certainly hear this!»
The prince emphasizes that even in the last moment, the realization of death remains. He imagines how the person realizes the sound of the falling blade, adding to the horror of what is happening.
Conclusion: Proposal for a Painting
The prince concludes his monologue with a proposal for a painting:
«The cross and the head — that’s the picture, the face of the priest, the executioner, his two assistants, and several heads and eyes below, — all of this can be drawn as if on the third plan, in the mist, for accessories»...
Here he expresses his deep need to convey the tragedy and multilayeredness of the moment through art. He sees this as a way to communicate to others the full depth of human suffering.
Conclusion
The prince’s monologue is a powerful emotional description of the last moments of a person’s life before execution. It conveys the horrors experienced by the condemned, emphasizes the senselessness and cruelty of capital punishment, and the prince’s desire to communicate these experiences to others through art. This monologue reveals not only the tragedy of one person but also broader philosophical reflections on life, death, and human suffering.
A different kind of happiness
Silence and Reaction to the Prince’s Story
The prince falls silent after his emotional monologue:
«The prince fell silent and looked at everyone».
This silence underscores the importance of what was just said and creates a tense atmosphere of awaiting the listeners’ reactions.
Reflections and Comments by Alexandra
Alexandra speaks up:
«— This, of course, is not at all like quietism», Alexandra remarked to herself.
Quietism is a religious-mystical movement that advocates passivity and submission. Alexandra’s comment points to the prince’s active and deep involvement in his thoughts and feelings, which is the opposite of quietism.
Transition to Another Topic: Adelaida’s Request
Adelaida changes the topic of conversation:
«— Well, now tell us how you were in love», said Adelaida.
She tries to distract the prince from his gloomy reflections and shift his attention to a more personal and possibly lighter topic.

The prince reacts with surprise:
«The prince looked at her in surprise».
Adelaida continues to insist, showing her interest in the prince’s life:
«— You owe us the story about the painting in Basel, but now I want to hear about how you were in love»...
She emphasizes that talking about personal feelings makes the prince less of a philosopher and more of a human being.
Aglaya’s Remark and Others’ Reactions
Aglaya makes an unexpected remark:

The general’s wife defends the prince:

The prince responds:

Adelaida’s Persistence and the Prince’s Promise
Adelaida continues to insist:
«— Well, all right, — Adelaida hurriedly said again, — but if you are such an expert on faces, you must have been in love, so I guessed right».
The prince replies quietly and seriously that he has not been in love but has been happy in another way:
«— I have not been in love, — the prince answered just as quietly and seriously, — I... have been happy in another way».
This statement intrigues those around him, and they ask him to clarify how he was happy:
«— All right, I will tell you, — the prince said as if in deep thought».
In this final part of the chapter, Dostoevsky masterfully shows the interaction between the characters, their personalities, and relationships. Prince Myshkin appears as a person of deep feelings and reflections who is not afraid to talk about difficult topics but remains mysterious and intriguing. The reactions of those around him emphasize their interest and sympathy for the prince, as well as the differences in their perceptions and approaches to life.
Conclusion
In this chapter, Dostoevsky delves deeply into the complexities of human emotions, character interactions, and philosophical reflections through the dialogue of Prince Myshkin and the other characters.

3. Myshkin’s recounting of the execution scene, his musings on the condemned man’s psychological state, and the discussions about personal experiences all contribute to a deeper exploration of existential themes.

This chapter is a testament to Dostoevsky’s mastery in portraying the intricacies of human psychology and the depth of interpersonal relationships. Chapter VI
Introduction
The following monologue is one of the deepest in literature, touching on themes of happiness and the search for truth, human compassion, and trust. In this monologue, Myshkin fully reveals his parallel with Jesus, and the monologue itself and the situation described in it are biblical motifs.
Monologue (Marie and kids)
Prince Myshkin believes that one must be honest with children and hide nothing from them because they understand a lot and can give useful advice.
Prince Myshkin describes his relationships with children as ideal, full of trust and understanding. Adults, on the other hand, are perceived by him as incapable of truly understanding children, prone to hypocrisy and cruelty. This underscores the contrast between the purity and immediacy of childhood and the corruption and prejudice of adult life. Myshkin’s sincerity and openness won the trust of the children, despite their initial distrust and even hostility towards him.
Myshkin then recounts the tragic story of Marie and «dissent». Marie is a thin, twenty-year-old girl who has a sick mother she diligently cares for. But one day, Marie is seduced and raped, left on the road. With incredible willpower, she returns to the village where everyone begins to condemn her.
Marie symbolizes innocence suffering under the weight of societal prejudice and cruelty. Her fate shows how society’s cruelty and rejection can break a person. Episodes where children throw stones and dirt at Myshkin and Marie symbolize aggression and rejection towards those who are different or try to help the «other». This also reflects human tendencies towards cruelty and judgment.
Children in Myshkin’s story symbolize purity, innocence, and truth. Myshkin asserts that «one can say anything to a child» because children can understand and feel much more than adults usually credit them for. Myshkin draws a parallel with «birds», for «there is nothing better in the world than a bird».
The schoolteacher Thibaut and Prince Myshkin represent opposite approaches to education and interaction with children. Thibaut, who envies and slanders Myshkin, symbolizes traditional, rigid, and formal education, while Myshkin shows an alternative — upbringing through love and trust. The prince says that with Thibaut, they will not learn anything, but instead, «they will teach us». This refers to the moral side of human development. It is no secret that experiencing life’s hardships makes a person harsh, rough, and unrefined. Lev implies that children can teach adults compassion and kindness, as they have not yet faced the hardships of this world and have not lost their humanity. Here, a biblical parallel is drawn, as Jesus considered children the only truly worthy of the kingdom of heaven.
The tragic story of Marie shows that even the closest people can consider you an outcast of society, that one wrong action can overshadow all the good, even if you are not guilty at all. Marie is symbolized by a spider because most people fear spiders; they are a sign of an empty, unwanted house, just like Marie. Marie’s mother, who was dying and rejected her daughter because she «dishonored her», refused to forgive her until her death. But Marie continued to care for her mother, «washing her feet and looking after her» (a biblical parallel with Jesus and Judas).
After meeting Marie, Myshkin noticed that she blamed herself for what happened, showing how much society’s opinion influences a person: «... she considered herself to be the lowest creature».
After her mother’s death, Marie was left completely destitute, even children began to mock her: «...especially the children, the whole group of them — there were over forty schoolchildren», and no one would hire her. «She asked the shepherd to let her watch the cows, but the shepherd drove her away. Then she started going with the herd on her own all day. As she was of great help to the shepherd and he noticed this, he no longer drove her away and sometimes even gave her the leftovers from his lunch, cheese, and bread». The shepherd considered this to be «...a great favor on his part».
Also, after her mother’s death, the church pastor «...was not ashamed to publicly disgrace Marie», an action that symbolizes the hypocrisy of religious and moral norms of society, often used to justify cruelty and judgment. Dostoevsky shows that religion can also be used as a means of mass manipulation. The subsequent accusations by the pastor that Marie killed her mother, although a blatant lie, show the hypocritical desire to advance his career. Dostoevsky shows that the prejudiced identification of people associated with religion as good is a mistake, which can be summed up by the phrase «a wolf in sheep’s clothing». But after the pastor’s words, the children stood up for Marie because they began to love her. Myshkin then tells how the children came to this understanding of Marie.
Myshkin showed incredible kindness by selling a diamond pin, worth «...all forty francs» for only eight, to give the money to Marie. After this gift, he kissed her and said, «so she wouldn’t think I had any bad intentions, and that I kissed her not because I was in love with her, but because I felt very sorry for her and never considered her guilty, only unfortunate». At that moment, Marie did not understand what Myshkin was trying to convey — that she should not see herself as low before everyone. After Myshkin’s act of kindness, Marie kissed his hand, but when Myshkin wanted to do the same in return, she «...quickly pulled hers away». This shows that Marie had truly fallen into a bottomless pit of self-loathing, considering herself unworthy of any forgiveness and being a lower creature, all because she was wrongfully condemned by hypocritical people. Marie’s gesture of kissing Myshkin’s hand symbolizes deep gratitude and recognition of him as the only one who showed her human kindness and compassion. At the same moment, the children were spying on Myshkin and Marie. They began «whistling, clapping, and laughing, and Marie ran away». Myshkin tried to explain to the children that it was not what they thought, but they started throwing stones at him. This episode symbolizes aggression and rejection towards those who are different or try to help the «other». It also reflects human tendencies towards cruelty and judgment.
After this story, Marie was hated even more. They even wanted to punish her, but «...thank God, it passed». Children chased after Marie, throwing dirt at her, and Myshkin says that once he «rushed to fight them». After that, he started talking to them, «...talked every day whenever he could». Myshkin managed to convince the children of Marie’s misfortune. After that, everyone in the village began to love Marie and Myshkin, and the prince himself started learning to read to tell stories because people loved to listen to him. Most adults did not understand why the prince hid nothing from the children: «When everyone later accused me — Schneider too — of why I talked to them like adults and hid nothing from them, I replied that it was shameful to lie to them, that they already knew everything anyway, no matter how much you hide it, and would find out in a worse way, but not from me. It was only necessary for everyone to remember how they were children themselves. They did not agree»....
Then Myshkin returns to the moment when the pastor slandered Marie, at the very moment, as it already became clear, all the children were on Marie’s side because Myshkin «...kissed Marie two weeks before her mother died». Myshkin says that he explained the pastor’s action to the children, and some got so angry that they even «...broke windows with stones». Of course, the prince explained that this was a bad deed. This shows the prince’s understanding that responding to evil with evil is a terrible idea.
The elders in the village, having learned about this, began to accuse Myshkin, saying he «...spoiled the children», even forbidding the children to meet with Marie.
Then the story talks about the children’s positive imagination, as they came up with the idea that Myshkin «...was in love with Marie», although he only felt very sorry for her. The children did not understand why their teacher fell in love with such a poorly dressed girl, so they found her new clothes. Unfortunately, tuberculosis took its toll, and Marie was already very weak. In gratitude, she kissed the prince’s hand, and he did not pull it away because it was happiness for her. After a while, Marie died, and here again, a parallel is drawn between birds and Marie, as they knocked on her window with «wings» every morning and wished her a good morning, which is also a biblical parallel with the «messenger of happiness».
After Marie’s death, children were forbidden to meet with Myshkin, the main instigators being the teacher and the pastor. In the end, Schneider (Myshkin’s doctor) declared to Myshkin that he himself was still a child. This shows that Myshkin is as pure-hearted and simple as children, finding it «...hard to be among adults». Children truly brought happiness to Myshkin because he found himself in them.
After leaving, Myshkin realized that he was entering a new, imperfect world where there would be «people», showing that all the imperfection of the world lies on the shoulders of adults who have changed due to external influences and are no longer as pure-hearted as they once were. Myshkin does not mind if he is considered an idiot: «They consider me an idiot, but I am still smart, and they don’t even suspect».... The story of Myshkin’s departure shows how much the children loved him.
After his story, Myshkin begins to talk about faces. He provides a good analysis of each member of the Epanchin family but says nothing about Aglaya.
Conclusion
        In this chapter of «The Idiot», Fyodor Dostoevsky, through Prince Myshkin’s monologue, reveals many complex and profound themes, including the nature of happiness, the search for truth, human compassion, and sincerity. Myshkin, symbolizing Christ, demonstrates his belief in the sincerity and understanding of children, contrasting them with hypocritical and cruel adults. Through the story of Marie, an innocent girl suffering under the weight of social prejudice and cruelty, Myshkin shows the destructive influence of society on an individual.
This chapter emphasizes the importance of compassion and kindness, which Myshkin embodies in his relationships with children and Marie. He asserts that children can understand and feel more than adults typically assume, and they can teach adults true compassion and kindness. The story of Marie and her sufferings caused by social condemnation and cruelty serves as a powerful example of how societal norms and prejudices can break people. Myshkin, displaying incredible kindness and compassion towards Marie, underscores the importance of sincerity and humanity in relationships between people.
The contrast between Myshkin’s and Thibault’s approaches to raising children also reflects different views on education and interaction with children. Thibault’s traditional, harsh education is opposed to Myshkin’s loving and trusting approach, who believes that children can teach adults compassion and kindness. Through his kindness and openness, Myshkin wins the trust and love of children, despite their initial distrust and hostility.
The story of Marie also shows how society, using religion and moral norms, can justify cruelty and condemnation. The pastor, slandering Marie, symbolizes hypocrisy and cruelty hidden under the guise of religiosity and morality. In this way, Dostoevsky criticizes the prejudiced association of religious figures with good and righteous people.
At the end of the chapter, Myshkin emphasizes his belief in sincerity and humanity despite the cruelty and prejudices of society. He acknowledges that children, with their purity and innocence, bring him happiness and help him maintain his faith in kindness and humanity. This monologue by Myshkin serves as a powerful reminder of the importance of compassion, sincerity, and understanding in human relationships.

The existential question posed by Raskolnikov, «Am I a trembling creature or do I have the right?» perfectly describes what Ganya wanted to convey about uniqueness and ordinariness.

Her appearance makes a strong impression on those around her, especially on Prince Myshkin, who sees «perfection» in her. Nastasya Filippovna has a significant influence on those around her. Her emotional states (from gaiety to gloom) and behavior can change the atmosphere in the room. She evokes strong feelings in men, such as love, admiration, and even fear. Nastasya Filippovna often behaves mysteriously and unpredictably. Her past and inner world are shrouded in mystery, making her image even more alluring and complex. This mystical charm is part of her «eternal femininity». Despite her external strength, Nastasya Filippovna feels a deep inner suffering and torment. She seems to sacrifice herself to fate and surrounding circumstances, which is also an element of her «eternal femininity».
The other guests react differently to the prince’s presence and behavior. Ganya notes the strangeness of his actions, which elicits ridicule and general laughter among the guests. Ferdyshchenko, displaying his penchant for eccentricity, proposes that all the guests reveal their worst deeds, which is picked up as a strange but intriguing idea.
Nastasya Filippovna finds herself at the center of attention, displaying a contradictory nature. She simultaneously acts as the hostess of the evening and as an initiator of unconventional and provocative actions. Her suggestion to drink champagne and play «petit jeu» (a game where participants reveal their worst deeds) emphasizes her penchant for extravagance and desire to bring hidden aspects of the guests’ personalities to the surface. Her emotional swings between laughter and irritation enhance the sense of instability and inner tension. The idea of a game where participants must reveal their worst deeds raises the question of the boundary between sincerity and deceit. It is assumed that participants will be honest, but at the same time, skepticism arises about whether anyone will actually reveal the truth. Ganya says that everyone will inevitably lie in the game because confessing to bad deeds is too vulnerable. This underscores that deceit can serve as a means of protecting one’s reputation and inner self. Nastasya Filippovna herself provokes those around her to make honest confessions, although her motives may be ambiguous. Perhaps she aims to expose hypocrisy or make others feel their weakness and vulnerability. Characters, especially Ferdyshchenko, use irony and cynicism to hide their true feelings and intentions. This shows how deceit can be masked as a joke or sarcasm. The idea of revealing one’s worst deed implies acknowledgment of guilt and acceptance of responsibility, which in theory could cleanse the soul. However, in reality, as Totsky notes, such a game leads to a demonstration of boasting and hypocrisy.
Conclusion
Prince Myshkin, despite his nobility and sincerity, becomes the object of ridicule and suspicion. He tries to remain sincere, which makes him vulnerable to the more cynical and cunning participants of the evening. His choice to participate in the lottery for the game shows his willingness to follow the rules even in absurd situations, again highlighting his naivety and idealism.
Chapter XIV
Petit Jue
Ferdyshchenko begins with self-deprecation, claiming that he lacks wit and therefore babbles unnecessarily. This foreshadows his tendency toward rash actions and loquacity. He asserts that everyone has stolen something at least once in their life, which provokes outrage from Darya Alexeyevna. Notably, Ferdyshchenko says this to justify his own behavior.
Darya Alexeyevna is indignant and firmly declares that she has never stolen anything. This contrast highlights her morality and purity against Ferdyshchenko’s frivolous attitude. Prince Myshkin, blushing, agrees with Ferdyshchenko but believes he is exaggerating. This moment underscores the prince’s sincerity and naivety, as well as his desire to find the truth even in unpleasant matters. The General and Nastasya Filippovna express disgust at Ferdyshchenko’s story, pointing out his moral degradation and inability to grasp the baseness of his action.
Ferdyshchenko admits that the theft was a thoughtless and spontaneous act that he cannot explain. However, his joy and participation in accusing the maid show his hypocrisy and cynicism. In conclusion, he admits that he would not repeat the act, which indicates his acknowledgment of guilt but not repentance.
Ferdyshchenko’s story and the subsequent reactions of the characters illustrate themes of honesty, hypocrisy, and moral decay in society. Ferdyshchenko tries to justify his actions by asserting that all people are sinful, but his words only emphasize his own downfall. Nastasya Filippovna is particularly outraged by the story, highlighting her complex and contradictory nature, as well as the high moral standards she sets for others.
The scene underscores the tension and hidden conflicts between the characters. Nastasya Filippovna attempts to maintain the evening’s atmosphere, but her irritability and indignation increase, reflecting her inner turmoil and struggle. The General recounts an act he considers the worst of his life. He feels guilty for berating a dying woman, even though he realizes it was an accident. His remorse shows that despite his outward sternness and military bearing, he has a deep sense of morality and compassion. Outwardly, the General is a strict and harsh person, but his actions reveal that he has a soft and compassionate side. This creates a contrast, emphasizing the depth of his character. The story contains an element of irony: the General, seeking to condemn the widow for a petty theft, commits an act that leads to her death. This underscores the randomness and unpredictability of human actions and their consequences. The General’s position allows him to act authoritatively and rudely towards the poor old woman. His social status enables him to avoid immediate consequences for his actions, highlighting the inequality and injustice of the social hierarchy. By establishing an almshouse for old women, the General attempts to atone for his guilt. This shows his desire to rectify his mistakes and soothe the pain caused, adding elements of nobility and sincere regret to his character. The reactions of other characters, such as Ferdyshchenko’s sarcastic remark and Nastasya Filippovna’s casual comment, highlight the difference in perceptions of the General’s act. This adds to the overall picture a diversity of viewpoints and shows how differently the same events can be perceived.
Conclusion
Afanasiy Ivanovich tells a story, demonstrating his act as the worst in his life, and this becomes a catalyst for subsequent events. Afanasiy Ivanovich’s story about buying red camellias for Anfisa Alexeyevna reveals his desire to please and impress, even if it causes suffering to others. His action leads to tragic consequences for Petya Verkhovsky, highlighting themes of guilt and remorse.
Chapter XV
Introduction
The scene where Nastasya Filippovna asks Prince Myshkin to decide her fate reflects her inner conflict and search for genuine love and trust. Her sharp statement that she rejects Ganya Ivolgin’s proposal based on the prince’s response demonstrates her determination and independence. This scene also highlights her disdain for social and material bonds that tied her to Afanasiy Ivanovich and other characters.
Dialogue
Nastasya Filippovna, a central character, in conversation with Parfen Rogozhin and others present, demonstrates her deep inner crisis. She cynically notes that Rogozhin has brought the promised one hundred thousand rubles, emphasizing her disgust for the role men force her to play by offering money for her. This sum becomes a symbol of her sale, humiliation, and objectification.
Nastasya sarcastically and angrily describes her encounters with Ganya Ivolgin’s family and his attempt to introduce her into his family despite their obvious hatred and contempt for her. She also vividly describes her behavior and interactions with other men, including Totsky, who kept her as a mistress for five years.
Rogozhin silently observes the events, but his venomous smile and quiet remarks show his confidence that Ganya is willing to do anything for money. This scene underscores his contempt and dislike for Ganya.
Conclusion
The climax of the scene is Prince Myshkin’s proposal. In his naivety and purity, he is ready to marry Nastasya Filippovna despite her past and scandalous reputation. His words about love and willingness to die for her sound sincere and touching but provoke laughter and giggles from the others. Nevertheless, he asserts that he has received a letter from Moscow about a possible large inheritance, which could change their future.
Chapter XVI
Introduction
Prince Myshkin suddenly becomes a wealthy heir, drastically changing his social status. This underscores the theme of sudden changes in life and their impact on society’s perception of a person. The characters express various emotions: surprise, envy, joy, and embarrassment. This reflects how quickly society is ready to change its attitude towards a person depending on their material status. Prince Myshkin acts as a bearer of moral values when he promises not to judge Nastasya Filippovna for her past. This contrasts with the behavior of other characters, especially Rogozhin and Darya Alexeyevna.
Material and spiritual world
Rogozhin acts as an antithesis to Prince Myshkin. His suffering and anger contrast with the prince’s calmness and mercy. His offer to «back off» and willingness to pay for Nastasya Filippovna show his view of her as an object of possession rather than a person.

The changes in Nastasya Filippovna, specifically her erratic behavior, are a consequence of Myshkin’s presence. Understanding this helps to better grasp her dynamics with the prince.

Part I analysis
Alright, let’s now analyze what Dostoevsky aimed to convey to us, not narratively but idealistically.
Prince Myshkin as an Empirical Example of the «Plato’s Cave» and Virtue (ἀρετή, arete); The Seven Virtues;
Indeed, to understand the character of Prince Myshkin, one needs to have an understanding of Plato’s ideas, particularly his allegorical situation, the myth of the cave, as well as his investigations into virtue.
Plato considered virtue as a state of harmony of the soul, achieved through knowledge and wisdom. He identified four main virtues: wisdom, courage, temperance, and justice. These four mains, «cardinal virtues» were reflected in Christian moral theology. Ambrose of Milan borrowed them from Plato, later Thomas Aquinas developed these ideas further, and together with the theological virtues identified by Apostle Paul — prudence, justice, temperance, and courage — they formed the seven virtues, sometimes also called cardinal. These virtues, of course, were criticized by modern philosophy. For example, Kant defined prudence and courage as qualities of temperament, which can be both useful and harmful. But now this is not of great importance, because all seven virtues are reflected in Prince Myshkin, and the very understanding of these Christian positive qualities is based on the understanding of Plato’s «ideal ruler». The virtues are reflected in Myshkin as follows:
Wisdom (σοφία, sophia): This is knowledge and understanding, especially of those things that have the greatest significance for human life. Myshkin, with his deep reflections and sincerity, demonstrates Platonic wisdom. He sees deeper than surface phenomena and values.
Courage (ἀνδρεία, andreia): This is steadfastness and the ability to maintain one’s conviction in difficult circumstances. Myshkin shows courage in his moral judgments and actions, even when they contradict societal norms.
Temperance (σωφροσύνη, sophrosyne): This is control over one’s desires and emotions. Myshkin, with his calmness and humility, demonstrates this virtue despite tense and conflicting situations.
Justice (δικαιοσύνη, dikaiosyne): This is harmony between the different parts of the soul and harmonious relationships with others. Myshkin strives for justice both on a personal and societal level, always trying to act conscientiously and honestly.
Indeed, the remaining three positive traits — faith (Latin: fides), hope (Latin: spes), and love (Latin: caritas) — are also reflected in Myshkin. His faith in the existence of true goodness (which is manifested, as he says, in children; of course, not only children, as Myshkin sees glimpses of positive qualities in everyone without any logical explanation; and, accordingly, faith in the existence of God, which did not find its place in the first part of this work, but will be vividly revealed in subsequent chapters), his hope that there is still something positive in the world (similar to faith), and love, of course, his feelings for Nastasya Filippovna, which are inextricably linked with Goethe’s «eternal feminine».
Understanding that Myshkin embodies the four «cardinal virtues» of Plato and the seven Christian virtues, we can move on to the allegory with the cave:
«...You may liken our human nature in respect of education and its lack to such an experience as this. Picture men dwelling in a sort of subterranean cavern with a long entrance open to the light on its entire width. Conceive them as having their legs and necks fettered from childhood, so that they remain in the same spot and can only see before them, being prevented by the fetters from turning their heads around. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance, and between the fire and the prisoners, and above them, runs a road, in front of which imagine a low wall built, like the screen over which marionette players show their puppets».
«I see», said Glaucon.
«See also, then, men passing along the wall carrying all sorts of vessels, and statues and figures of animals made of wood and stone and various materials, which appear over the wall. Some of the passers-by, as you would expect, are talking, others silent».
«A strange image you speak of», he said, «and strange prisoners».
«Like ourselves», I replied; «and they see only their own shadows, or the shadows of one another, which the fire throws on the opposite wall of the cave?»
«How could they see anything but the shadows if they were never allowed to move their heads?»
«And of the objects which are being carried in like manner they would see only the shadows?»
«Yes», he said.
«And if they were able to converse with one another, would they not suppose that they were naming what was actually before them?»
«Very true».
Myshkin, one might say, lived his entire life in «Plato’s Cave», as his illness prevented him from familiarizing himself with the entire external world, full of difficulties and injustice. Indeed, the life of the prince is divided into two worlds: «Plato’s Cave» and the «external world». While undergoing treatment in Switzerland, Myshkin only sensually penetrates the «external world» (chapter V, Asinus; chapter VI, monologue), he only familiarizes himself with real injustice. The story of Marie parallels that of Nastasya Filippovna, who endured no fewer hardships, but which Myshkin must come to understand. Due to his illness and life in «Plato’s Cave», Myshkin is such a naive, pure person. This is why Myshkin places spiritual values far above material ones; for him, all these material values are mere shadows, they exacerbate life, as evidenced by the story of Marie, who lived in deep poverty. The prince is alienated from society for this very reason; all his qualities, which seem strange to people of the «external world», are a consequence of emerging from «Plato’s Cave».
Full Shadow
The world of Myshkin indeed splits into two realms. However, if we delve deeper, we can notice a gradation that forms the foundation of Myshkin’s character. Living in the «Platonic cave», Myshkin inherently possesses a character that embodies the seven virtues.
The «full shadow» is the world where Myshkin lives during his epileptic seizures. This illness, which developed in him from early childhood, prevented him from developing mentally, leaving him a child in an adult’s body.
The «full shadow» is an absolute and stagnant world where a person achieves nothing and remains stationary.
Penumbra
The «penumbra» world is the one Myshkin enters after treatment in Switzerland. The circumstances he experienced in the past (such as the story with Marie and the execution scene) are the primary manifestations of the «penumbra».
In this world, Myshkin becomes acquainted with the injustices of the external world, but due to the «full shadow», he perceives everything with childlike innocence, kindness, and naivety.
Indeed, the «penumbra» is the world where Myshkin merely becomes familiar with the «external world», marking his entrance into the «light».
Light
The «light» is the world where the main action of the novel takes place. The «penumbra» parallels and «full shadow» is the antithesis of «light»; as mentioned, the stories of Marie and Nastasya Filippovna are similar.
In the world of «light», Myshkin begins to develop, and his gradual encounters with imperfection lead him back to the world of «full shadow», symbolizing the cyclicality not only of human lives within existence but also of the entire world in general.
Tabula Rasa
From Dostoevsky’s depiction of the world, we ascend to Aristotle’s assertion that every person is born a «blank slate»; only through understanding the surrounding world and through experience do marks appear on this slate, meaning knowledge.
This does not imply that genotype does not influence a person’s life. Indeed, due to the world of «full shadow», Myshkin retains a «blank slate» composed of the «substance» of genes. Therefore, Dostoevsky indeed tells us that Myshkin is born initially as an «ideally pure» and holy person, predestined by his genes.
«The main idea of the novel is to depict a truly beautiful person. There is nothing more difficult in the world, especially now. All writers, not only ours but even all European ones, whoever tried to portray a truly beautiful person, always failed. Because this task is immense. Beauty is an ideal, and the ideal, neither ours nor that of civilized Europe, has yet been fully developed», — F. M. Dostoevsky
Conclusion
Thus, through the lens of Plato’s philosophy, Prince Myshkin can be seen as the image of the ideal person, striving for virtue and the pursuit of truth but suffering from misunderstanding and resistance from the surrounding society.
Theory of ideas; Entelechy; Antithesis of the «golden mean»; Categorical imperative
Based on the idea of the «Platonic cave», we develop Plato’s «theory of ideas», which he represented as a ladder, with the world of things on the lower rungs and the world of ideas on the upper rungs. According to him, things are distorted ideas influenced by matter.
Dualism of the World
The world of ideas is an eternal, unchanging, and perfect realm where the ideal forms of everything exist. Ideas are archetypes that underlie all things. The material world is a changing, imperfect, and temporary reality that we perceive through our senses. Material objects are merely copies or shadows of the ideal forms.
Hierarchy of Knowledge
True knowledge is the understanding of ideas, which is possible through reason and philosophical thinking. This is the highest form of knowledge. Opinion is the understanding of material objects, which is based on sensory perception and is less reliable.
Soul and Idea
The human soul is immortal and has the ability to know the world of ideas. Plato asserted that the soul existed in the world of ideas before its embodiment in a body and can recall these ideas through the process of anamnesis (recollection).
Spiritual Ideals and Material Reality
Prince Lev Myshkin embodies the highest spiritual ideals such as kindness, compassion, honesty, and forgiveness. These ideals can be seen as reflections of Platonic ideas, which are perfect and unchanging. The world around Myshkin, full of cynicism, selfishness, and materialism, is an imperfect copy of these ideals. Myshkin constantly faces conflicts and misunderstandings due to his naivety and unpreparedness for the cruelty and greed of the material world.
Ideals and Practice
Myshkin’s striving to live by high moral principles often seems impractical and even foolish to others. This can be compared to Plato’s idea that true knowledge and wisdom do not always coincide with commonly accepted opinions and practices. Myshkin, as a bearer of ideals, often finds himself in situations where his kindness and honesty clash with deceit and cruelty. This highlights the gap between the world of ideas and material reality.
Knowledge and Suffering
Like a Platonic philosopher who seeks to understand the world of ideas, Myshkin searches for truth and goodness in the world. His sufferings and difficulties can be interpreted as a process of purification and striving for higher knowledge. Myshkin, as a representative of the highest ideals, is unable to fully realize them in the material world, which makes his figure tragic. This reflects Plato’s view that true knowledge and virtue often cannot be fully realized in an imperfect world.
Entelechy
Thus, we come to Aristotle’s concept of potentiality — entelechy.
All beings and objects have potential (dynamic possibilities) and strive for actualization (the transformation of this potential into reality). For example, an acorn has the potential to become an oak tree, and its entelechy is the state of a mature oak.
Entelechy suggests that every being strives to realize its form and fulfill its function according to that form. For humans, this means achieving virtues and the highest good.
According to Aristotle, all actions and processes are directed toward a specific goal or «telos». This goal is related to the natural inclination of each being.
Myshkin possesses an inner potential for achieving moral and spiritual perfection. His character includes innate virtues such as kindness, compassion, and honesty. His epileptic seizures and illnesses can be seen as external limitations that hinder the full development of his potential.
Myshkin begins to actualize his potential through interactions with the world. His trip to Switzerland and return to Russia become stages of his spiritual and moral growth. However, his naivety and childlike innocence clash with the reality of the adult world, leading to complex moral and psychological challenges.
Myshkin’s entelechy is ultimately tied to his striving for the highest good and moral purity. His goal is to live in accordance with high spiritual ideals. But the world around him is full of imperfection and evil, creating insurmountable obstacles on his path to this goal.
Myshkin embodies the conflict between a person’s inner potential for goodness and the reality of the surrounding world. This conflict manifests in his tragic experience when his spiritual ideals cannot be fully realized in the material world. His return to a state of illness at the end of the novel symbolizes the failure to achieve complete entelechy in an imperfect world.
In the context of Aristotelian entelechy, Myshkin can be seen as a philosophical hero who strives to actualize his inner potential through moral and spiritual development. His sufferings and trials are part of the process of actualization, but they also highlight the difficulties of achieving fullness of being in an imperfect world.
Golden mean, Categorical Imperative, The Ideal Man
The fact is that Dostoevsky created, as he says, an «ideal man», and this ideal man is only a «sensual representation» of this ideal person, who does not find a true representation in the real world. This «ideal man» follows Kant’s categorical imperative, representing the «ideal person of morality». Myshkin, with his physical ailments but spiritual purity, embodies the dualism of the material and the spiritual. His epilepsy symbolizes bodily limitations, while his moral qualities demonstrate spiritual heights.
Understanding this brings us to Aristotle’s «golden mean».
Dostoevsky, one could say, criticizes the «ideal man» as envisioned by religion and sensuality — a person who follows the highest moral duty but cannot find the fortitude to withstand the imperfect world. Myshkin, indeed, is the absolute opposite of the pseudo-objective, which finds a place in our world. That is, sensually-subjectively Myshkin is an «ideal person», but mentally-objectively Myshkin is an incomplete person who cannot find a balance between extremes.
Virtue is defined as the mean between two vices, one of which involves excess and the other deficiency. For example, courage is the golden mean between cowardice (deficiency) and reckless bravery (excess).
Each virtue has its golden mean. Wisdom, generosity, temperance, and justice — all these virtues can be described through the balance between extremes. It is essential to consider circumstances and context to determine the right measure in each specific case. Achieving the golden mean requires phronesis or practical wisdom, which allows a person to recognize and act according to the right measure in various situations.
Indeed, due to the world of «complete shadow», Myshkin lacks the «practical wisdom» that would help him find the «fortitude» to withstand the imperfections of the external world. Myshkin does not possess phronesis to the full extent, which manifests in his inability to find the golden mean in his actions and decisions. His actions are often extreme, leading to misunderstandings and conflicts with others.
Myshkin frequently demonstrates extremes in his behavior. His kindness and naivety can be seen as a lack of practical wisdom, and his striving for ideals as an excess of moral demands. For example, his kindness to Nastasya Filippovna and his desire to help her are often perceived as excessive naivety, leading to negative consequences. Myshkin strives for virtue, but his inability to find a balance between idealism and reality becomes the cause of his tragedy. For instance, his extreme honesty and sincerity often prove inappropriate in complex social situations, leading to misunderstandings and disappointments.
Issues of justice and moral choice are often challenging for Myshkin. His desire to be fair and kind to everyone without exception sometimes leads to injustice towards himself and others. His inability to find the golden mean between personal interests and the interests of others leads to his tragic end.
Part II
Chapter I & II
Introduction
Prince Myshkin goes to Moscow, allegedly on inheritance matters, but this is perceived as an attempt to escape the consequences of what happened at Nastasya Filippovna’s. During his six-month absence from St. Petersburg, various rumors circulate about him, but there is little reliable information. In the Epanchins’ house, the prince left a special impression, although he spent little time there. This is evidenced by the tense relations and vague mood in the family after his departure. In the city, there are rumors about the inheritance and the prince’s supposed marriage to a French cancan dancer, which later turns out to be either a lie or a confusion with another person. Rogozhin and his entourage also leave for Moscow after the prince, causing new rumors related to Nastasya Filippovna. In the Epanchins’ house, the mood remains tense, and they speak little about the prince. The General and his wife, Lizaveta Prokofyevna, react differently to the news about him. The General’s wife expresses disappointment, but soon calms down after receiving letters from the old Princess Belokonskaya, who reports good news about the prince. Attitudes towards the prince gradually change, and he begins to be perceived more positively, especially after confirming his noble deeds in Moscow. Ganya Ivolgin experiences a personal crisis after the events at Nastasya Filippovna’s. He returns the money Nastasya gave him through Prince Myshkin, indicating his inner conflict and feelings of guilt. Nastasya Filippovna’s disappearance and reappearance generate new rumors and speculations. Her supposed agreement to marry Rogozhin and the subsequent escape cause much gossip and concern among those around them. At the end of the chapter, the Epanchin family decides to spend the summer abroad, due to their weariness of family dramas and the parents’ attempts to arrange marriages for their daughters.
Changes in Myshkin
From the first lines, the psychological depth of Myshkin’s character is evident. After receiving money and traveling to Moscow for it, he undergoes a slight transformation. As we know from the previous chapter, rumors that have no basis in reality start circulating about him. Receiving the money puts Myshkin in the position of a prince not only symbolically but also materially. When arriving in Petersburg from Moscow and stepping off the train, Lev understands this and that’s why he «catches» a «strange, intense gaze» on himself. This «gaze» symbolizes both the rumors and Myshkin’s psychological state because he starts receiving unwanted attention due to these rumors, which cause his paranoid mood. Indeed, Myshkin is entering a new life, which is completely unfamiliar to him. From now on, he is burdened by his material status, which he doesn’t need but must conform to in order to achieve his goals, namely to «save» Nastasya Filippovna. Additionally, the prince wears new, fine clothes tailored in Moscow. Although these clothes are too fashionable and don’t quite suit him, they still indicate his improved financial situation. Previously, the prince was dressed in worn-out and old clothes, but now he appears in new attire. The prince has become more confident and active. He hastily leaves the hotel, as if he has important matters to attend to, which was previously uncharacteristic of him. This may indicate his new understanding of his social role and responsibilities. Myshkin now faces a new level of interaction. He visits Lebedev’s house, where he is received with respect, but he also notices how his presence makes the host nervous. Lebedev tries to present himself in the best light, possibly due to the prince’s new status. The prince still remains a person unaccustomed to luxury and wealth. He may look better, but internally he hasn’t changed. His moral purity and naivety remain unchanged despite external changes.
Conclusion
It turns out that Nastasya Filippovna fled «right from under the wedding veil», from Moscow, from Rogozhin, back to St. Petersburg. This troubles Myshkin and makes him ponder, as Nastasya asked Lebedev «not to tell the prince». Lebedev himself notices that Nastasya Filippovna fears the prince more than Rogozhin.
Lebedev suggests that the prince move from the hotel to his place and then go to Pavlovsk. Myshkin is touched by the fact that Aglaya Ivanovna, who will also often visit Pavlovsk, will be visiting Darya Alexeyevna.
Overall, Lev’s state is distracted; he himself says that he has «a headache, possibly from the journey». Lebedev is surprised that Myshkin left without saying «goodbye» or even nodding his head, as Lebedev knew about the prince’s politeness and attentiveness.
Chapter III & IV
Myshkin Visits Rogozhin
Myshkin feels anxious and unsure about his decision to visit Rogozhin. This is explained by several reasons:
Fear for his life
Rogozhin has repeatedly shown a tendency towards violence, as evidenced by his outbursts of rage (for example, the incident where he burned money belonging to Nastasya Filippovna).
Relationships with Nastasya Filippovna
Nastasya Filippovna plays a central role in the lives of both Myshkin and Rogozhin. Myshkin is aware of Rogozhin’s intense passion and obsession with Nastasya, which makes the situation extremely tense. Approaching Rogozhin’s house, Myshkin can sense that this passion could lead to tragic consequences, intensifying his anxiety.
«With extraordinary curiosity, he approached to verify his guess; he felt that it would somehow be particularly unpleasant if he guessed right», — this indicates that Myshkin is psychologically pressuring himself. His fears are rooted in the fact that Rogozhin’s residence is incredibly eerie: «The house was large, gloomy, three stories high, without any architectural style, of a dirty green color».
However, as soon as Myshkin confirms his suspicions: «Approaching the gates and looking at the inscription, the prince read: ‘House of the hereditary honorary citizen Rogozhin’», — he stops worrying: «Ceasing to hesitate, he opened the glass door, which slammed shut behind him noisily, and began to ascend the main staircase to the second floor
Indeed, this is a deep psychological observation by the author, which is difficult to explain; it is necessary to delve into the depths.
How does Myshkin perceive Rogozhin?
Their relationship is already incredibly complex at this stage. They are two opposing forces clashing in a «conflict of love», they are completely opposite but possess the same feelings for the woman. However, the motive for these feelings is completely opposite for both: while Myshkin sees Nastasya Filippovna as a broken person torn between the material and the spiritual world, standing on the edge with a dissociative identity disorder, not understanding that her life is still worthy and meaningful even if there are those who are better than her (she considers herself unworthy of Prince Myshkin, precisely because of him, she starts an internal struggle with the material world).
From the first meeting of Myshkin and Rogozhin, we observe their absolute difference, and yet they do not feel aversion to each other because of it. On the contrary, they try to find common ground, even when the situation forces them to fight against each other (their love for Nastasya Filippovna). This phenomenon lies in the fact that both face the absolutely unknown. Myshkin, as a native of the «noumenal world», «Plato’s cave», and «complete shadow», sees in Rogozhin the absolute embodiment of the material world; for him, understanding his antithesis means understanding the whole surrounding new world. Rogozhin, in turn, encounters absolute purity in an imperfect world for the first time. He, being a parallel to Judas, the embodiment of the material, finds interest in what is absolutely opposite and new to him, for he has always been «in the light», and a person who has crossed all three worlds is unique and unusual for him.
Myshkin sees in Rogozhin the opportunity to understand the «world of light», to find common ground with him, for they both possess the same feelings (love for Nastasya Filippovna), even though they are caused by different reasons (specifically, sensuous-prejudiced thinking).
Why does Myshkin stop worrying?
Indeed, this is one of the most deeply psychological moments in the entire novel.
Myshkin observes several houses built according to the same scheme. Myshkin does not fully trust his intuition (or any other information) that Rogozhin lives in the house described. This is what causes his anxiety. Myshkin sees that the building, which according to his information is Rogozhin’s residence, is the embodiment of «ghastliness», representing what Rogozhin is in Myshkin’s eyes — the embodiment of the material, which is vile and unpleasant. He himself says: «With extraordinary curiosity, he approached to verify his guess; he felt that it would somehow be particularly unpleasant if he guessed right». Although Myshkin sees in Rogozhin a comrade (as in everyone else, in principle) and an opportunity to find common ground with the material world (yes, one could say that his main aspiration is to save Nastasya Filippovna, but her salvation is a consequence of the above, not primary).
But, familiarizing himself with the inscription and understanding that the house belongs to Rogozhin, Myshkin’s theory is confirmed that the house belongs to him and that Rogozhin is what Myshkin sees him as.
The Prince’s Suspicions
At the beginning of Part Two, upon exiting the train, Myshkin notices «a pair of eyes staring intently», which causes him anxiety. This is another factor contributing to Myshkin’s unease, indicating his change.
Myshkin notices the same gaze in Rogozhin and needs to verify that it wasn’t his antithesis watching him back then: «Just now, as I was leaving the train, I saw a pair of eyes exactly like the ones you just looked at me with from behind».
He mentions that he feels like he did five years ago: «I don’t know; in the crowd, it even seems like I imagined it; everything begins to seem like an illusion. Brother Parfen, I feel almost the same way as I did five years ago when I still had seizures».
Psychological Duality. Depth and Complexity of the Dialogue
Myshkin notes that he noticed Rogozhin’s house from a hundred paces away. In asserting this, he says something interesting: «I would never have thought you lived in such a house, but as soon as I saw it, I thought: ‘Yes, this must be his house!’»
This shows the duality of Rogozhin’s image in Myshkin’s eyes; he indeed sees in Rogozhin a depth of soul untouched by the darkness of the material world, even though Rogozhin himself embodies the material. It demonstrates that neither Rogozhin nor Myshkin represents the absolute material and spiritual worlds, respectively.
Additionally, there is an evident theme of genetic determinism, suggesting a predisposition of certain families to particular prejudices. This is embedded not only in genes but also in the upbringing that was often consistent across generations at the time.
Myshkin’s Inability to Love
Myshkin begins his monologue by asking Rogozhin about his marriage to Nastasya Filippovna.
In his monologue, the prince says that he loves Nastasya Filippovna «not with love, but with pity» because «her heart is broken»; he wants to save her. If she wants to be with Rogozhin, then Lev will not even appear before her eyes and will never come to Parfen again. Myshkin states that he and Rogozhin have never been «rivals», meaning that he does not intend to take Nastasya away from him, only to save her, because if they are together: «destruction will come upon both», — two material entities with a spiritual core clashing and annihilating each other.
Rogozhin beat Nastasya for not seeing a fiancé in him. Later, he didn’t leave her house and knelt for an entire day, begging for forgiveness.
The dualism in Nastasya lies in her seeing Rogozhin as a materialistic and vile person but still wanting to be with him because it would be her «punishment». She considers herself even worse than Parfen, which is why she wants him to kill her. Nastasya swings back and forth (hence her multiple escapes from the altar), understanding that there is still something good within her that does not deserve a terrible fate — death. But at the same time, her self-deception and belief that she doesn’t deserve forgiveness prevail. Myshkin «sees» (though not fully understands) this, which is why he wants to «save» her. Myshkin serves as the stimulus for her punishment, having shown her that there is still goodness in the world and that she deserves forgiveness.
The Myshkin-Rogozhin-Nastasya Dynamic
This leads us to the triangular dynamic of Myshkin, Rogozhin, and Nastasya; Rogozhin truly loves Nastasya and embodies the material world; Nastasya represents a dualistic world of the material and the spiritual, seeking her punishment and not believing she deserves salvation, with Rogozhin as her punishment and Myshkin as her salvation. She does not want to «disgrace» Myshkin’s name. Myshkin, on the other hand, embodies the spiritual, who «loves out of pity», aiming to save Nastasya so she does not meet her «unjust» fate — death. Myshkin realizes that such a situation would ruin two lives: Rogozhin’s and Nastasya’s, and due to his inherent compassion for people, he cannot fight his urge to «save», which is a form of his «savior complex».
«You. Since then, since her birthday, she has loved you. But she thinks that marrying you is impossible because she would disgrace you and ruin your fate. ‘I, she says, am known for what I am.’ She still claims this. She told me all this herself directly to my face. She’s afraid to ruin and disgrace you, but it’s okay to marry me, she thinks — that’s how low she regards me, take note of that too!»
Nastasya Filippovna hasn’t drowned herself yet only because «Rogozhin might be even scarier». Myshkin does not believe Rogozhin’s words that Nastasya Filippovna is seeking punishment.
Myshkin unconsciously takes the knife in his hands after Parfen finishes talking about Nastasya’s nature. This indicates his desire to «save» Nastasya. He is shocked that Rogozhin is indeed capable of killing her, although he sees this (but again, does not fully understand).
«I somehow knew when I was entering Petersburg, as if I foresaw... I didn’t want to come here! I wanted to forget all this here, to rip it out of my heart! Well, goodbye... But what are you doing!» — after this, Myshkin again unconsciously takes the knife from the table. This again shows that Myshkin is incredibly disappointed that Rogozhin is capable of murder.
This knife, completely new and recently purchased by Rogozhin, symbolizes death. Myshkin even asks why he needs a «garden knife», whether he really «cuts leaves with it». This deeply psychological and symbolic gesture demonstrates Rogozhin’s capacity for murder.
The Body of the Dead Christ in the Tomb

The moment with the painting depicting the Savior shows that there is still something spiritual left in Rogozhin (precisely what Myshkin noticed in him):
«They went through the same rooms that the prince had already passed through; Rogozhin walked a little ahead, the prince behind him. They entered a large hall. Here, along the walls, there were several paintings, all portraits of archbishops and landscapes in which nothing could be distinguished. Above the door to the next room hung one painting, quite strange in its shape, about two and a half arshins in length and no more than six vershoks in height. It depicted the Savior just taken down from the cross. The prince glanced at it briefly, as if remembering something, but without stopping, wanted to pass through the door. He felt very uneasy and wanted to leave this house as soon as possible. But Rogozhin suddenly stopped in front of the painting. «All these paintings here», he said, «were bought by my late father at auctions for a ruble or two; he loved them. One knowledgeable person looked through all of them here: rubbish, he said, but this one - this painting above the door, also bought for two rubles - he said, is not rubbish. Another person offered my father three hundred and fifty rubles for it, and Savelyev, Ivan Dmitrievich, a big art lover among merchants, went up to four hundred, and last week brother Semyon Semyonovich even offered five hundred. I kept it for myself».
After this, he asks the prince if he believes in God.
The fact is that the painting Rogozhin did not sell is Hans Holbein’s «The Dead Christ in the Tomb». Myshkin notes that «from this painting, someone could lose their faith».
Prince Myshkin talks about his meetings with different people and their attitudes toward faith. He recalls a conversation with the scholar-atheist S—m, who, despite his knowledge and education, according to the prince, does not touch the essence of faith. The prince expresses the idea that non-believers and believers often speak and think not about the most important thing in faith.
The prince gives the example of a murderer, a peasant, who crossed himself and asked God for forgiveness before the murder. This example causes Rogozhin to burst into laughter, demonstrating his cynical attitude towards religious contradictions. The next example is a drunk soldier selling his cross, and a young mother sincerely rejoicing at her child’s first smile, expressing a profound religious thought about God’s joy.
Rogozhin first reacts to the prince’s story with laughter, then shows interest in the cross that the prince bought from the soldier. He asks the prince to show the cross and then suggests they exchange crosses, symbolizing a desire to get closer and «brother» with the prince.
The prince emphasizes that true religious feeling cannot be simply explained and does not depend on people’s actions and beliefs. He believes that this is particularly noticeable in the Russian heart. The prince shares his conviction that there are deep moral and religious foundations in Russia, which he began to understand after returning from abroad.
The exchange of crosses between the prince and Rogozhin symbolizes their striving for spiritual brotherhood and mutual understanding, despite differences in character and worldview. This act underscores the importance of faith and spiritual connections for the prince.
This whole section also shows that there is still something spiritual left in Rogozhin. Parfyon exchanged his much more expensive, golden cross for an incredibly cheap tin one. Rogozhin takes the prince to his mother, which can be seen as an attempt to show him an important part of his life. The rooms are described as dark, cold, and austere, which may symbolize Rogozhin’s internal state. Rogozhin’s mother, being in a childlike state, still manages to bless the prince, which underscores the sincerity and purity of this act despite her condition. This blessing is significant for Rogozhin, who, despite his distrust, acknowledges the importance of this act. When they go out onto the stairs, Rogozhin admits that his mother did not understand the words but still blessed the prince, which he interprets as a sign from above. The prince, feeling tenderness and wanting to end the meeting on a positive note, tries to hug Rogozhin, but he refuses, showing his internal conflicts and uncertainty. Rogozhin’s sudden change in behavior, his pallor, trembling lips, and burning eyes, show his deep emotional struggle. Rogozhin tells the prince that he is giving «her» to him, likely referring to Nastasya Filippovna. This concession can be seen as an act of despair or an attempt to rid himself of emotional pain. His words «Remember Rogozhin!» can be interpreted as a request to remember their connection and his sacrifice.
«— Don’t worry! Although I took your cross, I won’t kill you for the watch! — he mumbled indistinctly, suddenly laughing strangely. But suddenly his whole face changed: he turned terribly pale, his lips trembled, and his eyes lit up. He raised his hands, tightly embraced the prince and, gasping, said: — So take her if it’s fate! She’s yours! I yield!.. Remember Rogozhin! And, leaving the prince, not looking at him, he quickly entered his room and slammed the door behind him».
Truly, some of the most profound and incredibly complex chapters in the history of art, which can be called the best in literature.
Chapter V
Pre-Epileptic State
Prince Myshkin experiences agonizing tension and anxiety. He wanders through the city, trying to cope with his thoughts and feelings, but finds no peace. His condition is exacerbated by the anticipation of an epileptic seizure, which affects his perception of reality and behavior. Myshkin feels an acute need for solitude, yet this solitude becomes unbearable. He seeks some form of escape but faces both internal and external obstacles. The prince reflects on his epileptic fits, describing the moment before a seizure as one of supreme self-awareness and harmony, though he acknowledges that this sensation is a result of his illness. Myshkin concludes that despite the pathological nature of his experiences, they represent the highest form of existence and beauty. This paradox reveals his desire to find meaning and harmony even in suffering. Remembering a shop and an item worth sixty kopecks, the prince tries to verify the reality of his memories. This symbolizes his attempt to find a foothold in reality to cope with the internal chaos.
Myshkin’s paradox
Prince Myshkin’s pre-seizure experience suspends ordinary chronology. Moments stretch into an almost crystalline awareness, as if time itself were liquefied. This resembles Husserl’s notion of “transcendental time-consciousness”, yet Myshkin’s access is coerced by pathology rather than intentional reflection. The novel’s shifts from precise clock-time (half-past two, half-past three, six o’clock) to his wandering, dream-like perambulation dramatize the tension between measured reality and his inner, ecstatic suspension. Dostoevsky thus literalizes the collision of inner and outer temporalities, underscoring how illness can momentarily grant — and then violently revoke — profound clarity.
Myshkin himself calls these luminous instants “madness” and “disease”, yet he simultaneously venerates them as the “highest synthesis of life”. Romanticism celebrated ecstatic visionary states — think of Goethe or Coleridge — but for Myshkin there is no voluntary ascent. Each flash of illumination is prefaced by dread and followed by debilitating collapse. The text foregrounds this dialectic: the body rebels even as the mind perceives unity; the “supreme tension” is entwined irrevocably with pain. Myshkin’s paradox lies in valuing a pathological apex as if it were a chosen spiritual revelation, forcing us to question whether suffering-borne insights can be ethically or philosophically equated with genuine, healthy wisdom.
His recurring impulse to verify whether that sixty-kopeck trinket truly existed is far more than a quaint anecdote. It embodies his struggle to anchor subjective ecstasy in an objective world. This coin in the shop window becomes a litmus test of reality: if the shop and the object exist unchanged, then perhaps his disordered perceptions still yield genuine knowledge. Dostoevsky thus dramatizes a classical problem of epistemology — how do we distinguish authentic insight from mere hallucination — and makes it visceral. Myshkin’s repeated, almost compulsive checks mirror the anxiety of someone who has lost faith in his own senses and yet craves proof of external stability.
Russian literary tradition often links suffering to redemption (as in “Crime and Punishment”), yet here suffering yields no lasting transformation — only fleeting rapture followed by fragmentation. Myshkin’s self-analysis in “healthy” moments leads him to label these flashes as “abnormal”, even “low”, yet he admits they feel worth a lifetime. This contradiction underscores the tragedy of his self: each seizure offers a “clear, harmonious joy”, only to leave him more alienated. The beauty is ephemeral, unintegrable into the trajectory of his life. It is a beauty paid for with the disintegration of the self, raising the question of whether any ephemeral transcendence can justify such a cost.
The ambivalent cityscape of St. Petersburg functions almost as a character. Sunlit squares and welcoming cafes coexist with anonymous streets and brooding skies. Myshkin’s drift — from the “Vesy” hotel to the confectioner’s, across bridges and through gardens — mirrors his inner oscillation between hope and despair. The shop window, the bridge over the Neva, and the niche in the hotel staircase each mark moments of crisis and revelation. Everyday environments become charged with symbolic resonance: the Summer Garden suggests a false Eden of contemplation, while the narrow, dark stairwell prefigures the seizure’s “underworld”.
The prince’s neurotic looping — constantly “looking around” for something he cannot name — reveals how memory and delusion interweave. His “sudden idea” to find Nastasya Filippovna’s house, his obsession with Rogozhin’s eyes, and the momentary conviction that he hears a stranger’s plot all illustrate a mind in free-fall. Dostoevsky’s technique here — shifting swiftly among perceptions, memories, and anticipations — immerses us in Myshkin’s fracturing consciousness. This narrative style effectively dramatizes the claustrophobic spiral in which he cannot distinguish present reality from haunting echoes of past anguish.
Chapter VI
Introduction
At the beginning of Chapter Six, Fyodor Dostoevsky presents one of the key themes of his novel «The Idiot» — the theme of the illusory and futile nature of human aspirations. The detailed description of Lebedev’s summer house, with its ornate decorations and terrace adorned with orange, lemon, and jasmine trees, reflects Lebedev’s desire to create an illusion of beauty and prosperity. This motif is linked to Lebedev’s attempts, as a simple and materialistic man, to give his home an appearance of luxury to impress others and increase the rent. The character of Lebedev is portrayed as someone prone to vanity and showiness. His efforts to enhance the appearance of the summer house and his constant calculations of how much he can charge future tenants underscore his mercantile and selfish nature. He admires his property, mentally adding up the profit, which reveals his desire to extract the maximum benefit from the illusion of beauty he has created.
Prince Myshkin, the central character of the novel, despite feeling physically unwell, experiences joy and contentment during his stay at the summer house. This reflects his simplicity and sincerity, contrasting with the complex and often petty motives of the other characters. Despite the pettiness and fussiness surrounding him, the prince remains cheerful and welcoming, distinguishing him as an «ideal» person, free from selfish motives.
The relationship between Lebedev and General Ivolgin also emphasizes the theme of illusions and deceptions. Lebedev seemingly needs the general, which might indicate his need to interact with someone who shares his interests or is simply a necessary part of his social circle. However, their conversations, which often escalate into arguments, reveal the tension and mismatch between their true thoughts and behavior.
Lebedev also exhibits inappropriate concern for the prince, trying to shield him from interacting with the family and other visitors, which appears as an attempt at control. Despite the prince’s requests not to drive people away, Lebedev continues to do so, demonstrating his inner desire for power and manipulation of the situation. This concern for the prince can be seen as an illusion of good intentions, masking Lebedev’s selfish motives. Lebedev attempts to control the prince’s environment, hiding it under the guise of care. His behavior, including frequent visits, eavesdropping on conversations, and constant monitoring, creates an atmosphere of distrust and tension. It’s as if he takes possession of the prince, limiting his freedom and isolating him from others. His actions reveal manipulative motives, as he stages a «secret» meeting and artificially creates intrigue around the desire of a certain «famous person» to meet the prince.
The prince, despite his natural kindness and gentleness, begins to protest against Lebedev’s behavior. He feels discomfort from how Lebedev incessantly fusses around him, creating an illusion of care but in reality complicating his life. The prince directly expresses his displeasure, hinting that Lebedev is restricting his freedom and peace.
Lebedev is depicted as a comical character, overly accommodating and fussy, his excessive excitement and antics seem ridiculous. His behavior becomes particularly striking in contrast to the prince, whose sincerity and straightforwardness highlight Lebedev’s insincerity. Lebedev tries to hide his true motives behind a mask of care and respect, but his behavior only irritates the prince.
Lebedev tries to ingratiate himself with the prince, treating him with excessive deference and attempting to present himself in a favorable light. On one hand, he emphasizes his «lowliness», saying that he «feels» it, while on the other hand, he finds connections with high-ranking people (such as General Ivolgin), thereby trying to elevate his social standing.
Prince Myshkin remains sincere and simple, while Lebedev and other characters, like General Ivolgin, are engrossed in their petty intrigues and seek to make an impression. This underscores the main theme of the novel—the clash of an ideal, honest person with a world of hypocrisy and selfishness.
Lizaveta Prokofyevna is portrayed as an emotional and impulsive woman who strives to control situations and people around her. Upon learning about the prince’s illness, she immediately becomes anxious and begins to fuss, resolutely ignoring any reasonable arguments from her daughters. This highlights her excessive care and tendency to dramatize events. Her reaction to waiting for the prince and subsequent disappointment when he does not arrive enhances the impression that she is under the sway of her own emotions and preconceived expectations.
The relationships within the Epanchin family reveal a mixture of love, care, and underlying tension. Lizaveta Prokofyevna seeks to maintain control over her family, but her children, especially Aglaya, show independence, trying to resist her will. In this excerpt, Aglaya sarcastically comments on her mother, indicating her critical attitude toward Lizaveta Prokofyevna’s excessive emotional reactions.
Lizaveta Prokofyevna is a prisoner of her expectations. Her certainty that the prince must arrive immediately after sending the card and her subsequent disappointment when this does not happen highlight her inability to accept reality as it is. Her expectations and disappointments become a source of comic tension, especially in light of the fact that her anxiety turns out to be completely unfounded.
The appearance of Prince Sh. adds another layer of interest to the situation. He turns out to be an old acquaintance of Prince Myshkin and expresses a genuine desire to visit him. This shows that Prince Myshkin, despite his vulnerability, has friends and sympathizers who appreciate him. This also adds to the overall contrast between real relationships and those that Lizaveta Prokofyevna tends to dramatize.
Apocalypse
The theme of the Apocalypse in the novel may symbolize the inner crisis and transformation of the characters, especially Prince Myshkin. His «holiness» and purity clash with a reality full of hypocrisy, cruelty, and betrayal. This conflict can be interpreted as apocalyptic because it shatters Myshkin’s idealized notions of the world and people. For instance, his interactions with Nastasya Filippovna and Aglaya lead to his internal crisis when he realizes that his pursuit of absolute goodness may be impractical in the real world.
Dostoevsky was deeply religious, and his works are often imbued with Christian symbolism. The Apocalypse in the novel can be linked to the biblical concept of the end of the world and God’s judgment. This is particularly evident in the character of Prince Myshkin, who can be seen as a kind of «Christ» in this «sinful» world. His attempts to bring goodness and love to a society that does not understand him can be compared to Christ’s mission before the Last Judgment, where everyone will have to account for their sins.
In this context, the Apocalypse may signify judgment that comes not only at the end of time but also in everyday life — when people face the consequences of their actions and see their souls without embellishment.
In a broader sense, the Apocalypse in «The Idiot» can be seen as a symbol of the destruction of social order and the exposure of its imperfections. In the novel, Dostoevsky critiques a society permeated with hypocrisy and selfishness. Myshkin, as an idealist, tries to live by high moral principles but encounters rejection and ridicule. This can be seen as a form of apocalypse — a revelation of the true state of society, which cannot accept purity and honesty.
The Apocalypse can also symbolize the spiritual death of characters who have lost their moral compass. General Ivolgin, with his lies and fantasies, gradually destroys his own personality, which can be interpreted as his internal apocalypse. Myshkin, observing the downfall of such people, experiences a deep emotional and spiritual upheaval, ultimately leading to his own crisis.
However, the Apocalypse in the biblical sense does not only bring destruction; it also implies a new beginning, purification, and the possibility of salvation. In the context of «The Idiot», this can be interpreted as the potential renewal of society and individuals who have gone through trials and recognized their mistakes. Despite all his suffering, Prince Myshkin remains true to his ideals, which may symbolize the hope for rebirth and a new beginning, even in the most hopeless environment.
Thus, the theme of the Apocalypse in «The Idiot» is a multi-layered symbol that encompasses the destruction and revelation of the inner worlds of the characters, a critique of the social order, and religious aspects of the end of times and rebirth. It reflects the profound philosophical and spiritual questions that Dostoevsky raises in his novel, making it one of the key themes of the work.
General Ivolgin’s Truth
General Ivolgin is a character who, from the very beginning, presents himself as a man with grand pretensions to former greatness and glory. He often resorts to fabrications and lies to maintain the image he has created for himself in the eyes of others. His lies serve as a kind of defense mechanism, allowing him to hide his weaknesses and failures. Ivolgin immerses himself in a world of fantasies and myths, trying to portray himself as an important figure connected to great people and events.
This tendency to lie is linked to his inability to accept reality — his losses and insignificant position in society. In a sense, Ivolgin is a tragic figure who tries to avoid acknowledging his downfall by creating an alternative reality. His lies are not malicious or intended to harm others; rather, they testify to his inner weakness and fear of the truth.
General Ivolgin’s admission of the truth is a rare and significant moment in the novel. When he states (to himself) that he has told the truth for the first time, this event becomes deeply unsettling and, at the same time, destructive for him. For a man accustomed to living in a world of make-believe, the truth proves to be too heavy a burden. This admission of the truth can be seen as a kind of purification, but it also symbolizes the beginning of his final downfall.
Ivolgin’s lies gradually destroy his personality. He sinks deeper into illusions, losing touch with reality. Each new fabrication only intensifies his inner division and alienates him from those around him. He becomes a prisoner of his own self-deception, incapable of returning to a genuine and honest existence. This leads to his social isolation and the loss of respect from those close to him.
In this context, Ivolgin’s lies are not just a means of manipulation, but a way of surviving in a world he perceives as too harsh for his weak nature. However, this path leads to self-destruction, as the lies distance him from reality and prevent him from coping with life’s challenges.
When Ivolgin finally tells the truth, this event can be interpreted as a catharsis — a moment of purification through suffering. He realizes his insignificance and his inability to be the person he imagined himself to be. However, this realization does not lead to liberation or spiritual renewal, as is often the case in classical narratives. On the contrary, it leads to his ultimate downfall.
The truth becomes a destructive force for Ivolgin, tearing apart his inner world. He can no longer hide behind his fabrications and is forced to confront the reality he has long avoided. This leads him to a deep crisis and, ultimately, to moral and physical collapse (in the next chapters).
The Poor Knight
Aglaya Epanchina reads Cervantes’ «Don Quixote», and her exclamation, «there is no better poor knight», is significant because it highlights the romantic idealism and loyalty characteristic of the novel’s main character. The «poor knight» symbolizes a person who acts not for material gain but from the heart, following higher, spiritual principles. Don Quixote, despite his comical delusions, remains a symbol of nobility and the pursuit of ideals, even when these ideals seem irrelevant in the real world.
The concept of the «poor knight» is reflected in Prince Myshkin, who himself embodies kindness, purity, and selflessness. Like Don Quixote, Myshkin lives by his inner convictions, often clashing with the world around him, which misunderstands him and sees him as an «idiot». However, like Don Quixote, Myshkin remains true to his ideals, even though his good intentions often lead to tragic outcomes.
Myshkin also possesses the traits of the «poor knight» in that his actions are driven not by the desire for material wealth but by the desire to help others, to make the world a better place, even if it means sacrificing himself. His poverty is not only a financial state but also an inner state of the soul, free from selfishness and malicious intent.
The image of the portrait mentioned in the text is also symbolic. The painting that Adelaida is working on is significant for her because she strives to capture the «essence» of her subject, which is a challenging task, especially when it comes to Myshkin. His complex inner world, purity, and innocence are difficult to convey through an ordinary portrait. This further emphasizes Myshkin’s uniqueness and the extraordinariness that is hard to capture and convey through art.
Myshkin feels embarrassed because he sees something of Don Quixote in himself — a connection between his image and that of the «poor knight», who is often misunderstood and devalued by those around him. His embarrassment arises from the fact that he does not consider himself a hero or a knight, even in poverty. His idealism and kindness often lead to misunderstanding and tragic consequences, and perhaps he realizes this, feeling internal pain from the knowledge that his pure intentions may be perceived differently.
Aglaya wanted to start reading Pushkin’s poem «There lived a poor knight…», which is an important literary symbol in the context of the novel. These verses highlight the longing for eternal memory, for preserving what is important and valuable in life. In the context of Aglaya and Myshkin, these lines can be interpreted as an expression of her desire to immortalize the ideal that Myshkin represents for her — the ideal of a person who lives not according to the laws of the material world, but according to the laws of a higher, spiritual order.
The poem may also symbolize her unrequited love and the pain associated with the realization that people like Myshkin can be forgotten and misunderstood by society. For Aglaya, Myshkin becomes a kind of ideal that she wants to preserve in her memory, perhaps as something bright and pure, which is no longer possible to find in the world around her.
The verses Pushkin wanted to read are directly related to Myshkin’s image as a symbol of purity and innocence, who, nevertheless, may be forgotten and rejected by society. This also emphasizes the tragic nature of his fate — a person who would like to preserve light and goodness in the world, yet finds himself on the brink of oblivion and destruction.
Thus, through «Don Quixote», the concept of the «poor knight», Adelaida’s painting, and the verses Aglaya wanted to read, Dostoevsky reveals a deep connection between idealism, purity, and the tragedy of human existence. Myshkin, like Don Quixote, embodies the highest ideals that do not find resonance in the surrounding world, and this makes him embarrassed and vulnerable in his attempts to remain true to his principles.

After all, “He didn’t keep the fast.”

And welcomed, into the Eternal Kingdom,

Pushkin’s verses about the «poor knight» demonstrate the contradictions of religious beliefs».
Aglaya wants to read them because the poem itself is an allegory of «Don Quixote», and the image of the «poor knight» first finds its reflection in him — Don Quixote is the originator of this term.
In the poem, the «poor knight» follows his personal beliefs and the religious symbol of the «Virgin Mary», which compels him to live according to certain principles, but without fasting or praying to the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
Ultimately, Pushkin shows, through the abstract representation of «salvation» by the Virgin Mary herself, that it is not merely following religious rules, which claim that you will enter the higher world if you observe them, but rather the good intentions inspired by any symbol (whether religious or not) — in this case, the Virgin Mary — that grant you the possibility of ascension and finding forgiveness.
Chapter VII-XII
Myshkin’s isolation; Ippolit’s uncertainty
        Once again, Myshkin’s attempts to balance the situation are perceived by the surrounding society as an insult. This further emphasizes the tragic nature of Myshkin’s character: he strives for goodness, but his actions often lead to unpredictable and undesirable consequences. His own words about being guilty in relation to Burdovsky highlight his tendency for self-criticism and his feeling of personal inability to bring about good.
Ippolit’s character behaves contradictorily, standing on the edge of despair and openly expressing his thoughts and emotions while suffering from a severe fever and physical weakness. He expresses gratitude and hatred, fear and pride, self-pity and contempt for others. He is aware of his imminent death and possibly feels the need to make one final statement to the society with which he feels at odds. In his speech, Ippolit touches on themes of sincerity, beauty, and cruelty. He accuses those around him of valuing outward appearance and elegance more than genuine feelings and moral principles. These accusations extend to Prince Myshkin, whom Ippolit perceives as a symbol of paternalism and hypocritical charity. His hysterical laughter and coughing underline his psychological and physical vulnerability:
«I considered you capable of development», Ippolit began again, emerging from his reverie. «Yes! That’s what I wanted to say», he exclaimed as if suddenly remembering. «Look at Burdovsky, he sincerely wants to defend his mother, doesn’t he? But in doing so, he shames her. And here’s the prince, offering his friendship and financial support to Burdovsky from the bottom of his heart, and maybe he's the only one of you who doesn't feel disgust toward him. Yet they stand before each other like real enemies... Ha-ha-ha! You all hate Burdovsky because, in your view, he treats his mother in an ugly and inelegant way, right? Right? Right? You all love beauty and elegance so much, that’s the only thing you value, isn’t it? (I’ve long suspected that it’s all about form!) Well, know this: none of you might love his mother as much as Burdovsky! Prince, I know you secretly sent money through Ganya to Burdovsky’s mother, and I bet... (he laughed hysterically)... I bet Burdovsky will now accuse you of being tactless and disrespectful toward his mother. I swear it! Ha-ha-ha!» Here he ran out of breath and began coughing».
Ippolit’s interactions with Lizaveta Prokofievna and other characters show increasing tension. Lizaveta Prokofievna oscillates between pity and irritation toward Ippolit. Her behavior reflects a mix of maternal instincts and a desire to maintain control over the situation, which ultimately leads her to embrace Ippolit, trying to comfort him.
Evgeny Pavlovich and other characters, such as Keller and Lebedev, add comic and ironic tones to the scene, intensifying the sense of chaos and moral ambiguity. Their remarks and actions demonstrate the cynicism and duplicity typical of many of Dostoevsky’s characters.
In this episode, Ippolit displays intense emotional strain, expressing his fear of death and awareness that his life has been unfulfilled. His words about nature «mocking» the best of beings and his inability to leave a mark on the world reflect deep existential pain and a sense of life’s futility:
«I... you…» he began joyfully. «You don’t know how much I... He always spoke of you with such enthusiasm, there’s Kolya... I love his enthusiasm. I didn’t corrupt him! He’s the only one I leave behind... I wanted to leave everyone, all of them— but no one was there, no one at all... I wanted to be a man of action, I had the right... Oh, how much I wanted! Now I want nothing, nothing at all, I promised myself not to want anything; let them search for the truth without me! Yes, nature is mocking! Why does it create the best beings only to laugh at them later? It made it so that the only being recognized as perfect on earth... it made it so that, after showing him to people, it destined him to say the words that caused so much bloodshed that, if it all spilled at once, people would surely drown in it! Oh, it’s good that I’m dying! I, too, might have said some horrible lie, nature would have led me to it! I corrupted no one... I wanted to live for the happiness of all people, for the discovery and proclamation of truth... I looked at Meyer’s wall through the window and thought I could speak for just a quarter of an hour and convince everyone, everyone, and, once in my life, I was among you, even if not with people! And what came of it? Nothing! It came to nothing, you despise me! So, I’m unnecessary, so I’m a fool, so it’s time! I didn’t leave a single memory! Not a sound, not a trace, not a single deed, I didn’t spread a single conviction! Don’t laugh at the fool! Forget! Forget everything... forget, please, don’t be so cruel! Do you know that if it weren’t for this tuberculosis, I would have killed myself…». It seemed like he wanted to say more but stopped, threw himself into a chair, covered his face with his hands, and cried like a little child.
«What should we do with him now?» exclaimed Lizaveta Prokofievna. She ran over to him, grabbed his head, and pressed it tightly to her chest. He sobbed convulsively. «Well, well, well! Don’t cry, that’s enough, you’re a good boy, God will forgive you for your ignorance; well, that’s enough, be brave... You’ll be ashamed later as well…».
The culmination of his despair is an open expression of hatred toward Prince Myshkin, whom he blames for his humiliation and cowardice. Ippolit feels insulted and degraded that his emotional outburst and tears were perceived as weakness, and he lashes out at Myshkin, whom he once idealized.
Myshkin’s emotional instability
Prince Myshkin, while trying to maintain order and calm Ippolit, ultimately finds himself at the center of an emotional attack. Ippolit’s accusations of «syrupy charity» emphasize Myshkin’s inner weakness and inability to deal with such displays of negativity. Myshkin strives for peace and reconciliation, but his good intentions only exacerbate the conflict and further alienate him from those around him. He realizes that he cannot help Ippolit, and this awareness leads him to a state of extreme exhaustion and helplessness:
«— Well, this is exactly what I feared! — exclaimed the prince. — This is how it had to be!» Ippolit quickly turned to him with furious anger, and every feature on his face seemed to tremble and speak. «Ah, that’s what you feared! 'This is how it had to be,' you say? Then know that if I hate anyone here», he shouted with a hoarse, shrill voice, spitting as he spoke (I hate you all, all of you!), «but you, you hypocritical, syrupy soul, idiot, millionaire benefactor, I hate you more than anyone and anything in the world! I understood you long ago and hated you even when I first heard of you. I’ve hated you with all the hatred in my soul... It’s you who orchestrated all of this! You drove me to this fit! You drove a dying man to shame, you, you, you are responsible for my disgraceful cowardice! I would kill you if I lived! I don’t want your charity, I’ll take nothing from anyone, you hear me, nothing from anyone! I was delirious, and you have no right to celebrate! I curse all of you once and for all!» And then he completely choked up».
Prince Myshkin experiences a painful sense of guilt, which is intensified by his tendency toward self-doubt. He blames himself for being the cause of the «shameful» evening and awaits punishment. His inclination toward self-blame and his fear of possible consequences leads him into a deep internal crisis. This is particularly evident in his reflections on the events involving the eccentric woman who spoke with Evgeny Pavlovich from her carriage. Myshkin wonders if he is to blame for this incident, leading him to agonizing thoughts of a possible conspiracy. The entire episode with the carriage and Nastasya Filippovna’s involvement arouses serious concern in Myshkin. He feels that there is something significant and troubling behind this incident, affecting both himself and the other participants. However, he cannot fully understand what is happening, which exacerbates his sense of anxiety and helplessness.
The relationship between Nastasya Filippovna and Evgeny Pavlovich remains a mystery to the prince, one he cannot solve. This adds to his inner conflict, as he feels responsible for what is happening but cannot grasp how. Myshkin’s relationships with the Epanchin family go through a phase of tension and estrangement caused by the evening's events. Although the Epanchins gradually calm down, Myshkin senses their coldness and distance, which intensifies his feelings of isolation and loneliness. His meetings with Adelaide and Prince S. highlight this tension, as they come incognito and avoid openly discussing the conflict.
Myshkin also realizes that he is suspected of participating in intrigues, which adds to his anxiety. He cannot shake the thought that he is being implicated in events in which he is not involved, undermining his confidence and self-esteem.
At one point, Prince Myshkin seriously considers fleeing, feeling that staying in this web of intrigue and conflict is unbearable. However, he quickly realizes that running away would be cowardice, and he is obliged to stay and try to figure out what is happening. This decision emphasizes his moral steadfastness and sense of responsibility, despite his inner contradictions and doubts.
To remember. Myshkin’s letter to Aglaya
Once, at St. Petersburg, Kolya handed a letter to Aglaya, saying only that it was for her alone. Aglaya gave the presumptuous boy a stern look, but Kolya did not wait and left. She unfolded the note and read: «Once you honored me with your trust. Perhaps you have entirely forgotten me by now. How did it happen that I am writing to you? I don’t know, but I felt an irresistible desire to remind you of myself, specifically to you. There were many times when I needed you all three, but out of the three, I only saw you. I need you, very much. I have nothing to write about myself, nothing to tell. That was not my intention; I just wanted to wish for your happiness. Are you happy? That is all I wanted to say. Your brother, Prince L. Myshkin».
Having read this short and somewhat clumsy note, Aglaya suddenly blushed and became lost in thought. It would have been difficult to convey the course of her thoughts. Among other things, she asked herself, «Should I show this to anyone?» Somehow, she felt embarrassed. However, she eventually threw the letter into her drawer with a mocking and strange smile. The next day she pulled it out again and placed it inside a thick, leather-bound book (which is what she always did with her papers, to quickly find them when needed). And it was only a week later that she happened to notice what the book was. It was «Don Quixote of La Mancha». Aglaya burst into laughter — for reasons unknown.
Part III
At the beginning of the chapter, Myshkin experiences an intense inner conflict. One consequence of this conflict is fear. This fear stems not only from social expectations or insecurity but also from Myshkin's awareness of his own otherness and vulnerability. His painful reluctance to look at Aglaya, who is closely watching him, reflects his inner anxiety about something he deeply values and perhaps loves, yet fears losing. He hesitates to meet her gaze because he is uncertain about his feelings and how they will be perceived. This moment underscores his uncertainty and emotional vulnerability.
Myshkin remains an embodiment of extreme naivety and sincerity. He acts as a moral idealist who strives for reconciliation and forgiveness, even when it seems impossible. In his dialogue with Evgeny Pavlovich and others, the prince emphasizes the importance of spiritual forgiveness and inner harmony. He seeks to convince those around him of the necessity not only to forgive Ippolit but also to accept his repentance with kindness and sincerity. Myshkin does not view forgiveness as a formal act but as a profound spiritual action essential for restoring inner peace. This idea highlights his role as a bearer of Christian ideals of humility and compassion, which stand in contrast to the nihilism and cynicism of those around him.
Despite his good intentions, the prince becomes increasingly alienated from society. Myshkin, symbolizing purity and moral integrity, is often misunderstood by those around him. Even those who appreciate him, like Lizaveta Prokofievna, cannot fully grasp his ideals. His inability to integrate into societal norms and conventions accentuates his alienation. This isolation deepens as he becomes more entangled in conflicts he seeks to resolve. Myshkin clearly understands that there is a distortion of moral values in the world. His reflections on how the most horrific crimes occur when a person no longer considers themselves a criminal express his awareness of a moral crisis. He perceives the danger of youth losing their connection to moral foundations and sees this not merely as an isolated case but as a broader issue. However, his words, although filled with spiritual wisdom, fail to resonate with those around him. This gap between his spiritual aspirations and reality emphasizes the tragic nature of his situation.
Myshkin experiences constant emotional instability. Although outwardly he seeks reconciliation and spiritual harmony, inside he struggles between his ideals and the reality in which he lives. His reaction to Evgeny Pavlovich's words and the ensuing discussion about liberalism and crime causes him distress, making him short of breath, symbolizing his inner crisis. This moment points to his psychological and emotional vulnerability, which gradually intensifies as the novel progresses.
Myshkin has fallen in love with Aglaya. He is troubled by the fact that she does not love him in return, which is why he contemplates challenging anyone who dares to suggest they are «in love with each other» to a duel. He suppresses his feelings because he believes that he is merely a source of amusement for Aglaya, thinking that she only playfully teases him and does not see his truly kind heart and pure mind. Aglaya's behavior is explained by her duality, similar to Nastasya Filippovna. Aglaya understands that for most people, Prince Myshkin is merely an «idiot», and if she marries him, she will become a target of misunderstanding and perhaps ridicule.
After Myshkin’s reflections, Rogozhin comes to him and informs the prince that Nastasya Filippovna is waiting for him tomorrow. It is revealed that Nastasya wants to marry Rogozhin when the prince and Aglaya get married, and she wishes to bring them together to «make the prince happy». It also becomes known that Nastasya and Aglaya are writing letters to each other, which causes the prince deep and conflicting emotions.
Amid all of this, Myshkin expresses his hope that Aglaya loves him, drawing a parallel between her and Nastasya Filippovna, as both «torment» their love, yet «reward» it in the end.
Tomorrow is Myshkin’s birthday, and he convinces Rogozhin to come to his dacha.
At his dacha, Myshkin discusses the mindset of masters and slaves while everyone is talking about Lebedev's anecdote. Myshkin argues that due to hard labor (for instance, building castles), slaves’ revolt against their masters and, as illustrated in the anecdote, even devour those who are socially higher than them — the masters.
Then, Lebedev delivers a fascinating monologue:
In this monologue, Lebedev expresses deep reflections on the state of society, the moral crisis, and the difference between past and present times. His speech is filled with allegories and symbols, blending tragedy and comedy, revealing his mental state and philosophical views on the degeneration of humanity.
The Criminal and Medieval Symbolism:
Lebedev begins by analyzing a 12th-century criminal, symbolizing a man consumed by sin but tormented by guilt. His description of a criminal who devours monastic life and infants serves as a hyperbole to illustrate religious fear, the sense of sin, and redemption. This represents the symbolic struggle between the material and spiritual aspects of human existence, where a person experiences an inner conflict between bodily desires and spiritual guilt.
Conscience and Repentance:
The criminal, who decides to confess his crimes despite the terrible punishments awaiting him, demonstrates that the idea of spiritual salvation and fear of sin were stronger than physical suffering. Lebedev emphasizes that in the past, people had a powerful unifying idea — faith, which was stronger than all calamities and catastrophes. This highlights the role of spiritual values, which, according to Lebedev, no longer hold the same power in modern society.
Critique of Modernity:
Lebedev contrasts the past, where moral and spiritual foundations existed, with modern society, which he believes has degenerated. He mentions «vices and railroads» as symbols of the destruction of spiritual foundations in the world. Railroads here serve as a metaphor for technological progress, which, in his view, has brought material benefits but weakened the spiritual «sources of life». Lebedev criticizes society for losing the strength of spiritual ideals, believing that material prosperity has come at the cost of moral values.
Irony and Self-Awareness:
Lebedev acknowledges that he is drunk but still asserts his righteousness. This moment highlights the comedic and tragic nature of his character — despite his weaknesses, he still believes that his words about the serious crisis in modern society are true. His statement, «everyone is decaying», is a vivid image symbolizing the decay and weakening of humanity.
Beauty will save the world
One of the best parts of the novel begins right here. Ippolit wakes up from a brief sleep and decides to read his obituary. He indirectly claims that there is no salvation in beauty, while Myshkin had said that «beauty will save the world» simply because he «fell in love».
Many are against Ippolit reading some «article», but the first one surrenders to chance and suggests «flipping a coin» to determine whether they will read or not. This shows that Ippolit is indifferent to what happens next, as he himself claims that «there will be no more time» and later adds, «as the Apocalypse says». For him, even his final words — the most important words of his life — mean nothing, and he challenges the universe, science, and probability theory. «Let the world decide whether I should read or not», he seems to say with his actions.
He begins reading with an epigraph titled «Après moi le déluge» or «After me, the flood». The article itself is called «My Necessary Explanation».
The epigraph's title refers to the famous phrase of Louis XV, which implies that after the end of some process, it doesn't matter what happens next. In this case, it relates to Ippolit’s life — he no longer cares about the consequences of his actions, knowing he will soon die.
The first paragraph reflects Ippolit's despair and catastrophic nihilism. For him, it makes no difference whether he dies in peace or anxiety; he understands the outcome is the same, and the circumstances of his end are irrelevant.
Ippolit's obituary is a heavy psychological document of his absolute nihilism. He «tries» to prove that he has no reason to live, that his «final words» tell everyone that «we all meet the same end». But contradictions overcome him; he even admits he wouldn't endure terrible torture, even if he only had two weeks to live.
Ippolit’s dream about the «terrible beast» symbolizes his fear of death. The monster that appears in his room is «death» itself. He is terrified of it, but his loved ones are not — they merely «try to catch it», though they «do not understand what it is». The first refers to a postponement, an attempt to conquer the cause of Ippolit’s death, while the second reflects a lack of complete understanding and acceptance of what is coming. His dog, Norma, symbolizes Ippolit’s salvation, his peace; he apparently loved her deeply. In the end, his pet saves the room from the «terrible beast» by sacrificing herself.
Facing the threat of death, Ippolit becomes interested in observing other people, finding himself through them. It’s not what people bring into the world that interests Terentyev now, nor is he concerned with understanding them to become their equal or at least empathetic. Rather, it's the people themselves — their deeds and thoughts. Again, Ippolit’s catastrophic nihilism emerges — he rejects the pursuit of knowledge, as he doesn’t have long to live. Ippolit speaks of the critical nature of death and its inevitability. He cannot understand how people, «with sixty years ahead of them», fail to become rich; it baffles him how people harbor resentment towards the injustice of the world. He expresses the idea that everything can be changed and fixed since death is the only certainty. He himself doesn’t fully understand why in this world, death alone is the critical, fatal, and absolute reality, while most people do not accept this and surrender to less pressing and serious problems, failing to appreciate their lives.
The next lines should not be «analyzed», but simply read by those who seek to understand:
«Ask them, ask each and every one of them, what they think happiness is? Oh, be sure that Columbus was happy not when he discovered America, but when he was discovering it; be sure that the highest moment of his happiness may have been exactly three days before the discovery of the New World, when his mutinous crew, in despair, nearly turned the ship back to Europe! It’s not the New World that matters, though it may well collapse. Columbus died having hardly seen it and, essentially, not knowing what he had discovered. It’s life that matters, one single life — the act of discovering it, endlessly and eternally, not the discovery itself! But what can I say! I suspect that everything I am saying now sounds so much like the most common phrases that I’ll likely be seen as a low-grade student presenting an essay on ‘the sunrise,’ or they’ll say that maybe I wanted to express something, but for all my efforts, I couldn’t ‘develop.’ However, I’ll add that in any genius or new human thought, or even just in any serious human thought that arises in someone’s mind, there always remains something that can’t be conveyed to others, even if you wrote whole volumes and explained your thought for thirty-five years; something will always remain that will never leave your skull and will stay with you forever; you’ll die with it without passing on, perhaps, the most important part of your idea. But if I, too, failed to convey everything that has tormented me these past six months, at least you’ll understand that, having reached my current ‘final conviction,’ I have perhaps paid too high a price for it; this is what I considered necessary, for reasons known to me, to highlight in my ‘Explanation.’»
Some of the deepest lines in the novel. Ippolit believes that happiness is not the final goal, but the process of achieving that goal. The discovery itself, in Ippolit's opinion, loses significance compared to the moment of discovery, the emotions, and the efforts that accompanied the process. This idea resonates with the concept of finding the meaning of life through continuous effort, rather than through achieving the final result. Ippolit, aware that not everyone will grasp his idea, asserts the «elusiveness of human thought». He speaks of how every great thought contains something that cannot be fully expressed. Even if one writes volumes and explains their idea over a lifetime, something remains hidden, inaccessible to others. This highlights the complexity and subjectivity of perceiving the world and ideas. The thought a person carries within them is a unique internal experience that cannot be fully shared, even with detailed explanations. Ippolit also acknowledges that his attempt to explain his ideas might seem undeveloped or generic. He assumes that his words may be perceived as trivial phrases or failed attempts to convey something profound. This demonstrates the sense of insecurity and dissatisfaction common to many thinkers who realize the limitations of language and the ability to express their thoughts. It’s as if he is saying that the most significant personal revelations always remain unsaid.
Ippolit further touches on the personal price for his convictions. He emphasizes that in order to reach his «final conviction», which he now holds, he had to pay a high price (his life). This process of self-analysis and self-understanding was painful, and though he is unsure if his ideas will be understood, it is important for him to make clear that the price for this realization was immense. This hints at the spiritual and emotional journey one undergoes while reflecting on the meaning of life and happiness.
Later, Ippolit reflects on his isolation and detachment from people due to his impending death. He has left his friends, «distanced himself from his family».
Final Conviction
Ippolit tells the story of a doctor who couldn’t find a place for himself in St. Petersburg after being dismissed from his provincial post. Ippolit performed a few good deeds: first, he returned the doctor’s lost wallet with all his and his wife’s documents, and later he helped him find a job using his limited connections. During a conversation with the mediator of those connections, Bakhmutov, Ippolit expresses his idea of what he calls his «final conviction» through the story of a poor old general who befriended convicts being sent to Siberia. The general always supported them, and though his acts were more quantitative than qualitative, he became remembered across Russia. Even hardened criminals, people who had killed a dozen others for no reason, would recall this old man every twelve years or so.
Ippolit believes that if he only had two months left to live, he would have to give up larger, more troublesome good deeds, like helping the doctor, and instead prioritize smaller, simpler acts of kindness. This reflection brings him to his ultimate «final conviction» — the decision to kill himself.
Ippolit encounters the painting of a dead Christ at Rogozhin’s house after their conversation. He comments that «les extrémités se touchent» (opposites meet). Rogozhin lives a «full life», focused on immediate experiences without deep philosophical considerations. This contrast between the two characters intensifies Ippolit’s inner crisis. He realizes that he has «counted his days», while Rogozhin lives in the present without concern for such existential questions. The encounter exhausts Ippolit and fills him with deep unease.
Ippolit contemplates the suffering of Jesus, who was able to overcome the laws of nature but couldn’t triumph over human cruelty, even though it was necessary. This human cruelty led to the martyr’s death, even though he had conquered it in life. Ippolit wonders if, knowing what awaited him — how he would look upon death, what suffering he would endure — Jesus would have ascended the cross or used his power to stop the madness. This thought casts doubt on divine strength and the concept of resurrection, questioning the very idea of victory over death.
Ippolit ponders how Christ’s disciples could believe in his resurrection after seeing such a frightening, ravaged body. This vision deepens Ippolit’s sense that the laws of nature are relentless and that even those with supernatural powers cannot escape death. He sees nature as a «massive machine», senseless and destructive.
After meeting Rogozhin, Ippolit experiences something like a vision. Rogozhin appears in his room, but Ippolit cannot tell whether it is a real person or a ghost. This moment reflects Ippolit’s psychological state — his anxiety and uncertainty. Despite his doubts, he doesn’t feel fear at the possible apparition. However, he perceives the vision as humiliating. He feels he can no longer remain in a life that takes such «strange, offensive forms». The culmination of Ippolit’s reflections is the realization that his decision to end his life stems not from logical reasoning but from revulsion toward life and its manifestations. The vision of Rogozhin, which he sees as humiliating, becomes the final straw that pushes him toward his ultimate decision. He can no longer tolerate the «dark force», which he perceives as senseless and destructive, like the tarantula he hallucinates in his fevered state.
Torture
Ippolit reflects on how he prepared himself for suicide, mentioning his childhood pistol, which he planned to use for his final act. His decision to end his life is driven not only by physical suffering but also by philosophical reasons. He knows that his time on earth is limited and sees no reason to remain in this world. He feels that his death is a protest against the meaninglessness of existence, and he seeks control at least over this last act of his life.
Ippolit questions the meaning of moral and religious commandments, especially those concerning suicide. He wonders who benefits from his living the last few weeks of his life. He rejects the idea that he should continue to live because of some moral rules, as his life is essentially over — he has only a few weeks left. This challenges societal and religious norm that try to dictate how people should live and die.
Ippolit doesn’t entirely reject religion, but he is critical of its dogmas, especially the ideas of obedience and submission. He cannot reconcile himself to the thought that his life is just a part of some universal harmony that demands sacrifice. He believes that if there is a God, it is unjust to require unconditional submission in the face of suffering and death, especially when the meaning of this suffering is incomprehensible. Ippolit acknowledges the possibility of Providence and eternal life, but he cannot grasp their nature, and therefore sees no point in being held accountable for what he cannot understand.
Ippolit expresses deep existential despair. He feels like an «outcast» at the feast of life, where every element of nature has its place and happiness, while he is deprived of it. He cannot accept that his life has no significance in the grand scheme of things. Even the beauty of nature, which others see as a source of solace, becomes for him a symbol of his isolation and insignificance.
For Ippolit, suicide is not merely an act of despair, but a form of protest against a world he doesn’t understand and that doesn’t understand him. He realizes it’s not a «great rebellion», but for him, it is a way to assert his will in at least one action. His death is not just an escape from suffering but a statement against the very existence, which seems to mock him. Ippolit sees death as a release from the futility of life. He rejects the consolations offered by society and religion and chooses to take his death into his own hands rather than live in circumstances imposed upon him. He views death as the final act in which he can express his free will.
Act of Final Conviction
After Ippolit reads his obituary, many don’t take him seriously. He is given an ultimatum — either he hands over the gun or he’ll be taken to the police. Ippolit deceives everyone and attempts to commit suicide. He does so, but forgets to load the cap, which results in many scornful remarks, undermining his «honor». With his last strength, Ippolit tries to prove that he merely «forgot» to load the gun, and wasn’t staging a farce.
This scene illustrates the ultimate act of «final conviction». Ippolit’s pitiful attempt shows his readiness to die, but not his readiness to kill himself. Even if he had forgotten, he still didn’t load the cap initially, though he justifies it by saying he feared «accidentally shooting himself in the pocket».
Ippolit sharply criticizes Myshkin's ideas, seeing in his kindness and sincerity something naive and pointless in a world that, in his opinion, is steeped in injustice. This contrast represents a symbolic clash between idealism and cynicism. Ippolit seeks to challenge Myshkin, testing his faith in human virtue and compassion. Aware of his inevitable death, Ippolit rejects any attempts by the prince to console him or offer him spiritual salvation. He wants to devalue Myshkin's virtues, as if trying to prove that even someone like the prince cannot save him or change his worldview. Myshkin, despite all of this, continues to show sympathy towards Ippolit. He does not judge him for his cynicism and perceives him as a victim of circumstances, highlighting the humanistic side of the prince's character. Ippolit sees his illness as a symbol of his own defeat and powerlessness in the face of life. His suicide attempt is a climactic moment in which he tries to express his ultimate disappointment in life and protest against the injustice of fate. However, his failed suicide attempt only deepens his sense of helplessness. Myshkin, in turn, does not see evil in Ippolit's actions, but rather an expression of his despair and inner pain. He continues to view him as an unfortunate person whom one could try to help, even if Ippolit himself does not want it. Between Ippolit and Myshkin, a kind of spiritual struggle unfolds. Myshkin offers hope, compassion, and faith in humanity, while Ippolit tries to prove that these concepts are meaningless in the face of impending death. Ippolit despises life and people, but his encounter with Myshkin forces him to question whether it is truly possible to believe in goodness. It is important to note that despite his caustic remarks and attempts to provoke Myshkin, Ippolit, in some way, craves his attention and recognition. He strives not only to prove his point but also, perhaps, to receive a response from Myshkin that would change his worldview, even if he is not fully aware of this desire. The prince feels growing anxiety and melancholy as he wanders through the park. He experiences an internal discord, manifested in contradictory emotions — ranging from laughter to indignation. Myshkin tries to find answers to his feelings but feels detached from the world around him, as if he is an outsider at the «eternal celebration» of life. This detachment and sense of alienation are key themes in the novel, illustrating his inability to integrate into society. The image of the «fly», described by Ippolit in his essay, strikes Myshkin, as he identifies himself with this image. The fly, which «knows its place in the grand choir», symbolizes the harmony of nature, while Myshkin feels excluded from this harmony. For him, this is not just a description of an insect but a metaphor for his own lack of belonging in a world where all beings, except him, have found their place and purpose. His memories of Switzerland emphasize Myshkin's discord with the world. He recalls standing before the endless horizon and feeling his isolation and inability to join this «great celebration». This episode symbolizes his spiritual search and longing to find his place in the world, which he cannot find, despite the natural beauty and harmony around him. He feels that everyone, including the tiny fly, has their role — except for him. Myshkin is astonished by how Ippolit's words, describing a sense of alienation and lack of meaning, seem so familiar to him. He feels as though Ippolit has stolen these thoughts from him. This connects them on a deep level — both experience existential torment, but while Ippolit reacts with pessimism and nihilism, Myshkin tries to find solace in his spirituality and kindness. Myshkin's dream about a woman with the face of a criminal, who evokes contradictory feelings in him, highlights his fear of what he cannot change or control. This dream can be interpreted as a premonition of tragedy or disaster, which is already looming. The woman with a tear and criminal features may symbolize his attitude toward Nastasya Filippovna — a woman whom he does not want to recognize as a criminal, despite her complex and tragic nature. Aglaya, laughing and grabbing his hand at the end, brings Myshkin back from his anxious reflections to the real world. Her laughter and physical presence contrast with his inner fears and anxieties. She represents light and spontaneity, temporarily pulling the prince out of his dark thoughts. This moment underscores the difference between reality and his inner world, where he is in constant struggle with anxieties and premonitions.
Aglaya sees Myshkin sleeping and wakes him up. The main point of their dialogue is Aglaya’s duality, where she teases the prince by claiming she will marry Ganya. She insists that she wants to elope with Myshkin, but when he says it's «nonsense» and talks about Nastasya Filippovna (their conversation arises from the letters Aglaya writes to Nastasya), Aglaya becomes disappointed and, through her words about marrying Ganya, reproaches the prince. Myshkin experiences growing anxiety, which is reflected in his relationship with the letters. His dreams of the «criminal», who calls him to her, symbolize his feelings of guilt and fear of the approaching tragedy. The letters he opens heighten this feeling, plunging him into complex emotions. He perceives them as something nightmarish, almost like a dream, which underscores his disconnection between reality and his inner turmoil. The letters remind him of his own sufferings and emotions, as if he had anticipated their contents long ago. Nastasya Filippovna is portrayed as a tragic figure whose letters are filled with mad, yet painfully sincere thoughts. She acknowledges Myshkin as «perfection» and elevates him, but her love for him and her suffering bring destruction. The letters reflect her inner conflict: she realizes that love for the prince is impossible, yet her passionate feelings give her no peace. Through her letters, her self-deprecation and despair, especially regarding her future with Rogozhin, are revealed. This adds a tragic shade to her character — she foresees her doom but cannot escape it. Nastasya views love as the highest form of sacrifice, especially in relation to Myshkin. Her belief that he should love her as a «pure spirit», without selfishness, highlights her desire for an idealized love, free from earthly desires. However, her passion and jealousy make this impossible. Meanwhile, Myshkin tries to help her, but his inability to cope with her emotions and suffering leads to his own crisis. Rogozhin symbolizes the fatal force driving events towards a tragic end. His relationships with Nastasya Filippovna and Myshkin are full of tension and jealousy. He mocks the prince’s suffering and foresees the inevitable conclusion. His behavior shows that he is aware of his role in Nastasya’s fate and his inability to save her, despite his love for her. Nastasya’s despair and belief in her own demise reflect a sense of the inevitable tragedy. The meeting between Myshkin, Nastasya, and Rogozhin emphasizes this fatalism. Rogozhin bitterly asks the prince if he is happy, and this scene symbolizes the impossibility of happiness for everyone involved. The tragedy lies in the fact that each of them feels powerless in the face of their emotions and circumstances. «— Well, goodbye, — said Rogozhin, — for I’ll be leaving tomorrow too; remember me without ill will! But what, brother, — he added, quickly turning around, — why didn’t you answer her? ‘Are you happy or not?’» «— No, no, no! — cried the prince with boundless sorrow». «— As if you’d have said "yes’! — Rogozhin laughed maliciously and walked away without looking back».
Part IV
Ordinariness and uniqueness
Dostoevsky reflects on «ordinary» people and their role in society, revealing the tension between the everyday and the exceptional. He explores how the majority of people are considered «ordinary» and how this majority constitutes the main fabric of daily life. Dostoevsky contrasts these people with literary characters, who often represent exaggerated or rare qualities, standing out more vividly against the backdrop of ordinary life.
Dostoevsky argues that «ordinary» people often go unnoticed in both life and literature. Although they may lack remarkable talents, ideas, or eccentricities, they form the core connections of everyday life. However, in literature, there is a tendency to depict types or exaggerate certain traits, as in the case of Podkolyosin in Gogol’s works. This allows readers to recognize and classify the people they already know but haven’t been able to name. The narrator asserts that such typification is a common literary strategy, whereas real people often exist in a more «diluted» form. Ganya represents the «intelligent» ordinary person, struggling with the desire to be unique. This drives him to seek recognition through risky decisions, but each time he retreats due to his caution and sense of decency. The internal tension between the urge to be someone special and the realization of his limitations causes him deep suffering. This reflection shows how the thirst for originality can even push a person toward morally questionable actions, revealing the complexity of human ambitions. Varvara, Ganya's sister, is depicted as more practical and balanced. While she also aspires to a life beyond the mundane, she accepts her limitations and acts accordingly. Her marriage to Ptitsyn was a calculated step — she values his honesty and ability to provide her with stability, despite his lack of ambition. Unlike Ganya, she does not chase grand dreams of originality but focuses on achieving realistic goals. This makes her more resilient to the disappointments that plague her brother.
Varya informs Ganya that Prince Myshkin is now officially Aglaia’s fiancé, putting an end to his attempts to win her. However, to his sister's surprise, this news does not provoke an emotional outburst from Ganya. Instead of being upset, Ganya responds philosophically, which may indicate his inner exhaustion and perhaps the realization of his own futility in this situation. Ganya was not seeking happiness with Aglaia, and for him, it was more of a way to assert himself or gain a certain status. Ganya displays sarcastic humor while discussing his failure with Aglaia, revealing his inner detachment and sense of failure. He feels humiliated and despises himself for his failure, yet he laughs at it. This combination of sarcasm and self-destructive irony reveals his deep internal crisis, related to his inability to achieve his ambitions. Ganya expresses disdain not only for his father, whom he considers a drunkard and a thief, but also for Varvara's husband, Ptitsyn, calling him a moneylender. His words reveal deep dissatisfaction with his social standing and the people around him. He believes that it is this environment and those around him that are holding him back from success. Ganya also harbors resentment toward Ippolit, whom he sees as a schemer and an enemy. He suspects that Ippolit is deliberately spreading rumors and turning others against him. This conflict with Ippolit shows how Ganya sees enemies in anyone who threatens his position, even in those who are sick and dying. Ganya despises Ippolit for his weakness, but at the same time, fears his influence.
Madness
General Ivolgin, who has long shown signs of mental instability, enters into a sharp conflict with Ippolit. The general accuses Ippolit of being like a «screw», drilling into his soul. This metaphor reveals how helpless the general feels in the face of Ippolit’s silent contempt and mocking attitude. Ippolit, in turn, does not hold back and responds with biting remarks, further intensifying the situation. This confrontation symbolizes a generational struggle, where the older generation loses touch with reality, while the younger generation, suffering from illness and depression, harbors deep disdain for the adult world.
General Ivolgin appears as a tragic figure. He rambles, confuses words and thoughts, and his accusations against Ippolit seem both absurd and painful. The general retreats from reality, creating images and stories in his mind that those around him no longer take seriously. For instance, his tale about Captain Yeropogov (who likely does not exist) becomes the subject of mockery from both Ippolit and even his own son, Ganya. This highlights how the general is no longer respected within his own family, losing control of the situation. The family conflicts are especially evident in this episode. Ganya openly expresses contempt for his father, showing irritation and even anger. He accuses his father of «disgracing» their family, effectively challenging his authority as the head of the household. At this moment, it becomes clear that Ganya has no respect for his father and is willing to confront him, despite their familial ties. At the same time, Nina Alexandrovna, Ganya's mother, trembles and weeps, showing how deeply the situation tears her apart.
Ippolit, though gravely ill, plays the role of a catalyst for the family tension. He provokes the general with his taunts, essentially triggering the general’s outburst of rage. While Ippolit outwardly appears calm, his words deeply wound the general, and he seems to derive some satisfaction from it. Ippolit symbolizes nihilism and the rejection of traditional values, embodying the cynicism and disillusionment of the younger generation.
Despite his grievances toward his father, Ganya demonstrates inner conflict. He despises the general and doesn’t hesitate to show his indignation, but when it comes to direct confrontation, Ganya still holds back. His words and behavior reveal a mixture of fear, shame, and anger, highlighting his weakness as a person. He calls his father «mad», which shows how lowly he regards him, yet Ganya is also afraid of the consequences of such an attitude, especially if others find out about it.
The general, whose mental state continues to deteriorate, decides to leave the house after another conflict, triggered by mockery and doubts about the existence of Captain Yeropogov. This symbolizes the crisis of his personality and his loss of control over the situation. His outburst, «My curse upon this house!» underscores his theatricality and tragedy. The general is no longer capable of perceiving reality accurately and sinks deeper into his illusions.
Ippolit, though physically weak, remains a careful and cold-blooded observer of the events. He skillfully provokes Ganya, acting as a catalyst for the tension in the house. Despite his physical frailty, Ippolit demonstrates sharp intellect and sarcasm, which infuriates Ganya. Ippolit uses mockery to show his disdain for Ganya, whom he considers the embodiment of «vulgar ordinariness». His cold demeanor and calm discussion of serious matters contrast with the emotional instability of the other characters, making his figure even more tragic.
Ganya is on the verge of a breakdown. His outbursts of anger directed at Ippolit and his father show how much the family problems and personal failures torment him. He suffers from feelings of humiliation and envy, but he is unable to control his rage. Ganya accuses Ippolit of manipulating the situation and provoking others into conflict. However, Ganya himself is vulnerable to Ippolit’s taunts, which further emphasizes his weakness.
The confrontation between Ganya and Ippolit reaches its peak. Ippolit openly accuses Ganya of trying to use him for his own purposes — particularly, in his desire to oppose Prince Myshkin. This exposes Ganya’s hypocrisy and envy, as he seeks success at any cost. Though suffering from illness, Ippolit finds the strength to sarcastically mock Ganya, calling him the embodiment of «ordinariness» and «routine». This highlights the conflict between Ippolit’s inner world, filled with suffering and philosophical reflections, and Ganya’s world, dominated by ambition and envy.
Varya, Ganya’s sister, acts as a mediator in this conflict. She tries to calm the escalating situation and prevent her brother from fighting with Ippolit. However, her efforts prove futile, and she essentially becomes a witness to how Ippolit masterfully destroys her brother’s self-esteem. Despite her attempts to keep peace, Varya realizes that the situation is spiraling out of control and expresses her disappointment in her brother, understanding his weakness before Ippolit.
An important element is the note from Aglaya Epanchina, who suggests meeting with Ganya. This letter becomes a glimmer of hope for Ganya, offering a possible success in his relationship with Aglaya. However, the self-satisfaction he feels upon receiving the letter sharply contrasts with Ippolit’s humiliating predictions of his future failures. Ganya sees the letter as a chance for redemption and victory, but his reaction also betrays his insecurity and fear of potential failure.
General Ivolgin is on the verge of a mental and emotional breakdown. While he had previously shown signs of instability, his behavior is now becoming increasingly erratic and enigmatic. The general is torn between his old habits and a new, more anxious state. He has become more irritable and suspicious, his speech is disjointed, he interrupts sentences, and his thoughts jump from one to another. All of this points to a worsening mental disorder, which intensifies the tension within the family. Every family member, including Kolya, Nina Alexandrovna, and even the prince, notices that something strange is happening, but no one dares to openly address it. Kolya suspects that something is wrong with his father, possibly related to alcohol or his quarrel with Lebedev, but he cannot get a straight answer from either the prince or anyone else involved. The general is clearly experiencing an internal crisis, but its causes remain hidden.
Lebedev plays a key role in maintaining the general’s mental instability. It is revealed that Ivolgin had stolen Lebedev’s wallet containing four hundred rubles. The general is tormented by guilt, which is why he leaves the wallet in a visible spot — under the chair where Lebedev’s coat hangs — but Lebedev decides to toy with the general's emotions, purposely leaving the wallet where it is. Two days later, the wallet ends up in the «left side of the coat», having fallen there through a torn pocket. The prince pleads with Lebedev to stop tormenting the general, even though Lebedev claims he «loves him dearly». Lebedev listens to the prince and stops the mischief, eventually admitting that he «found the wallet».
In the end, the tragic figure of General Ivolgin suffers a «stroke». This occurs after his dialogue with Myshkin, in which he recounts his supposed encounter with the most significant figure of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth century — Napoleon. Ivolgin, before telling his story, declares that he has renounced Lebedev for mocking him, and the conversation ends with him cutting ties with the prince as well. Ivolgin deeply believes in what he says, even if his words are absurd. In earlier chapters, we had already touched on the full extent of his tragedy (Part II, Chapter VI: «General Ivolgin's Truth»), but in these concluding chapters of the novel, Ivolgin's lies lead to his inevitable end — the «stroke». Even lies can no longer «save» him from the harsh external reality he has been trying so hard to escape. His final refuge is Kolya, in whom he sees his last hope, calling his son «le roi de Rome», the very phrase Napoleon used to describe his son (who was said to have «not loved him») in Ivolgin’s myth. In the end, everything becomes confused, and the general begins to speak complete nonsense, which signals the severity of his mental and physical condition.
Myshkin's Capacity for Love
After everything that happened between Aglaya and Myshkin, Lizaveta Prokofyevna reflects on the prince, still troubled by the possibility that «such a fool might marry Aglaya». The prince had visited the Epanchins, and Aglaya kept him waiting for thirty minutes. When she finally came out, she immediately suggested they play chess. It turned out that Myshkin didn’t even know the basics — Aglaya defeated him easily and mocked the prince for his «stupidity». Afterward, Aglaya proposed they play a card game, Durak. Here, the situation was completely reversed, with the prince playing cards like a «professor». No matter how hard Aglaya tried, Myshkin effortlessly won. This upset Aglaya, and she became very angry with the prince, who left feeling downcast, as if he were «leaving a funeral». Fifteen minutes later, Aglaya came downstairs with tear-filled eyes, because Kolya had arrived with a hedgehog. Aglaya began begging Kolya to sell her the hedgehog. The situation, although confusing, was eventually resolved, and Aglaya bought the hedgehog, then asked Kolya to take it to the prince as a gift and a «gesture of her deepest respect». In this context, the hedgehog symbolizes Myshkin’s emotional simplicity and pure-hearted kindness.
Myshkin himself had fallen deeply and irrevocably in love with Aglaya. Although she behaves carelessly and mockingly towards him, this is her defense mechanism; she doesn’t want those close to her to know that she is in love with an «idiot» (this was mentioned earlier as well, though I can't find exactly where >3).
Myshkin Reaches «Light»
For the first time in his life, he saw a glimpse of what is referred to by the fearful name of «society».
Myshkin enters «high society», an experience that fills him with incredible joy. To him, it seems as though everyone who «came to the Epanchins' house this evening» is the best of friends, as if they’ve known each other for a thousand years and had just seen one another yesterday: they talk pleasantly and drink tea together. But this «light» blinds Myshkin, and he fails to see the darkness of the people around him. This takes us back to the triple division of Myshkin’s worldview: «full shadow», «penumbra», and «light». Myshkin’s position in this setting falls into «penumbra» — he stands on the threshold between «light» and «shadow», but he doesn't realize that he is surrounded by darkness while he alone shines like the brightest star. The so-called «high society» is merely a facade; each person plays the simple role of a «close friend» to everyone else, but secretly, they harbor disdain for their «friends». Unfortunately, Myshkin doesn’t see this reality.
Myshkin’s sharp reaction to the news that Pavlishchev had converted to Catholicism demonstrates his deep religious convictions. He perceives Catholicism as the opposite of true Christianity, even as a threat equal to, or greater than, atheism. Myshkin sees Catholicism not as a religion, but as a political system based on power and violence. To him, Catholicism has lost its spirituality and transformed into a worldly power, which is unacceptable to the prince. His emotional response highlights the depth of his beliefs and his inability to calmly face what he sees as a threat to Christianity as he understands it.
It's important to note the contrast between Prince Myshkin’s reaction and that of the other characters in the scene. While Myshkin speaks with extraordinary passion and inner conviction, those around him — especially Ivan Petrovich and the elderly gentleman — respond with surprise, misunderstanding, and even boredom. Their reactions express skepticism and weariness toward Myshkin’s strong opinions, further highlighting his isolation and perceived inadequacy in the eyes of society. This is confirmed by the old man’s laughter and Ivan Petrovich’s cold tone.
Myshkin connects Catholicism with the social problems of Europe, including atheism and socialism. He believes that these phenomena are the result of the Catholic Church’s spiritual crisis, having forsaken true Christian values in favor of power. His claim that atheism «originated from Roman Catholicism» is linked to his belief that the Catholic Church’s ideals have been distorted and destroyed, leading to the rejection of faith. He also points out that socialism, like atheism, arises from this loss of faith and spiritual foundation. Myshkin sees socialism as another form of violence aimed at replacing spirituality with secular power.
In his speech, Myshkin emphasizes that the cause of the spiritual crisis is a thirst for truth and spirituality, which he believes is especially pronounced in the Russian people. He speaks of the Russian person, who, having lost faith in his homeland and traditions, finds himself in a spiritual void, making him an easy prey for extreme ideologies — Catholicism, atheism, socialism. Myshkin views this as a tragedy for the Russian people, who, unable to find spiritual support in their native culture, seek it in extreme and foreign doctrines. He calls for the revival of Russian spirituality and ideals, which could become a foundation not only for Russia but for the entire world.
Myshkin’s speech also contains the idea of Russian messianism, the belief that Russia, as the guardian of the true Christ, must offer the world spiritual renewal. He prophesies that the Russian people, once they find their spiritual «shore», will become a great and wise nation capable of showing the world the path to salvation. This idea reflects Dostoevsky’s views on Russia’s role in the future and its mission as the keeper of true Christian values. However, this prophecy also highlights the naive and utopian nature of Myshkin’s beliefs, which are not shared by those around him.
After his speech, Myshkin accidentally drops the vase that Aglaya had warned him about before the evening gathering. Myshkin froze and fell silent, but the reaction of those around him caused cognitive dissonance — rather than scolding him, they patted him on the back and laughed good-naturedly. Myshkin snapped out of his trance and began to speak about the troubling thoughts that had haunted him before his «entry into society». He explains that he was convinced that there were no longer any educated and sincere people left in high society, but this situation had proven him completely wrong. Unfortunately, Myshkin became too agitated and fell into an epileptic seizure. After the gathering, everyone was dissatisfied and called Myshkin a «foolish suitor» for Aglaya, though they also confirmed that he was «wise» and «good enough».
Apotheosis
Myshkin learns from Ippolit that Aglaya plans to meet Nastasya Filippovna in the evening, which explains why Aglaya had strictly forbidden him to leave the house until nine o'clock in the evening.
The entire scene at Nastasya's home serves as the climax of the novel, leading to irreversible consequences. This scene, set in Nastasya's drawing room, is the confrontation between two women, both deeply in love with the same man. Nastasya and Aglaya are remarkably similar — one refuses to be with Myshkin because she sees herself as «unclean», and the other because the «high society» around her might react negatively to her decision (which is why, in previous chapters, she had suggested that Myshkin run away with her from her own home).
Rogozhin and Myshkin become mere observers of this conflict, this clash between two forces that are so alike and yet so incredibly different. Only at the very end is Myshkin allowed to speak, faced with a difficult choice: salvation or love. Myshkin declares, «— Is this even possible? After all, she... she’s so miserable!» — these are the key words of the novel, words that lead to the tragic consequences of the entire story. Myshkin, due to his «savior complex», chooses Nastasya, even though he truly loves Aglaya. For him, salvation takes precedence, while love is secondary. In this way, he is like Jesus, whose primary goal was salvation, for only through salvation can love be offered (for Myshkin loves not just Nastasya as a woman, but everyone, out of his inherent kindness). Ascending to the world of «light», Myshkin is met with a blow that forces him to step back into the «penumbra» for someone like him has no place in an imperfect society.
Rogozhin remains a silent observer throughout the scene. He says nothing, and at Nastasya's command, he leaves, allowing Myshkin and his beloved to be alone.
After everything that has happened, Myshkin is tormented by his situation. He realizes that he «fears Nastasya’s face» because «she is insane». But within him, two opposing forces clash: fear and the need for salvation. He longs to help the woman who is drowning in her own self-hatred, a woman who, through her suffering and situation, is so much like Myshkin himself.
Nastasya Filippovna's entire being is a clash of two opposing personalities, one of which is drawn to Rogozhin (materialism, fatalism, nihilism, sadism, and masochism), and the other to Myshkin (absolutism, morality, salvation, spirituality). In the end, under the pressure of the society surrounding her (and, of course, not only that, but also her sense of guilt towards Myshkin, as she believes she burdens him with her presence), Nastasya once again flees from the altar to Rogozhin.
A terrible clash of fates. The suicide of three: completed, incomplete, and entangled — thesis, antithesis, and synthesis. A return to the original state: «Idiot!»
The entire conclusion of «The Idiot» is built on the fact that three entirely different people, sharing the same fate, collide. Rogozhin and Myshkin are bound by Nastasya, who embraces fatalism. Her death becomes inevitable after she leaves Myshkin for Rogozhin, and perhaps even earlier — at the climax of the novel. This marks the end of three lives, none of whom desired such an outcome, but each, lost in themselves, led the others to that fatal room. The very knife that Myshkin worried about back in the second part became the instrument of the murder of the woman who feared for her life around Rogozhin throughout the novel. Myshkin, upon seeing Nastasya’s face, predicted her tragic fate, though he never would have guessed how deeply they would become intertwined... or did he?
Perhaps Myshkin withdrew from this imperfect world, perhaps he is the very Antichrist, or perhaps he is the purest man, a Christ-figure who couldn’t bear the vices of this world and finally uttered that fateful phrase at the climax of the work. Myshkin longed to return to the state in which his old friend and doctor Schneider would wave him off and say, just as certainly as before: «Idiot!»
Rogozhin was driven mad by a madwoman, Myshkin by a mad world, and yet the three of them form a single whole, for all of them can be described in the same terms as Myshkin. The prince no longer resembles a person; he is no longer human at all, but something transcendent, sacrificing himself for the sake of... but for what? And that question remains unanswered. Although, there is an answer, a deeply tragic one that has already been voiced: Myshkin sacrificed himself for the sake of himself. Better to live in complete darkness than in the light. Not knowing this world allows one to remain pure; acquaintance with it creates the desire to return to ignorance. This is the collision of the perfect with the imperfect, and if it is possible that we live in «the best of worlds», it certainly does not seem that way from the perspective of the purest of men.
