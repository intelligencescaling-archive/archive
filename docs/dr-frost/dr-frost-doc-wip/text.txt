The Bar Scene

At the start of the chapter, we meet a couple entering a bar. Inside the bar, there’s Dr. Frost, who works as the receptionist. The boyfriend greets another receptionist there, showing that they know each other. They seem comfortable in the environment as they settle in and order some drinks. After a while, the other receptionist, likely trying to make conversation, asks the boyfriend, "Who's the lady?" The boyfriend then introduces the girl, explaining that she's his girlfriend and that they've been dating for two months. It seems like the relationship is fairly new, but serious enough for them to be spending time together in this kind of setting.

As time passes, the scene shifts to a more intimate moment between the couple. The boyfriend, trying to show affection, presents his girlfriend with a ring. The girl, clearly surprised by the gesture, mentions that the ring looks quite expensive. She seems to appreciate it, but there’s a slight hesitation in her response. She tells him, “I would like to give you something in return…” but as she says this, she covers her mouth with her hand. This small action seems innocent, but it’s an important clue that will come up later. It suggests that maybe she’s not being entirely honest, or at least that she’s holding something back. The boyfriend, however, doesn’t seem to notice or mind. He reassures her, saying that it’s okay and that they have the rest of their lives to take care of each other. His words are filled with hope and commitment, but they may not be received the way he expects. The girl responds by calling him cute, and she reaches out to touch his ears, an act of affection that again might seem natural on the surface, but carries more meaning than it appears.

While all of this is happening, Dr. Frost is quietly observing the scene. He’s not just watching like any other person; his demeanor suggests that he’s analyzing the situation. There’s something about the couple’s interaction that makes him suspicious, as though he can already sense that things aren’t quite what they seem. He stays silent, but he’s thinking deeply about what might really be going on between them. His experience with reading people’s behaviors seems to put him on alert, even though there’s no obvious sign of trouble at this point.

As more time passes, the girl’s attention shifts to the wall behind the counter. She notices that the wall has strange symbols and drawings on it, and she comments that they look like masks. This might seem like a casual observation, but to Dr. Frost, it’s much more significant. The mention of masks triggers something in his mind, confirming suspicions that he had already begun to form. He doesn’t say anything just yet, but internally, he’s piecing together clues that the boyfriend seems completely unaware of.

At this point, Frost takes note of the girl’s body language. Specifically, he observes that she’s sitting with her legs crossed, and they’re pointed toward the exit. This is another subtle but important detail. According to Frost’s understanding, people’s body positions often reflect their mental state, even when they’re not consciously aware of it. By crossing her legs in the direction of the exit, the girl is unconsciously signaling that she’s not fully invested in the conversation or the relationship, and might even be thinking about leaving.

Shortly after this, the girl receives a text message and tells her boyfriend that she needs to step outside to make a call. The boyfriend, still oblivious to any problems, lets her go without any concern. As she leaves, he starts thinking to himself about how lucky he is to have found someone like her. In his mind, everything is going well, and he feels genuinely fortunate to be with her. Unfortunately for him, this happy thought is soon shattered. Dr. Frost, having observed the situation closely, interrupts and tells the boyfriend that his girlfriend won’t be coming back. His statement is blunt and shocking, but he says it with complete certainty. The boyfriend, completely taken aback, can’t understand how Frost could possibly know this. He had no reason to doubt her, and everything seemed fine to him.

Frost then explains that the truth was there all along, in the girl’s behavior and body language. He tells the boyfriend that in this bar, people’s thoughts reveal themselves unconsciously, as if they were looking into a mirror. Frost’s sharp ability to read these hidden signals allowed him to see through the surface of the couple’s interaction and understand what was really going on.

Frost goes on to break down the details that led him to his conclusion. First, he points out that the temperature in the bar is always kept at a comfortable 24 degrees, yet the girl was sitting with her legs crossed. This is an unnatural posture for someone who is supposed to be relaxed in such an environment. Frost explains that body language can reflect a person’s true thoughts. In this case, the girl’s closed posturecrossed legswas a clear sign that she was not mentally open to her boyfriend, even though she hadn’t said anything outright.

Another clue, according to Frost, was the position of the girl’s purse. He explains that when a woman first meets someone, she tends to keep her purse close because it’s a personal and valuable item. However, as a relationship deepens and trust builds, she becomes comfortable with her partner being near it. But in this case, even after two months of dating, the girl still kept her purse away from her boyfriend. This indicated that she hadn’t grown comfortable or close enough to him, suggesting emotional distance between them.

Frost admits that at first, he considered the possibility that the couple might have had a fight before coming to the bar, which could explain the girl’s cold behavior. But he quickly ruled this out by observing how the girl reacted to receiving the ring. When she was given the ring, her eyes dilated, which is a physical reaction that shows genuine happiness. However, when the boyfriend talked about spending the rest of their lives together, her body language changed. Frost noticed that she touched her ears when he said this, which is a common unconscious gesture people make when they hear something they don’t want to hear. This subtle action told Frost that she wasn’t as enthusiastic about the idea of a long-term commitment as her boyfriend believed.

Another detail Frost picked up on was the girl’s facial expressions. Although her mouth smiled when she received the ring, her eyes did not. This lack of alignment between her smile and her eyes was a sign of insincerity. True happiness involves the whole face, especially the eyes, and the fact that her eyes didn’t reflect her smile showed that her emotions weren’t genuine.

The final piece of evidence came when the girl commented on the masks she saw on the wall. Frost explains that the wall is actually a psychological test created by a famous psychologist, designed to reveal something about a person’s subconscious. The fact that the girl saw masks was significant because masks symbolize hiding or concealing something. This observation reflected her inner desire to hide the truth, reinforcing Frost’s belief that she wasn’t being honest with her boyfriend.

In the end, all of these small clues added up to a clear conclusion: the girl wasn’t committed to the relationship, and she had been giving off signals of this throughout the evening, even if her boyfriend couldn’t see them. Frost’s ability to read these unconscious behaviors allowed him to predict that she would leave, and sure enough, she did. When the girl texted her boyfriend saying she wasn’t coming back, it only confirmed everything that Frost had already known.

The Therapy Center
Dr. Frost, or as he's also known, as Baek, is invited by Professor Oh to join a therapy center as a way to test his skills. The professor sets up this trial period so that if Frost can demonstrate good results with clients, he’ll have the opportunity to move up to a higher position within the center. Essentially, it's a test to see how effective his methods are in practice.

On Frost’s first day at the therapy center, he meets his office assistant, Yoong. Right away, he instructs her to move the clock in the room. He explains that its current location would be distracting for clients and make it harder for them to focus during their sessions. He believes that having the clock too visible would cause clients to unconsciously track the time, taking their attention away from the therapy. To avoid this, he tells Yoong that the clock should be placed in a corner, where it’s out of the client’s sight. This way, only Frost will be able to check the time without the client noticing. He also advises Yoong not to wear a wristwatch during sessions, as checking the time in front of the client would suggest a lack of interest in their story, something that could harm the therapeutic environment.

As the two continue to talk, Yoong, eager to learn more about Dr. Frost, asks him questions to get to know him better. Frost’s responses are curious and a bit unconventional, quickly drawing Yoong into a deeper discussion. At one point, Frost makes a striking statement that leaves her puzzled. He says that it’s a myth that people can truly "understand" one another's feelings. This remark immediately catches Yoong’s attention, and she asks him what he means by that.

In response, Baek quotes a passage from one of his favorite books, a source of wisdom for him. The conclusion of the excerpt is clear and direct: "There is no definition of empathy or understanding of each other. Human beings cannot have empathy for other people. It's all an illusion." By this, Frost means that the idea of empathy, the ability to feel what another person is feeling is an illusion. In his view, it’s impossible for one person to completely understand the emotions or inner experience of another. People might think they understand each other, but that connection is never as deep as it seems.

Baek goes on to explain how this belief affects his approach to therapy. Instead of trying to "empathize" with his clients, Frost relies on data. He gathers information from other cases and uses it as a reference when trying to help new clients. His method is scientific and detached, based on facts rather than feelings. He doesn’t believe in trying to "feel" what the client is feeling, but rather in using objective information to figure out how their minds work. Indirectly, this also reveals something about Baek himself: his mind is like a library of data, full of knowledge and case studies that he draws upon in every session. This collection of information allows him to solve many cases and help his clients, even if his approach is more clinical and less emotional than one might expect from a therapist.

This explanation frustrates Yoong, as her own view of therapy is completely different. She believes that therapists should be empathetic and connect emotionally with their clients. To her, this emotional connection is essential in helping people open up and heal. She gets angry when she hears Frost’s impersonal view of therapy, because it clashes so strongly with her own beliefs. In her opinion, therapy isn’t just about solving problems logically it's also about understanding and caring for people on a human level. However, despite her frustration, Frost doesn’t argue with her. He simply says that her way of thinking is valid, but it’s not the way he works. For him, his method is based on data and logic, not emotional involvement.

Frost also makes another surprising claim during their conversation: he says that he doesn’t understand emotions. This isn’t just about not using emotions in therapy he means that he genuinely doesn’t know what emotions feel like. He even hints that he doesn’t really experience emotions himself, or if he does, he feels them at a much lower level than most people. This admission adds a new layer to Frost’s character, suggesting that his detachment from emotion isn’t just a professional choice, but part of who he is as a person.

The difference in their views causes tension between Frost and Yoong. While she values empathy and emotional connection, Frost values objectivity and detachment. He doesn’t dismiss her point of view, but it’s clear that he won’t change his methods. For Yoong, this realization is frustrating, because she believes that a therapist needs to connect with their clients emotionally to be effective. For Frost, however, solving the problem logically and understanding the client through data is enough. This contrast between them highlights the central theme of the scene: the clash between emotional understanding and intellectual problem-solving in therapy.

The Empty Man

Not long after the previous situation, the man from the beginning of the story reappears. Earlier, Dr. Frost had given him a business card at the bar, offering to help him with something that seemed to trouble him. They engage in conversation, and during this time, Frost, who had already figured out that the man worked at a pharmacy, notices something unusual. He sees that the soles of the young man's shoes are quite worn, which means that he walks a lot, likely to make sales. He then observes the timeit’s 4 PMand remarks to himself about the fact that the man is here in the middle of a weekday.

“If you’ve got so much free time that you can come here in the middle of the week, you must work outdoors quite often,” Frost thought. His suspicions grow when he notices that the young man is using a special pen. This pen is only given to elite employees at Sangdong Pharmaceuticals, a detail that adds to the picture Frost is forming in his mind.

When they officially start the therapy session, Dr. Frost immediately picks up on the client’s defensive posture. As usual, Frost tries to extract as much information as he can about the young man’s situation. After hearing about the problems the man faces, Frost suggests a test known as the "Twenty Statements Test" (TST), where the client is required to write 20 sentences, each beginning with "I...," describing things he likes or identifies with. Normally, this test only takes around five minutes, but Frost purposely omits this detail, letting the man take as much time as he wants to complete it. Afterward, the young man is asked to take another test, this time a much longer one called the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI).

The MMPI consists of 567 questions and typically takes about an hour and a half to complete. However, the man, claiming he’s too busy, finishes it quickly and hands in the test early without answering all the questions. As he leaves, he demands that Dr. Frost get the results to him promptly, showing a sense of entitlement and impatience.

After the client leaves, Frost asks his assistant, Yoon, to print out the results from the test. While doing so, Yoon thinks to herself that Frost is always complaining. To her surprise, Frost responds to this unspoken thought by telling her, “You better get used to it if you want to be a psychologist.” This remark reveals that Frost has an uncanny ability to read people’s minds, or at least their body language, so well that it’s as if he knows what they are thinking.

After a while, the test results are ready. Frost carefully reads through them, and afterward, he decides to test Yoon’s capabilities by asking her what she thinks about the case. Yoon gives her analysis, which matches what Frost had already concluded: the man is a classic narcissist. Dr. Frost had picked up on this long before the results confirmed it, based on the client’s behavior and answers.

Once all the tests are analyzed, Frost concludes that in order to fully understand the man, he needs to look beyond the surface and dig deeper into his personal life. Frost then devises a somewhat questionable plan: he decides to break into the client’s house to gather more information, and he plans to use Yoon as bait to lure the man away from home. The plan is that Yoon will call the client, Oh Jeonghyuk, and set up a meeting under the pretense of asking him more questions for an interview. She will have to pretend that she’s romantically interested in him, as Frost explains that men are more likely to believe that a woman they’re dating is in love with them than the other way around.

While Yoon distracts Oh on their “date,” Frost will break into his house to better understand his way of life. Frost doesn’t seem the least bit uncomfortable with the idea of breaking into a client’s home, despite how ethically questionable it may be. Yoon, however, is completely against the idea, but she still obeys Frost’s instructions, showing that outside of what we see, Frost has manipulated and persuaded her to go along with the plan.

Following the plan, the scene shifts to the execution. At first, Oh doesn’t fall for Yoon’s bait and remains skeptical. But Baek had anticipated this and had already prepared Yoon for the possibility. He had instructed her to keep her legs crossed in a particular way and to let one shoe hang loosely from her foot. She was also told to laugh while showing her wrist or to tuck her hair behind her ears so that her pulse would be visible. Frost even told her to wear a skirt for the occasion. Essentially, Yoon’s job is to seduce Oh as much as possible to keep him distracted.

While Yoon works on distracting Oh, Frost sneaks into Oh’s house. However, there’s a problem: the front door is locked with a password-protected system. To gain entry, Frost tries guessing the password. On his first attempt, he enters Oh’s birthday, but that doesn’t work. His second guess is an important date in Oh’s life, but that fails as well. It’s only on his third attempt that Frost successfully enters the house, although the password isn’t revealed at this point.

But my theory on this:

Frost’s process of abductive reasoning in discovering Oh’s password:

P1: Frost’s first clue comes from his immediate understanding of Oh’s personality. From the start, Frost realizes that Oh is a classic narcissist. This is evident in the way Oh responds to questions and interacts with others. Throughout the therapy session, and especially in the results of the psychological tests like the TST (Twenty Statements Test), Oh’s answers reveal that his primary focus is on boasting about himself and his achievements. Frost had already detected this narcissism before even looking at the test results, meaning that he had a clear idea of who Oh was deep down. This insight plays a crucial role in Frost’s reasoning, as it provides a framework for understanding what is most important to Oh: his accomplishments and the validation he receives from others, particularly his mother. This ties into the earlier analysis where Frost realized that Oh’s world revolved around his achievements and external validation, particularly his mother’s praise, which shaped his entire identity.

P2: As Frost continues to investigate, he encounters further confirmation of his theory about Oh when he sees the family photo album. Upon viewing the album, Frost comments that it was “exactly what he expected.” This simple statement holds significant weight, as it indicates that Frost had already developed a hypothesis about Oh’s emotional connections and relationships before even stepping into the apartment. The family album serves as a key piece of evidence that reinforces Frost’s understanding of Oh. The fact that Oh’s family photos align with Frost’s expectations suggests that Frost had already begun to piece together the emotional significance of Oh’s mother in his life, even before encountering the password-protected door. The photos provide visual confirmation of Oh’s narcissism and his need for approval, particularly from his mother, which had become a core aspect of his self-worth and identity.

P3: When Frost encounters the password-protected door, he takes a step back and reflects on what kind of password Oh might choose. Frost states that there are two key criteria for selecting a password: it must be something deeply personal and important enough that it can never be forgotten. This observation leads Frost to narrow down the possible options for the password. For someone like Oh, whose entire sense of self revolves around his achievements and the validation of others, the password would likely be connected to a defining moment in his lifeone that is both highly personal and emotionally charged. Frost knows that Oh’s life has been dominated by the pursuit of external validation, and so the password must be tied to an event that disrupted this pursuit and left a lasting emotional impact.

P4: Frost’s understanding of Oh’s psychology, reinforced by the TST results, leads him to conclude that the most memorable moment in Oh’s life was being praised by his mother. Throughout Oh’s development, his mother’s approval had been the driving force behind his achievements and sense of self-worth. Oh’s responses to the TST revealed that his identity was hollow, filled only with the validation he received from others, particularly his mother. This lack of internal substance meant that Oh had nothing to cling to except his accomplishments, which he pursued in order to continue receiving the praise that validated his existence. However, when his mother passed away, the source of this validation was cut off, leaving Oh unable to receive the praise and love he once thrived on. Her death became the most significant emotional event in his life, marking the end of the only meaningful connection he had. This realization leads Frost to conclude that the password must be tied to this moment specifically, the date of his mother’s death.

C: From all of these observations, Frost arrives at the conclusion that the password is the date of Oh’s mother’s death. Throughout the session and his investigation, Frost pieced together clues about Oh’s narcissism, his dependence on external validation, and the emotional significance of his mother’s praise. The family photo album confirmed that Oh’s identity was largely shaped by his mother’s approval, and her death represented the moment when that approval was lost forever. This is why Frost believes that the password would be linked to this event. It is the most personal and important moment in Oh’s life, a memory that he could never forget because it symbolizes both the end of his source of validation and the emotional void that he has been trying to fill ever since.(Huge Creds to Chronos)

Inside the house, Frost begins to analyze the space. He explains to the readers how a person’s home is filled with hidden details that reveal a lot about their personality and lifestyle. While this is happening, we see that Yoon is keeping Oh engaged in conversation. Frost comments that it’s exactly what he expected, though he doesn’t explain why at the moment.

Just as Oh reaches the final corner before his apartment, Frost sees something on his phone that makes him decide to leave the house quickly. As soon as Oh rounds the corner, he finds Frost casually waiting for him, pretending to be there for a scheduled appointment.

Frost explains in detail how the test works and how Oh’s subconscious thoughts were reflected in his responses.

P1: Once Oh arrives at his apartment, Frost formally asks to enter and is allowed in. He begins by explaining the purpose of the Twenty Statements Test (TST). Typically, as one progresses through the assessment, their answers evolve from surface-level, social statements to deeper, more introspective reflections about their feelings and identity.

P2: Frost intentionally did not impose the usual five-minute time limit for Oh to complete the TST. This was done to test his earlier suspicion that Oh was emotionally hollow and unable to engage in deeper self-reflection.

P3: It took Oh four times longer than the standard five-minute limit to write 20 statements, suggesting that he struggled with the task.

P4: Frost views Oh’s living room as a "name tag"—a space designed to showcase how someone wishes to present themselves to others. Oh’s living room, much like his statements, reveals only superficial traits, reinforcing the idea that there is nothing deeper to display.

P5: Considering everything we’ve learned about Oh’s personality so far, it becomes clear that his inability to move beyond superficial statements in the TST reflects a broader truth about his psychological state.

C1: Oh couldn’t write anything deeper because he is incapable of introspection. His emotional void makes it impossible for him to explore or express anything beyond surface-level traits. Instead, they revealed that Oh had nothing significant to say other than boasting about himself. When it came to topics like friends, family, happy times, or sad times, Oh had little to offer. Everything seemed to revolve around him.

After breaking down Oh’s mindset, Frost asks him a final, seemingly simple question: “If you were an animal, what animal would you be? Or what color would you be?” Oh has no answer to this, which Frost interprets as a sign of a deeper issue. He explains that during adolescence, people often define themselves by comparing themselves to animals, colors, or characters. Those who have never thought about such things have a harder time understanding their true selves. Frost warns Oh that if he continues down this path, he’ll face significant problems later in life.

For the first time, Oh seems shaken and vulnerable. He asks Frost, “So... what should I do?” Frost sees this as a sign that Oh’s defense mechanisms are starting to weaken.

Oh then shows Frost a photo album filled with pictures of himself, photos of him accomplishing things, learning new skills, winning awards, and so on. He explains that experts say photos with parents typically return after marriage, but in all of Oh’s albums, there are almost no pictures without his mother.

Frost concludes that children often use their parents as a source of inspiration or as figures they want to emulate. However, people like Oh, who never received enough parental love, end up seeking love from others in a never-ending cycle because they can never feel satisfied. He then leads Oh and Yoon to the room filled with Oh’s narcissistic self-portraits and reveals the picture of the woman that he found earlier. Frost tells Oh, “All the women you’ve ever been with were people who gave you love… in place of your parents.”

Oh breaks down in tears, finally facing the truth about himself. In the middle of his crying, Frost holds up the photo of the woman, and the readers are finally told what the password to Oh’s front door was: it was the date of his mother’s death.

A few days later, Frost is explaining everything to Yoon. He tells her that Oh had mentioned dating 17 women, but in the room, there were only pictures of 16. This made Frost wonder: where was the 17

the woman? According to Frost, this last woman was different from the others. She was the only one who truly loved Oh for who he was, not for who he pretended to be.

A month later, Oh makes an unexpected visit to Frost’s office, and he’s not alone. With him is the girl from the photograph, the one who truly loved him.

The Black Wave

Yoon was feeling impatient because one of her acquaintances was struggling to leave the house and attend school. Without needing her to explain the details, just by observing her facial expressions and behavior, Dr. Frost could already tell that Yoon wanted to ask him for help.

She then explains Nari's situation to Dr. Baek. Dr. Frost suggests they meet up, but Yoon says Nari isn't ready to see a therapist. With that information, Dr. Frost devises a plan. The plan is for him to pose as a private tutor for Nari, using Yoon as an excuse for why he was requested.

Frost proceeds to pose as the tutor, successfully convincing Nari’s mother, and then begins speaking with Nari while pretending to be her tutor. During the conversation, he quickly realizes that Nari isn’t tense and continues to maintain eye contact, signaling that she does not have social anxiety disorder.

Frost devises a psychological strategy to make Nari feel more comfortable. He tries to bring up deeper subjects during meals, as he notes that mealtime is when people are in a more relaxed psychological state. At first, Frost uses various behavioral tactics with Nari, such as:

* Content repetition
* brief repetition changes.
* Reflection techniques.

He also observed that she wasn’t tense and maintained eye contact, while combining this with the anxious responses Nari gave in the questionnaire provided by Seonga. Dr. Frost decided that, for this particular case, he would need to build rapport with Nari, despite usually avoiding this. He used the following tactics to build rapport:

1. He peeled and ate apples with Nari. This allowed her to act instinctively, rather than socially, making her feel more comfortable and less guarded. He explained that eating together can often help people open up, similar to how friends or blind dates start by sharing a meal.

2. He asked her questions, and when she said she didn’t mind studying, he used a technique called "backtracking" by repeating what she said. He noted how rare it was for a student to enjoy studying and repeated her words to show he was listening.

3. He let her talk about the things she liked, knowing people often feel more at ease when discussing topics they are passionate about.

4. He used a technique called "mirroring," subtly imitating her actions, like taking a sip of his drink when she did, which signaled to her subconscious that he was a companion.

After building rapport, Frost noticed that Nari spoke openly about school, which is rare for someone who dislikes it. Sensing an opportunity, he asked her directly why she refused to go. He added, "If your heart doesn’t feel like going, there must be a reason, right?" This made Nari feel heard and reassured that he wasn’t sent by her mother. Continuing the mirroring, Frost acted afraid of a cockroach, mimicking Nari’s reaction. When he saw her body language upon seeing the cockroach, Frost inferred she had a panic disorder. He quickly covered her mouth to prevent hyperventilation and controlled her panic attack, using his knowledge of behavioral tactics.

The Premise
(P1): When Nari spots the cockroach, her body language changes abruptly, her breathing becomes erratic, and her physical reaction suggests she is about to lose control. This sudden escalation is a sign of an oncoming panic attack.
(P2): Frost, observing Nari’s quickening breath and panicked state, knows from his experience that hyperventilation is a common reaction during panic attacks. Without immediate intervention, hyperventilation can worsen, leading to further anxiety and loss of control.
C1:
Based on Nari’s immediate physical reaction and his knowledge of panic disorder symptoms, Frost quickly infers that she is about to hyperventilate and takes action by covering her mouth to prevent it, using his understanding of behavioral tactics and panic management.
Frost noticed that although Nari was open to talking about school, studies, and friends, she didn’t display any aversion, which contradicted the assumption that school itself was the issue. This observation led him to believe that the root problem wasn’t the physical place of the school.

Meanwhile, Yoon didn’t know that Frost was carrying out this plan, but she found out when Nari’s mother called her, asking about the new tutor. Yoon was shocked, remembering a warning from another woman that Frost was a dangerous man. While Yoon was on the call, a scream erupted from Nari’s room. Frost had his hands over Nari’s mouth, and it looked as if he was abusing her.

The scene shifts to the police station, where officers discuss Frost’s situation. While they talk, Frost calls Yoon and tells her to visit Nari’s house and then go to her school. Despite her anger, Yoon agrees to do it, as Frost insists it's in the best interest of the patient.

Frost tells himself that he has already figured out the core of Nari’s problem, though he still doesn’t know why she isn’t opening up about it. This leads to a confrontation with Professor Song, an acquaintance of Yoon’s, who criticizes Frost by saying, "You’re even worse than I thought. I knew you weren’t capable of helping people." Frost remains calm and retorts that Song is the one relying on second-hand information and assumptions. This infuriates her, but Frost doesn’t get upset.

Frost also reminds Song that her most famous work was initially his, though he doesn’t show any anger about her taking the credit. As Dr. Frost expected, Nari’s house was far from her school. While examining the information on the side table, Frost realized everything was falling into place as he had predicted. He asked Yoon, over the phone, if there was a history of mental disorders on Nari’s mother’s side of the family. As he suspected, this was confirmed, which allowed Frost to solve the case.

Later, Nari began to have another episode and started screaming. Frost tried to help her, but the local police officers prevented him from intervening.
