The strongest and most overlooked strategy performed by Akagi

In the context of this work, and specifically because of the game itself, the focus is placed entirely on strategies related to the game or the opponent. However, when we take a step back and look at the bigger picture, we discover an approach employed by Akagi that may well be the greatest display of genius throughout his entire career, one that his opponents fail to comprehend until it is too late.

I am referring to a strategy known as "Darkness", which represents the cornerstone of the philosophy Fukumoto established throughout his manga. Fukumoto presents Mahjong and competition in general as something far beyond mere rules, strategies, or even reading one's opponent. Instead, he introduces something much deeper: an approach that makes its practitioner virtually impervious to conventional forms of intellectual superiority based on understanding and analyzing the opponent.

# What is "Darkness"?

To understand the meaning of this concept within the manga, we must examine it through its opposite: **Light.**

Fukumoto portrays Light as the conventional approach, the hope that players cling to. It encompasses logic, survival instinct, greed, and the desire to stay alive. Every decision they make, consciously or otherwise, ultimately serves to protect themselves above all else. It is simply human nature, the fundamental truth of how people behave when pushed into extreme situations.

Darkness, on the other hand, is **the void of ego and self-preservation**. It is a state in which an individual abandons the desire to survive or protect themselves, even when circumstances become increasingly dire.

# The strongest flawless application of such a strategy:

As I mentioned earlier, Darkness is an approach Akagi has followed throughout the story. However, I will focus on its application during Washizu Mahjong, where Akagi pushed this philosophy to its absolute limits, turning it into an almost unassailable strategy that dominated the match. Despite eventually understanding what Akagi was doing, Washizu was unable to counter it or deal with its implications until it was already too late.

Akagi's strategy of Darkness against Washizu consisted of several layers, or smaller strategies, working together.

First, Akagi secretly arranged to receive a blood transfusion before the match. This was not motivated by any desire to survive or anything of the sort. Rather, Akagi understood that he would never be able to maintain complete control over the course of the match and would inevitably lose a considerable amount of blood by the end. That is simply the nature of Washizu Mahjong.

Therefore, this precaution was essential to the success of his strategy, as it served three distinct purposes:

**Extending his available time:** In order to execute a long-term strategy that required a considerable amount of time, Akagi had to ensure that his body could endure for as long as possible. Given that his opponent was someone as wealthy as Washizu, dragging him into Darkness would inevitably take a long time. In other words, Akagi had effectively purchased himself additional time.

**Psychological intimidation:** Washizu sees himself as someone who controls others and takes pleasure in watching their lives disappear through the blood transfusion tube. However, Akagi's confidence and smile even in moments when he appears to be facing death planted fear in Washizu's mind. Since Washizu was unaware of the blood transfusion, he perceived Akagi as something monstrous rather than human. This gradually fueled his panic and hallucinations.

**Removing the fear of loss:** As long as a person has something to lose, they will naturally approach situations with caution. Akagi understood this perfectly. To eliminate this natural tendency, he arranged the blood transfusion and effectively used it as an invisible safety net, allowing him to approach the brink of death while remaining capable of enduring for much longer. This enabled him to eliminate hesitation and defensive calculations, allowing him to focus entirely on attacking Washizu.

Second, Akagi exploited the very rules established by Washizu. Although Washizu held an overwhelming advantage over Akagi, the rules he designed were inevitably based on certain assumptions: that Akagi was an ordinary human being who, above all else, wanted to survive and obtain money.

This was precisely why the blood-drawing option was incorporated into the rules, allowing Akagi to exchange his blood for money.

However, Akagi turned this arrangement against its creator, exploiting it in the exact opposite way it was intended to be used and directly incorporating it into his philosophy of Darkness.

Third, as is well known in Mahjong, each tile has four copies.

In Washizu Mahjong, ordinary tiles are used alongside transparent tiles, which can be identified from both sides. Each tile therefore has three transparent copies and one ordinary copy, making it considerably easier to read other players' hands.

This is precisely what Washizu believed. And reasonably so, because under these rules, a player would naturally try to conceal ordinary tiles as much as possible, since they represent uncertainty and provide protection for their hand. Conversely, they would tend to discard transparent tiles to eliminate certainty from their hand and make it harder for others to read it.

Based on this principle, Washizu could read approximately 75% of the table and deduce the remaining information through logic and probability. This is where Akagi quickly recognized an opportunity and exploited it.

He deliberately violated the conventional behavior that an ordinary player would follow under these rules. He began retaining transparent tiles while discarding ordinary ones in unusual patterns, planting misleading ideas and false clues in Washizu's mind. These moves further reinforced Washizu's confirmation bias and arrogance.

In essence, Akagi was feeding Washizu's deductive abilities (Light) through these maneuvers. Washizu failed to realize that the opponent sitting across from him was not an ordinary person who operated according to Light, but someone who had adopted Darkness as his fundamental philosophy, someone who simply did not care about protecting himself.

Furthermore, Darkness was not merely an offensive weapon for Akagi. It was also a trap designed to drag Washizu into it.

Gradually, Washizu became overwhelmed by fear and panic as he sank deeper into Darkness throughout the match. Watching Akagi smile in the face of death shattered the logical worldview (Light) upon which Washizu relied.

Being forced to confront the possibility of death in this manner caused him to hallucinate and make increasingly suicidal decisions, such as the moment when he was eventually hypnotized.

Simply put, Washizu cannot read someone who has no desire to survive.

Fourth, Suzuki, who was supposed to be Washizu's assistant, was completely unaware that he would become a crucial element in Akagi's calculations. Neither Suzuki nor the person he was working for realized the role he would play.

Akagi used Suzuki to gain a clearer understanding of Washizu's movements and reactions. He also exploited the possibility of a head bump whenever the opportunity presented itself.

# Conclusion:

All these elements were smaller strategies that Akagi employed based on his understanding of the rules, carefully coordinating them to serve his overarching strategy: **Darkness.**

He systematically removed every obstacle that could stand in its way, allowing it to gradually penetrate Washizu's mind.

This was not merely a strategy for winning a game. It was an entire philosophy that redefines the concept of survival and fundamentally alters the opponent's mindset, ultimately changing their understanding of competition forever.
