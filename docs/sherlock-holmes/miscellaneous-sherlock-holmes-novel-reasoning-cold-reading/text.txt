Miscellaneous Novel Sherlock Holmes reasoning, cold reading and analysis feats

–Upon a quick inspection of Watson’s new pocket watch, Holmes observes the H.W. on the back, the W of course referring to Watson, leading Holmes to believe it belonged originally to Watson’s father, as evidenced by the date of its manufacture being written as fifty years ago, yet was left first to Watson’s elder brother, as Holmes knew Watson’s father died many years ago yet he only got the watch recently. He noted that there were scratches and marks along the lower part indicating that it had been kept in the same pocket as objects such as coins and keys, and Holmes therefore extrapolated from such information that a man who treats such an expensive watch so thoughtlessly is therefore likely to be careless. He also reasoned that for the man to have inherited a watch of this value, he was likely well-provided for in his inheritance in other aspects. Holmes also noted the four ticket numbers scratched on the inside of the case, as is customary for pawnbrokers to do when they take a watch, and Holmes therefore infers that Watson’s brother was likely low on money often, having to hand over his father’s watch, yet had occasional bouts of prosperity as he could redeem the pledge. Holmes finally noted the thousands of scratches around the keyhole, and guessed Watson’s brother was most likely an alcoholic as that would cause his hand to slip carelessly and scratch up the watch when trying to insert the key to wind it up, leading to the final summation that the elder Watson brother was a man of untidy habits and carelessness, left with good prospects yet he squandered his chances ending up living in poverty. He had brief flits of prosperity, and finally, taking to drink in his depression he died tragically young. Holmes does note though that all of these were basically guesses he weighed as the most probable.

–Upon seeing a woman waiting outside his room at Baker Street, Holmes notes the way she fidgets, the way her body sways with anticipation even when stationary, and notes that such oscillation denotes she seeks advice, yet still is unsure of if the nature of her problem is too sensitive for outsider involvement. The most likely bet is a matter of love, of a man, yet it is not one who has wronged her in some great way, as she would not be oscillating then, rather she is confused, and seeks guidance. Upon seeing his client in person, Holmes observes an imprint a little above the wrist, right where she would be pressing her hands upon a bench were she to frequent the use of a typewriter, thus he infers she has been doing much type-writing recently. He also notes a couple of little indents upon her face on either side of the nose, the kind pince-nez would leave, and thus also infers that she is short-sighted, this inference and the last being made in an instant. What’s more, he notices that one of the toecaps of her boots was slightly more decorated than the other, and both were half-buttoned. Furthermore, she had ink showing both on her glove and on the patch of finger showing through a hole in her glove, from which Holmes infers that she had dipped her pen too deep in her ink writing just before she left, and with this and the observations of her boots, he infers she must have been in a rush to get to Holmes in spite of her hesitation of entering.

–A hat and goose are brought to Holmes after they were found having been dropped by a man. He observes that the hat is of a high quality from the band of ribbed silk and the excellent lining, yet is three years old and rather faded, from this he infers that for the owner to have bought so nice a hat three years ago yet no new one since, he’s likely not as financially secure as he once was. He furthermore notes that there a little disc and loop of the hat securer, something never sold on hats unless ordered, implying that the man has a level of care he endeavors to take as he took such a precaution against the wind, yet the elastic has broken recently and he’s yet to replace nor have a spare one, which indicates to Holmes that such foresight has declined, likely in tandem with worsening financial security, strengthening a growing idea that the man’s life if going downhill over the years. However, he has notably attempted to cover some stains on the hat with ink, indicating that he still has some self-respect. What’s more, the hat has an accumulation of dust, not gritty grey dust from a street, but fluffy brown dust from a home, implying he spends much of his time inside, additionally, this collection of dust would likely imply a rift between him and his wife, as she’s not brushed it clean in quite a while contrary to, say, Watson who is happily married and whose hat is brushed rather regularly by his wife. He knows the man is married as the goose had a card on it addressed to his wife, most likely as an attempt to rekindle their drifting apart over the years as the goose was very well-fed and exceptionally cared for. Also, there are no less than five tallow marks on the hat, which means there’s a good chance the man uses tallow candles as opposed to gas lamps, altogether leading to the conclusion that this man and his wife have had their comfort declined over the last three years that has depressed their spirits and left them with a growing chasm between them, even impacting their day to day expenses such as the lack of lamps and the ink stains to cover the fading on the hat as opposed to buying a new one, albeit they hide this insecurity from the outside and even likely each other as seen with buying such a grand goose and said ink covering.

–Holmes, in his youth, observed that his friend’s father had obtained his staff in the past year based on its inscription, and that he had had lead poured into a hole to make it a weapon, and thus inferred that he most likely feared some personal attack on himself. He also notices the man’s ears as having the signature flattening and thickening to them that denotes that he was a boxer in his youth, the calluses of his hands betray that he did a great bit of mining when he was younger, and he finally noticed through the man’s sleeve a tattoo of J.A. which had been blurred in a manner as though an attempt had been made to obscure them, indicating he was once well acquainted with someone of initials ‘J.A.’ but has since fallen out with.

–A rich man named Acton had had his library robbed. A coachman of the rich young Alec Cunningham and his father, who coincidentally had been engaged in a lawsuit with Acton, has been shot by a fleeing robber, and was in his hand holding the fragment of paper titled Source 1 on the second slide. Upon inspecting the note, Holmes is able to tell based on the strong t’s of ‘at’ and ‘to’ compared to the weak ones in ‘quarter’ and ‘what’ that the note was written by two people, taking turns filling it in with words. He can also tell that the ‘maybe’ was written in the stronger hand. Holmes then is able to perceive that as whatever business these conspirators were in was a bad one, one distrusted the other so determinedly that they had decided write together, as to have equal hand in their misdeeds. He is also able to determine that the stronger hand wrote their words first, judging by how ‘quarter’ was squeezed in, and based on this, the stronger hand is likely the more dominating, the leader of the two. What’s more, looking at the two hands and the minute differences in legibility, Holmes can even determine the stronger handed leader as being a much younger man than his aged associate, and finally is also able to discern that the two are closely related, as there were many small points in the writing, such as the Greek e’s, that indicated family mannerisms, as well as the possibility the older one taught the younger one. This all leads to the conclusion it was the Cunninghams all along.

–Someone has visited 221b Baker street, and left their walking stick behind.The stick has “To James Mortimer, MRCS, from his friends of the CCH” with the date 1884 engraved upon it. Holmes notices that the stick is worn down a great deal, especially in the iron ferrule, thus pointing towards it being used frequently, and that therefore Mortimer must have done much walking, too much for a town practitioner, and he therefore is much more likely a country one. He also recalls the Charing Cross Hospital from the CCH, backed up by Mortimer’s being MRCS (Membership of the Royal Colleges of Surgeons) and reasons that he most likely was a house-surgeon or house-physician, little more than a senior student as opposed to being on staff, as he would then have been well established in a London practice, and such a man would not have drifted to country practice. Holmes then has Watson look it up, confirming that at Charing Cross there was a Doctor James Mortimer. He further infers that Mortimer is relatively young, under thirty, from his status, unambitious, as he chose to work as a country practitioner as opposed to in the city, amiable, as otherwise he wouldn’t have received such a token of appreciation, yet absent minded, as he left it behind. Holmes then notes the bite marks on the stick as being from Mortimer’s dog, most likely a curly hair spaniel based on the size and shape.

–Holmes reads a letter sent to his client, Sir Henry Baskerville, made out using cut out words stuck to a page, and instantly recalls the exact newspaper article he read yesterday which the words were cut from thanks to their specific font, as well as the use of a couple of consecutive words. Holmes can also tell on sight that the scissors used to cut out the strips were nail-scissors. One word though had been written, and was written in a rough hand, enough so that Holmes is able to tell it was a fake hand, especially seeing as the other words were cut from the Times, a paper read almost exclusively by upper class, educated people, allowing Holmes to infer that the writer is educated but trying to pretend otherwise, and may also have handwriting known to Baskerville. Holmes also infers a good chance of the creator of the letter being in a hurry or expecting interruption, as the pasted somewhat messily, some higher than others or not lining up, while they otherwise seem to have put a great amount of time and thought into the endeavor. Holmes as well notices that the pen used to write it had trouble and the ink was not plentiful, in a way that someone, especially someone as careful as this person seems to be, wouldn’t allow to happen, but would be very likely if they were using a hotel’s ink and pen, and so guesses that they were staying in a hotel. Holmes additionally perceives the scent of perfume, recognizing it as white Jessamine, as he has memorized the scents of 75 perfumes, and thus draws the final inference that there is a good chance of the creator’s being a woman.

–A man named Hilton Cubitt comes to see Holmes about a number of strange ciphers he’d been receiving on paper. These papers had on them a parade of little stick figures all in different positions. Such positions seem to be causing an extraordinary amount of distress unto Hilton’s wife, Elsie, an American girl of a mysterious background she’s not comfortable divulging. The first message he brings to Holmes is Source 2 on the second slide. Holmes begins his analysis. First, he notes that the most commonly used letter in the English language is ‘E’ and that the first message alone contained the symbol titled Source 3 twice, so that as the substitute for 'E' seems viable. What’s more, based on the distribution of the figures holding flags, it can be assumed that they are used to break up words.

–Cubitt returns days later to add Sources 4, 5 and 6, with 6 being found under the first message recieved to help with Holmes’ analysis. rom here, he analyzes the third of these messages, noting that there are two ‘Es’ as the second and fourth letters. Holmes further conjectures that there is a high chance of this being a response to the message it was stuck under, and therefore most likely ‘never’ and written by Elsie. This then also means that the symbols on Source 6 respectively stand for ‘N’, ‘V’ and ‘R’. This is beginning to point to the culprit being someone from Elsie’s past, and that her name may have been in one of the messages as an appeal. So searching for a five letter word beginning and ending with ‘E’, Holmes finds one in the message preceding her ‘Never’ and what’s more, the four letter word preceding ‘Elsie’ ends with ‘E’. Trying out all four letter words ending with ‘E’ he finds that ‘Come’ fits best, and thanks to this, he gains the letters ‘L’, ‘S’, ‘I’, ‘C’, ‘O’ and ‘M’. Holmes then uses all of this gathered information to decode the first message, leading to an uncovered ‘AM HERE A.E SLANE.’. Holmes fills in the missing letters in the name as ‘B’ and ‘Y’, helping to also strengthen the theory of it being someone from Elsie’s past, as Abe is a common American contraction of ‘Abraham’. He then goes back to the second message, getting ‘A. ELRI.ES’ and of course fills in the obviously missing letters to get ‘AT ELRIGES’
