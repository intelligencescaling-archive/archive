Moriarty’s Opera Strategy from Sherlock Holmes: A Game of Shadows (2011)

Hello everyone!

This is an analysis of a strategy from my favorite version of Moriarty: the one from the RDJ Sherlock films. I highly recommend checking them out, especially *A Game of Shadows*, which features a very well portrayed cat and mouse outsmarting battle between Sherlock and Moriarty. This analysis will cover what is arguably Moriarty's best strategy in the film. If anyone has corrections, objections, or any other comments, please let me know!

It goes without saying that anything below is spoilers for the film. So, without further ado, let's get into it.

**Overview**

The year is 1891. Professor James Moriarty wants to murder Alfred Meinhardt, a German industrialist who owns a major artillery and munitions factory. This is part of a larger plan that attempts to provoke a war between France and Germany in order to initiate a World War while acquiring the industries needed to profit from it. Moriarty has already purchased a large share of Meinhardt’s company through an unidentified investor. By killing Meinhardt, he can take control of the factory and expand his ability to supply weapons during the conflict he is creating.

Moriarty disguises the assassination as part of a larger terrorist attack. Meinhardt will attend a business conference at the Hôtel du Triomphe in Paris, where a bomb concealed inside a ceremonial cake will kill numerous guests and make the event appear connected to the escalating Franco-German conflict. Colonel Sebastian Moran is separately assigned to shoot Meinhardt immediately before the explosion, ensuring that the one indispensable victim dies while the bombing conceals that he was specifically targeted.

Sherlock Holmes already suspects Moriarty’s involvement in the attacks and acquisitions occurring across Europe, so Moriarty also constructs a deception to prevent him from reaching the hotel in time. He plants evidence suggesting that the bomb is hidden inside the Paris Opera across the square and attends the performance himself to strengthen that conclusion. The operation therefore aims to kill Meinhardt, conceal the commercial motive behind his death, divert Sherlock during the intervention window and make the attack appear to be another act in the political conflict Moriarty is engineering.

**Preparation**

The political foundation is established through an earlier bombing in Strasbourg and many other attacks across Europe. France and Germany have been blaming each other, with newspapers and governments debating whether the attacks are being conducted by nationalists or anarchists. As such, each new explosion can be interpreted as part of an existing cycle of retaliation, even when its real purpose is commercial or personal. The Paris attack therefore arrives with its own explanation already prepared: it will appear to be Germany’s retaliation for Strasbourg. Beyond Meinhardt’s death, the bombing would worsen Franco-German relations, as well as disrupt travel and increase the probability of war. Sherlock later notes that the bombing causes the border crossing between France and Germany to be closed.

Likely months before the bombing, Moriarty takes control of an existing French anarchist group led by Claude Ravache, an experienced bombmaker who operates from the cellar beneath a Parisian bakery or restaurant. Ravache and his associates initially accepted money from an anonymous English benefactor who claimed to support their political cause. Slowly, they became dependent on that support, and with that, Moriarty began dictating their actions and forcing them to accept responsibility for attacks committed for his purposes. Ravache builds the devices, moves them through Paris and provides the authorities with a credible radical suspect. Moriarty’s name remains outside the visible chain, as he doesn’t openly deploy any of his men while continuing to further his objectives. It can be speculated that the chain of anarchist and nationalist bombings across Europe have all been engineered by a similar mechanism, showing that Moriarty’s plan is flexible and not contingent on one terrorist group or bombing, all while remaining clean and anonymous.

A few days before killing Meinhardt, Moriarty purchases a large share of his arms company through an unidentified investor. The idea is that once Meinhardt dies, Moriarty gains control over his company, including the factory at Heilbronn. Sherlock explicitly connects the recent anonymous purchase to Moriarty and concludes that the factory now belongs to him. Had Moriarty murdered Meinhardt privately, investigators might’ve examined beneficiaries or competitors or shareholders and similar suspects. But the bombing reverses that priority as Meinhardt would appear to be one casualty among many. The authorities would once again be pushed toward nationalism, anarchism, terrorism and international retaliation as the explanation, removing the need to investigate Moriarty’s hostile takeover. The operation thus conceals both the method of murder and the motive behind it.

Sherlock reaches Ravache through Madame Simza Heron, a Roma fortune-teller searching for her missing brother René. René once belonged to Ravache’s movement and has since been drawn into Moriarty’s organization. Before disappearing, he sends Simza several drawings and a letter. Sherlock notices that the paper is unusually thick and designed for a printing press. It smells musty, suggesting cold and damp storage, and carries a wine stain. Simza knows of a wine cellar used by Ravache’s group near a printing press, allowing Sherlock, Dr. John Watson and Simza to locate the group’s Paris headquarters.

When confronting Ravache, Sherlock learns that Moriarty enforced his continued cooperation by having taken his wife and children sometime before the bombing, leaving him convinced that obedience and silence are the only ways to keep them alive. This prevents Ravache from defecting. Even after admitting that an Englishman has seized control of his movement and forced him to plant another bomb, Ravache refuses to disclose its location. He tells Sherlock that fewer than ten minutes remain, then reveals an arrangement he had with Moriarty, *“no loose ends,”* before killing himself. Sherlock loses the only person present who can identify the target directly. As will be explained shortly, Moriarty anticipated Sherlock’s investigation of Ravache and specifically prepared a misdirection for him after the latter kills himself. The confrontation itself was designed to buy time so that Sherlock can’t reach the bomb in time, while the gunshot from Ravache’s suicide alerts those in the bakery/restaurant, setting a trap for Sherlock to further waste his time. Given the limited timeframe Sherlock has at his disposal, he is forced to rely on environmental deduction rather than direct testimony or further investigations. Therefore, he must form and act upon a hypothesis immediately. This is the foundation of the opera deception.

The hotel bomb is smuggled into the conference through the bakery itself. Ravache’s establishment has been contracted to provide a large ceremonial cake for the dinner at the Hôtel du Triomphe. The explosive is concealed inside that cake, allowing it to be carried into the hotel as an expected part of the evening’s service. The delivery attracts less suspicion than an unidentified crate or unattended parcel, and it can be positioned close to the assembled guests without requiring one of Moriarty’s identifiable agents to remain in the room. Alfred Meinhardt is among the businessmen attending the conference.

Sherlock actually sees the cake before learning where the bomb has been planted. While moving through Ravache’s premises, he observes workers transporting the large delivery intended for the Hôtel du Triomphe. At that point, it appears to be routine activity inside a bakery serving a prestigious event. Sherlock registers the image without connecting it to the impending attack. Moriarty’s later false clue gives Sherlock a more specific and intellectually satisfying interpretation, causing the cake delivery to remain an unexamined background detail until the trap has already consumed most of the deadline.

Moriarty separately assigns the targeted assassination to Colonel Sebastian Moran, his principal enforcer and one of Europe’s most capable military marksmen. Moran expects to attend a performance of Mozart’s *Don Giovanni* at the Paris Opera. A day or so before the bombing, Moriarty informs him that he will no longer need his ticket and orders him to follow Meinhardt instead. Moran tracks Meinhardt to the Hôtel du Triomphe and takes up a long-range firing position opposite the hotel. Moriarty attends the opera himself while Moran prepares to shoot across the square.

The rifle and the bomb thus give Moriarty two independent methods of killing Meinhardt. Moran can shoot him even if Sherlock discovers or disables the explosive. The bomb can still kill him if Moran misses, loses his firing position or cannot take the shot. Under the intended sequence, Moran kills Meinhardt first and the explosion follows, destroying the room and causing his body to be processed with the other bombing casualties. The shooting secures the assassination; the bombing conceals its individual nature.

**Execution**

After Ravache’s suicide, Sherlock, Watson and Simza are attacked by the remaining men in the building. Sherlock uses environmental clues to reconstruct a sequence that leads him to discovering a secret escape route through a subterranean passage connected to the cellar, their only escape route, and likely the trail Moriarty wanted them to follow. Inside the tunnel, Sherlock notices part of a theatrical structure bearing the word *IMPERATOR*. The object belongs to the scenic apparatus used in the Paris Opera’s current production of *Don Giovanni*. In the opera’s final act, the dead Commendatore returns in the form of a stone statue and confronts Don Giovanni. The statue’s pedestal in this production carries the same inscription. Sherlock possesses enough musical and theatrical knowledge to recognize the connection between the word, the statue and the performance taking place nearby that evening. He concludes that the tunnel and the marked structure point toward the Paris Opera, thinking it’s where the bomb is going to be delivered. Theoretically, it makes sense; a crowded opera would fit the pattern of a major terrorist attack, is difficult to search quickly because of its stage, machinery, props and backstage areas, and is a location Moriarty can enter publicly without appearing suspicious.

Sherlock believes the bomb has been placed inside the Commendatore statue used onstage. His line of thinking was probably something like this: a large hollow theatrical prop can conceal an explosive, move through backstage areas without attracting attention and detonate above a crowded audience; the opera will be full, and Moriarty has an established interest in the performance, and the marked scenic structure appears to connect Ravache’s underground premises directly to the production. With fewer than ten minutes remaining, the statue presents a complete explanation of the available evidence. Verifying every alternative would require returning to the bakery, tracing the cake delivery, investigating the hotel gathering, searching nearby buildings, alerting the Parisian authorities, and probably a lot more, none of which are feasible given the time constraints. Hundreds of people may die while he does so. Sherlock consequently acts on the strongest immediate interpretation rather than dividing the remaining minutes among several weaker possibilities. Moriarty correctly predicts that Sherlock will prioritize immediate prevention.

The opera and the hotel are situated across the same square, which allows Moriarty to keep Sherlock physically close to the real attack while still making intervention impractical. Sherlock can see the Hôtel du Triomphe from the opera, but the interior of the theatre absorbs the available time. He must enter through the crowded building, force his way backstage, descend beneath the stage, identify the machinery controlling the Commendatore statue and search the pedestal while the performance continues above him. Moriarty’s presence in the audience also strengthens the false conclusion. Sherlock knows that Moriarty is directing the terrorist network, and finding him calmly watching *Don Giovanni* makes the opera appear even more closely connected to the operation. Moriarty can also observe whether Sherlock has followed the trail while remaining publicly visible in a crowded theatre across from the site of the bombing. Moran, meanwhile, is absent from the performance and positioned for the shot Moriarty assigned him. The man Sherlock most wants to stop is placed inside the decoy; the man carrying out the targeted murder is positioned outside Sherlock’s search.

Beneath the stage, Sherlock watches the Commendatore statue rise during the opera’s climactic scene. He opens the section of the pedestal marked *IMPERATOR*, expecting to expose the bomb concealed inside it. Instead, he finds a black king chess piece placed within the hollow center of the letter “O.” Looking through that opening, he sees Moriarty seated in his box and watching him. The chess piece confirms that the trail was constructed as a personal move against Sherlock. There is no explosive inside the statue. Moriarty has anticipated the exact location Sherlock will search and has left an object that can only be understood once Sherlock reaches the end of the false trail.

Sherlock then mentally reviews the observations he dismissed while pursuing the opera interpretation. The crucial image is the large cake leaving Ravache’s establishment for the Hôtel du Triomphe. Ravache had admitted planting a bomb that evening; his bakery had supplied the hotel’s conference dinner; the cake provided the container and delivery mechanism; and the hotel stood directly across from the building Moriarty had encouraged Sherlock to search. Sherlock realizes he made a mistake, and it’s now too late for him to stop the bomb. Moriarty watches as Sherlock makes the realization, further taunting him, while the chess piece functions as the signature that seals the trap.

Meanwhile, Moran has Meinhardt in his line of sight inside the hotel. He takes a long-range shot from the opera-side rooftop shortly before the bomb detonates. Meinhardt is addressing the assembled businessmen when Moran fires from his prepared position across the square. The shot kills Meinhardt before the bomb detonates. Moments later, the explosive inside the cake destroys the conference room and kills numerous other attendees. Sherlock, Watson and Simza leave the opera as soon as the deception becomes clear, but the distance across the square still exceeds the few seconds remaining. Sherlock later reconstructs the firing position from pieces of evidence left on the scene: the tripod scrape, shooting stick, wind-gauge placement and Moran’s distinctive cigarette blend. He estimates the shot at roughly 600 to 650 yards in significant wind and identifies Moran as one of the few European marksmen capable of making it. The gunshot therefore serves as a contingency, and when coupled with the bomb, they both cover the principal failure condition of the other. If the bomb detonates normally, Meinhardt has already been shot and is guaranteed dead; the blast damages his body and the surrounding crime scene;  witnesses assume he died with the other victims; investigators search for explosive evidence rather than a bullet wound; the gunshot becomes almost impossible to separate from the explosion. Watson later states: *“no one looks for a bullet hole in a bomb blast.”* If the bomb fails or is disarmed, then Moran can still kill Meinhardt. Moriarty may lose the political bombing and forensic concealment, but he retains the immediate economic objective, and Moran slips away without being identified. By the time Sherlock gathers conclusive evidence against him, he’d have already left Paris and France with Moriarty. If Moran misses or cannot take the shot, then the bomb still kills Meinhardt with everyone else.

The explosion gives every visible element of the event a common explanation. The destroyed room, multiple bodies and previous sequence of political attacks direct investigators toward terrorism. France and Germany are already expecting retaliation; the device has been constructed and delivered through a known anarchist network; and Ravache is dead before he can explain that Moriarty controlled him. The public question becomes which radical or nationalist faction committed the bombing and how France or Germany will respond. The anonymous investor who benefits from one attendee’s death remains outside that political narrative.

The same blast conceals the rifle assassination, as Meinhardt appears to have died with everyone else when the cake exploded. A gunshot occurring seconds before a major explosion is easily absorbed into the larger event: witnesses hear the blast, the room is destroyed, and the authorities have little initial reason to search one supposed bombing victim for evidence of a separate attack. The damaged body, collapsed surroundings and mass of casualties all discourage individualized examination. Moriarty can therefore guarantee Meinhardt’s death through Moran without allowing the rifle to define the official crime.

Once Meinhardt is removed, Moriarty’s concealed shareholding gives him control of the Heilbronn munitions factory. The killing appears politically indiscriminate while its economic result is highly specific. Ravache’s bomb supplies the public perpetrator, Strasbourg supplies the international motive, the conference supplies additional victims, the cake supplies access, the opera supplies the false target, Moriarty’s presence supplies confirmation for Sherlock’s mistaken hypothesis, and Moran’s rifle ensures that the one indispensable victim cannot survive.

What makes the strategy so impressive is how thoroughly every foreseeable problem is absorbed into the same design. Moriarty accounts for Holmes’s interference, Ravache’s possible exposure, the bomb’s potential failure, Moran’s potential failure, the need to conceal Meinhardt’s individual murder, the need to disguise the financial motive, and the political consequences required for his larger scheme. The components are not merely placed beside one another; they are built into a causal structure in which each action prepares, protects or completes another. The Strasbourg bombing establishes the retaliatory narrative, Ravache’s organization supplies a credible perpetrator, his family secures his silence, the opera diverts Sherlock, Moriarty’s presence reinforces the false target, Moran guarantees Meinhardt’s death, and the hotel explosion conceals the shot. Even if Sherlock reaches Ravache, the location remains protected; if he recognizes the opera deception, he does so too late; if the bomb fails, Moran can still complete the assassination; if Moran fails, the bomb still kills Meinhardt. The operation repeatedly places expendable layers between Moriarty and the central objective, while allowing those same layers to shelter one another. Perhaps its greatest strength, however, is the amount Moriarty accomplishes through a single coordinated event: he eliminates Meinhardt, acquires an arms factory, destroys the evidence of a targeted assassination, directs suspicion toward political extremists, worsens Franco-German hostility, creates future demand for his military industries, and neutralizes Sherlock during the only period in which intervention could have mattered. Almost every component performs several functions at once, making the operation exceptionally efficient despite its complexity.
