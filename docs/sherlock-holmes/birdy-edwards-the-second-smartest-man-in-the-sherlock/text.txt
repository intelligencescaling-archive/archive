\# Story

Sherlock visited a mansion to solve a mysterious case in a country village, one believed to involve Moriarty.

There he found the body of the master of the house, his face half destroyed by a shotgun blast from a barrel sawed off to about 30 cm. Near the body was a card reading <V.V.-341>, and bloody slipper prints were found on the windowsill. His wedding ring was gone, and so was one of a pair of dumbbells.

Another notable thing was a mysterious mark on Douglas's right arm, as if burned in with fire: a triangle inside a circle. The servants said it had been on the master's arm for a long time.

The murder took place in the evening and was first discovered by a servant and James, an old friend of the master. Anticipating that Douglas's wife, Ivy, would be shocked, James kept her from seeing the scene.

Mysteriously, the window was open, yet the bicycle the culprit seemingly rode in on was in the yard. In other words, the culprit came by bicycle but did not leave on it. Also, the drawbridge, the only way in or out of the mansion, had been raised at 6 o'clock, so it was certain that whoever committed the crime after 6 had not come in through the front gate. And since the drawbridge was surrounded by a deep moat, the mystery deepened even further.

Watson falls into a vague notion that James and Ivy were having an affair and did away with Douglas. Why? While strolling in the garden, he saw James and Ivy laughing together cheerfully... Also, the bloody slipper prints were presumed to be his (James's). To be laughing and chatting right after a friend and husband had died! When Sherlock hears this, he falls into thought and does nothing but walk around the house, lost in contemplation.

JACK MCMURDO and Birdy Edwards

When Holmes said he would drain the water from the moat, Barker tried to secretly retrieve the sack containing the knife, shoes, and clothes from the moat in the middle of the night, but he failed because he was ambushed by Holmes's party. Then Holmes told Mrs. Douglas to persuade John Douglas to come out and explain things himself, and Douglas, who had been thought dead, walked out of a hidden place inside the study. And just as Sherlock's deduction went, Mrs. Douglas and Barker had known that Douglas had killed the man who tried to kill him, and had helped Douglas disguise that man, who had been killed while trying to kill Douglas, as Douglas himself, in order to hide Douglas.

In the past, he had worked as a secret agent of the Pinkerton Detective Agency, operating as a detective under the alias Jack McMurdo in order to wipe out a secret organization in Vermissa Valley, the Freemen.

When he first arrived in Vermissa Valley, he quietly dropped hints to the people around him that he was a member of the Freemen in Chicago.

(The Order of Freemen was originally a mild fraternal organization spread across every city in America, but the Vermissa Valley lodge had been corrupted into a gangster organization under the touch of some dark hand, and was known by the nickname "the Scowrers.")

Also, because he knew the organization's character well, to show off a violent disposition he displayed rough behavior in front of police officers he had recruited in advance.

Thanks to these early appearances, he was easily introduced by people around him to the boss, Jack McGinty.

McGinty tested him from the start by threatening him with a gun, so it was quite a dangerous situation. Even in this life-threatening situation, Jack easily talked his way through, explaining that he had been in prison because he had previously been in the business of making counterfeit money. Then he tossed out a real coin and said it was his handiwork. The boss, recognizing his usefulness, accepted him. It was later revealed that, in case he was shot, he had already put a gun in his coat pocket with the hammer cocked, ready.

Of course, this alone could hardly be considered a complete pass of the preliminary test, so, as arranged in advance with the police, he occasionally showed signs of a feud between himself and the police, and through this his fellowship with the organization grew firm.

According to what he investigated, the Scowrers were quite large, and their total membership was described as exceeding 500. The two mountain lodges took turns carrying out each other's evil deeds, aiming at the anonymity effect of having unknown faces. They also dominated the entire town economically, and anyone who offended them was buried, so no one would testify against them in court. Because they had money to burn, even when they were prosecuted, they were found not guilty, and this continued for ten years. Furthermore, since they already cooperated with the police, they were so powerful that even when imprisoned (it is too long, so I will skip it, but this is the story of when our protagonist was arrested while pretending to cooperate with them), they could hold a drinking party inside and spend the day, then walk out the next day.

He quickly became close with this crowd, and soon the boss had him take the "real test."

1. Making him walk one step forward in a state where he felt a sensation like his eyes were being stabbed and he feared that taking a step forward would blind him. (The exact mechanism has not been revealed.)

→ Success

2. Branding the organization's distinctive symbol on his arm with heated iron.

→ He succeeded, and, unexpectedly, he stood there without even making a groan. Even among the members of this organization, the reaction was that they had never seen anyone complete it so admirably.

He was thus formally admitted and then, completing other tasks one by one, even reached the point of being recommended as the next leader.

At last, when the Scowrers' violence reached its peak, a member named Morris spoke to McMurdo. Morris had formerly done business in the East and had a friend there, and he came to McMurdo for advice, saying he had received an important letter from that friend. The letter said that in the East, several large companies regarded the damage they suffered from the Scowrers as serious and had commissioned the capable detective firm Pinkerton, and that the Pinkerton Detective Agency had sent a capable agent named Birdy Edwards to investigate and bring down the Scowrers. Morris was agonizing over this: if he revealed the letter, Birdy Edwards would die, and if he did not, everyone would be ruined.

(It seems that Birdy deliberately leaked the information, so that someone would bring it up for discussion at a meeting.)

Having obtained the letter from Morris, McMurdo, both to ease Morris's worries and to resolve the organization's problem, attended a Scowrers meeting, revealed the letter, and argued that Birdy Edwards must be eliminated. He said that he had seen that Birdy Edwards was already disguised as a newspaper reporter named Steve Wilson, and proposed that he dangle the bait of offering information to Birdy Edwards, and that when Edwards took the bait, they would all pounce together and eliminate him.

At Jack McMurdo's suggestion, Black Jack McGinty decided to set a trap for Birdy Edwards, and with Ted Baldwin, Harraway, Carter, the Willaby brothers, Cormac, and other pillars of the organization, prepared to kill Birdy Edwards at McMurdo's house. But in fact, Jack McMurdo himself was Birdy Edwards. All that time, while deceiving them with a false identity, he had been secretly helping people who might be killed even from within.

(Before a family was killed by a bomb, he secretly leaked information so that the family living there could escape in advance.)

The organization tried to make a last desperate stand, but Edwards, who had been disguised as McMurdo, mobilized Inspector Marvin and dozens of police officers, surrounded the house where they were gathered, and rounded up McGinty's gang in one sweep. In addition, using the information he had gathered earlier, he put all the remaining members behind bars. (He had already planned this: he had chosen a house whose layout was exposed to the outside, so that no one could take cover if a shootout broke out. In addition, he kept sending coded telegrams to the detective agency.)

Afterward, he was not executed but came out of prison, and to avoid those who would take revenge on him, he crossed over to England. There he met Baldwin, the most vicious assassin, who had pursued him all the way to England (a man advised by Moriarty), fought a life-and-death struggle, and killed him.

He always kept a gun in places like his bedroom to guard against assassins. The day Baldwin burst in happened to be an unlucky day when Edwards's own guard was loose. Even so, he quickly detected the intruder hiding in a dark corner of the study, who had slipped into the house before 6 o'clock, attacked him with a hammer in an instant before he could even draw his gun, won the fight over the gun, and killed him. (Considering that the house was not in good size or condition, and that he let his guard down thinking the mansion had perfect defenses, this shows astonishing caution and judgment.)

Afterward, he noticed that the mark on his own arm was the same as the man's mark and that the man's head had been destroyed beyond recognition. To make it look as though he had died, so that no more assassins would need to interfere with his life, he persuaded his friend James to clean up the scene and hid in a hideout he had prepared. (Of course, When preparing in the beginning, he probably had not intended to turn the victim into the culprit. He was probably just going to make it look as if he had gone missing or been kidnapped.)

(Considering there was no fingerprint or genetic technology, I think the plot of reversing victim and culprit is ingenious.)
