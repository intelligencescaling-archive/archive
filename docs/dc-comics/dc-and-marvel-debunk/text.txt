Why Logic Is Essential To Debate: The Collapse of Discourse Without Non-Contradiction
Debates about fictional universes, powerscaling, or any subject matter fundamentally require logical principles to function as actual debates rather than mere exchanges of arbitrary assertions. The application of logic to debate isn't merely a stylistic choice or academic convention—it's the foundational requirement that makes debate possible at all.
The Definitional Necessity of Logic in Debate
A debate, by definition, involves the exchange of reasoned arguments aimed at establishing truth or reaching consensus through persuasion. This process depends on several logical prerequisites:

* Inferential structure: Debates operate through chains of inference where conclusions follow from premises. Without logical consistency, an argument cannot move from premises to conclusions in any meaningful way.

* Burden of proof: Participants in a debate must be able to support claims with evidence and reasoning that others can evaluate. This evaluation process requires logical principles to determine when a claim has been adequately supported.

* Refutation mechanism: The ability to demonstrate that an opponent's position contains errors or contradictions is central to debate. Without the principle of noncontradiction, refutation becomes impossible.

* Shared standards of validity: Participants must recognize the same rules for determining when an argument succeeds or fails. Logic provides these shared standards.

When these elements are removed, what remains is not debate but something fundamentally different—perhaps self-expression, rhetoric, or performance, but not an activity aimed at distinguishing between more and less defensible positions.
The Collapse of Discourse Without Non-Contradiction
When the principle of noncontradiction is abandoned, several critical failures occur that transform debate into incoherence:

* Argumentative immunity: Without non-contradiction, any position becomes immune to refutation. If a position can simultaneously be true and false, no counterargument can ever undermine it.
* Dissolution of disagreement: Real disagreement requires that opposing positions are genuinely incompatible. If contradictions are permitted, then seemingly opposed positions can both be "true," eliminating the substance of disagreement.
1. * Evaluation impossibility: Without logical standards, there is no basis to evaluate competing claims. Any method of assessment would itself be arbitrary and subject to contradiction.

* Communicative breakdown: Communication depends on shared meanings maintained through consistent usage. If terms can simultaneously hold contradictory meanings, communication itself breaks down.

* Reduction to authority: Without logical standards for evaluation, debate inevitably devolves into appeals to authority, where the "winner" is determined not by the quality of arguments but by external factors like status, popularity, or force.
The Special Case of Fictional Analysis
Some might argue that fictional universes, being imaginary constructs, are exempt from logical requirements. This position fails for several reasons:

* Interpretation vs. invention: Analyzing fiction involves interpreting existing material, not inventing new content. Interpretation requires logical principles to distinguish between more and less plausible readings of the text.

* Author intention: Even when considering author intention, we must use logical inference to determine what that intention likely was based on textual evidence.

* Suspension of disbelief: Fiction asks readers to suspend disbelief about certain premises (e.g., superpowers exist), but still expects logical consistency within the framework of those premises. This "internal logic" is what makes fictional worlds intelligible.

* Cross-textual comparison: Any attempt to compare characters or elements across different fictional works requires logical principles to establish meaningful connections.
Practical Manifestations in Powerscaling Debates
In powerscaling debates specifically, the rejection of logical principles leads to:

* Unfalsifiable claims: Without logical constraints, claims about character power become unfalsifiable. Any evidence against a character's power level can be simultaneously accepted and rejected.
* Selective reasoning: Participants begin applying logic selectively, using it when convenient and discarding it when disadvantageous.

* Definitional collapse: Terms like "omnipotent," "universal," or "multiversal" lose any stable meaning when they can simultaneously apply and not apply to the same entity.

* Preference masquerading as analysis: Without logical constraints, apparent "analysis" reduces to mere preference or bias concealed behind analytical language.
Conclusion: No Debate Without Logic
What emerges from this analysis is a fundamental truth: a "debate" without logic is not a debate at all but merely an exchange of preferences disguised as reasoned discourse. While fiction may create worlds with different physical laws or fantastical elements, the discussion of those worlds remains bound by logical principles if it aims to be anything more than arbitrary assertion.

Accepting contradictions doesn't enhance fictional analysis—it eliminates the possibility of analysis altogether, replacing it with a form of rhetoric where any claim is simultaneously defensible and indefensible. For powerscaling or any form of fictional analysis to function as a meaningful enterprise, it must operate within a framework where contradictions are recognized as problems to be resolved rather than features to be embraced.

Seven Arguments Against Dialetheism
1. Semantic Instability
Detailed Explanation:
Dialetheism posits that some statements, such as the Liar Paradox ("This sentence is false"), are both true and false, introducing "truth-value gluts" into logical semantics. In classical logic, truth and falsity are mutually exclusive: if a proposition P is true, its negation ¬P is false, and vice versa. This bivalence ensures a stable semantic framework where truth guides reasoning normatively (i.e., we aim to believe true propositions and reject false ones). Dialetheism disrupts this by allowing P∧¬P to hold, which critics argue creates an incoherent or unstable account of truth and negation. If a statement can be both true and false, the concept of truth becomes ambiguous, and negation loses its oppositional role (i.e., ¬P no longer guarantees falsity). This instability undermines the logical foundation needed for consistent reasoning and fails to resolve paradoxes effectively, as it replaces one problem (paradoxes) with another (semantic chaos).
The Liar Paradox illustrates this critique. In classical logic, the Liar’s self-referential nature leads to oscillation: if the sentence is true, it must be false, but if false, it must be true, resulting in a paradox. Dialetheists like Priest propose that the Liar is both true and false, a dialetheia, resolved within paraconsistent logics like the Logic of Paradox (LP), where contradictions do not entail everything. Critics argue that this solution does not clarify the Liar’s truth value but complicates the semantics of truth, making it difficult to assign consistent meanings to propositions. For example, if "This sentence is false" is both true and false, what does it mean to assert its truth? The lack of a clear answer destabilizes semantic theory, as truth no longer serves as a reliable guide for reasoning.

Moreover, dialetheism’s semantics require revising standard logical connectives. In LP, negation behaves non-classically, allowing P and ¬P to coexist without explosion. Critics contend that this revision sacrifices the intuitive clarity of negation as opposition, leading to a fragmented semantics where logical operators lack universal applicability. This instability extends to truth’s metaphysical role: if truth can encompass contradictions, the correspondence theory (truth as alignment with reality) falters, as reality cannot coherently instantiate contradictory states (e.g., an object being both wholly red and not red).

Supporting Examples:
* Timothy Williamson (1994, "Semantic Paradoxes and the Logic of Truth"): Williamson argues that dialetheism fails to provide a coherent semantics for truth. Truth, he contends, is normative: if P is true, we should accept P, and if ¬P is true, we should reject P. Truth-value gluts disrupt this, as accepting both P and ¬P leads to cognitive dissonance. For the Liar Paradox, Williamson proposes an epistemicist solution, where the sentence has a determinate but unknowable truth value, preserving classical bivalence and semantic stability.
* Jc Beall (2000, "On Truth and Dialetheism"): Beall criticizes truth-value gluts for overcomplicating semantics and metaphysics. He advocates a deflationary theory of truth (truth as a minimal property, e.g., "snow is white" is true if snow is white), which avoids gluts by maintaining bivalence. Beall argues that dialetheism’s non-standard semantics introduce unnecessary metaphysical commitments, such as positing states where contradictions hold, destabilizing logical theory.
* Achille C. Varzi (1997, "On the Very Idea of a Dialetheia"): Varzi asserts that true contradictions are semantically untenable. He argues that allowing P∧¬P disrupts the logical structure of language, as truth values become indeterminate across propositions. For example, if a dialetheia is both true and false, how do we evaluate related propositions (e.g., conjunctions or implications)? Varzi contends that this leads to semantic collapse, where distinctions between propositions erode.
Implications:
Semantic instability is a severe critique because logic depends on clear meanings for truth and negation. If dialetheism cannot provide a stable semantics, it undermines reasoning’s reliability, as propositions lose their ability to guide thought coherently. This affects philosophical inquiry, where precise semantics are crucial for debates about knowledge, belief, and reality. The argument suggests that dialetheism’s solution to paradoxes creates more problems than it solves, as semantic ambiguity outweighs any resolution of logical anomalies.

Dialetheist Counterarguments and Rebuttals:
* Counterargument: Priest argues that classical semantics are overly restrictive and fail to handle paradoxes like the Liar intuitively. In LP, truth-value gluts provide a formal model where contradictions are manageable, and paradoxes are resolved by accepting their dual status. He cites examples like borderline cases in vagueness (e.g., "This is a heap and not a heap") as potential dialetheias, suggesting reality may tolerate contradictions.

* Rebuttal: Critics counter that LP’s semantics are less intuitive than classical bivalence, requiring complex adjustments to negation and connectives. Williamson’s epistemicism, for instance, resolves the Liar without gluts, maintaining truth’s normative role. Beall’s deflationary approach avoids metaphysical excess, and Varzi’s critique highlights the impracticality of indeterminate truth values. The counterargument fails to show that gluts are necessary, as classical solutions are more stable and widely applicable.
Depth of Critique:
The argument engages dialetheism at its core—its semantic framework—making it a foundational critique. It draws on formal logic (e.g., truth tables, connectives), metaphysics (truth’s correspondence to reality), and epistemology (truth’s normative role), providing a multi-faceted attack. Its reliance on established semantic theories (e.g., Tarski’s truth definition) strengthens its rigor, while its critique of LP’s non-classical negation exposes dialetheism’s technical weaknesses.

2. Unnecessary Complexity
Detailed Explanation:
Dialetheism is criticized for introducing unnecessary logical and philosophical complexity by positing true contradictions to resolve paradoxes. Classical logic addresses paradoxes through simpler, well-established methods, such as linguistic clarification, semantic distinctions (e.g., truth-value gaps, where statements are neither true nor false), or hierarchical approaches (e.g., separating object and meta-languages). These methods preserve the LNC and bivalence, avoiding the need to overhaul logical foundations. The principle of Occam’s Razor—favoring theories with fewer assumptions—bolsters this critique, as dialetheism requires revising truth, negation, and entailment, creating a more complex system without clear justification.

Consider Russell’s Paradox: whether the set of all sets that do not contain themselves contains itself. In classical logic, this is resolved by type theory (e.g., Russell and Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica), which prohibits self-referential sets, or by Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory, which restricts set formation. These solutions eliminate the paradox without accepting contradictions. Dialetheism, conversely, might treat the paradox as a dialetheia (the set both contains and does not contain itself), requiring paraconsistent logics to manage the contradiction. Critics argue that this approach is unnecessarily complex, as classical solutions are mathematically robust and philosophically conservative.
Similarly, the Liar Paradox is addressed classically through Kripke’s (1975) truth-value gaps (the Liar is neither true nor false) or Tarski’s hierarchy of languages (truth predicates apply to lower-level languages). These avoid contradictions by adjusting semantic rules, whereas dialetheism’s gluts demand a radical redefinition of truth and logic. The added complexity includes non-classical semantics (e.g., LP’s three-valued logic: true, false, both), revised entailment (to avoid explosion), and metaphysical commitments to contradictory states, all of which critics deem extravagant when simpler alternatives exist.

Supporting Examples:
* David Lewis (1982, "Logic for Equivocators"): Lewis argues that paradoxes often arise from equivocation—ambiguous or shifting meanings of terms. For the Liar, he suggests the paradox stems from unclear self-reference, resolvable by refining language (e.g., specifying truth’s context). This linguistic fix avoids true contradictions, making dialetheism’s complex semantics unnecessary.
* Susan Haack (1979, "Do We Need Fuzzy Logic?"): Haack defends classical logic’s ability to handle paradoxes through established methods, such as truth-value gaps or language hierarchies. She argues that non-classical logics, including dialetheism, introduce complexity without improving explanatory power, favoring classical simplicity for philosophical clarity.
* Laurence Goldstein (1999, "The Barber, Russell’s Paradox, and Dialetheism"): Goldstein focuses on paradoxes like the Barber Paradox (a barber shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves). He advocates classical solutions, such as denying the barber’s existence or restricting self-reference, as simpler than dialetheism’s acceptance of true contradictions, which requires paraconsistent frameworks.
Implications:
Unnecessary Complexity challenges dialetheism’s justification, as radical changes to logic require compelling evidence. Classical solutions are entrenched in mathematics (e.g., set theory), philosophy (e.g., semantics), and science, suggesting they are sufficient. Dialetheism’s complexity—revising truth, negation, and entailment—risks alienating its applicability, as philosophers and logicians prefer systems with minimal assumptions. The argument positions dialetheism as a last resort, only warranted if classical logic fails entirely, which critics argue it does not.

Dialetheist Counterarguments and Rebuttals:
* Counterargument: Priest argues that classical solutions are themselves complex and artificial. Type theory restricts mathematical freedom (e.g., banning self-referential sets), and Tarski’s language hierarchy feels ad hoc. Dialetheism, he claims, is simpler by embracing paradoxes as true contradictions, avoiding convoluted workarounds.
* Rebuttal: Critics counter that classical solutions, while technical, are intuitive and integrated into established disciplines (e.g., Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory in mathematics). Dialetheism’s revisions—non-classical negation, paraconsistent entailment—are more disruptive, requiring a complete rethinking of logic. Lewis’s linguistic approach, Haack’s gaps, and Goldstein’s restrictions are less metaphysically extravagant, preserving Occam’s Razor.
Depth of Critique:
The argument engages dialetheism’s methodological foundations, invoking philosophical principles (Occam’s Razor), formal logic (classical vs. paraconsistent systems), and practical considerations (applicability in mathematics). It compares the technical machinery of both approaches, highlighting dialetheism’s additional assumptions (e.g., three-valued semantics) and their philosophical cost. Its reliance on established solutions strengthens its rigor, making it a formidable critique.

3. Philosophical Extravagance
Detailed Explanation:

The LNC ensures logical coherence by prohibiting contradictory states, underpinning reasoning in philosophy, science, and everyday discourse. Dialetheism’s acceptance of true contradictions is seen as counterintuitive and metaphysically excessive, challenging the basic norms of rationality. Critics argue that this rejection lacks sufficient justification and risks opening the door to irrationality, as contradictions traditionally signal errors in thought or reality.
The LNC’s intuitive appeal lies in its alignment with our understanding of reality: an object cannot be wholly red and not red simultaneously, as this violates the coherence of properties. Dialetheism posits that some propositions (e.g., the Liar) are dialetheias, requiring a metaphysics where contradictory states are possible. Critics contend that this is extravagant, as it demands a radical revision of ontology (the nature of being) and epistemology (the nature of knowledge). For example, if P∧¬P is true, how do we know or justify it? The cognitive dissonance of accepting contradictory beliefs undermines rational inquiry.
Furthermore, dialetheism’s rejection of the LNC affects logical connectives, particularly negation. In classical logic, ¬P opposes P, ensuring clarity in reasoning. In dialetheism’s LP, negation allows gluts, weakening its oppositional role. Critics argue that this revision is philosophically costly, as it disrupts the intuitive structure of logic and risks trivializing contradictions, traditionally seen as fatal flaws.
Supporting Examples:

* Achille C. Varzi (1997, "On the Very Idea of a Dialetheia"): Varzi challenges the conceptual coherence of dialetheias, arguing that true contradictions are fundamentally irrational. He contends that accepting them violates intuitive principles of thought, as contradictions signal errors, not truths. Varzi sees dialetheism as an unnecessary departure from philosophical tradition.
Implications:
Philosophical Extravagance threatens dialetheism’s legitimacy by questioning its rational foundations. The LNC is deeply ingrained in Western philosophy, and rejecting it requires overwhelming evidence, which critics argue dialetheism lacks. The argument suggests that dialetheism’s metaphysical and logical revisions are too costly, risking a slide into irrationality where contradictions lose their diagnostic power.

Dialetheist Counterarguments and Rebuttals:

Premises and Logical Development
Premise 1: Canon Requires Minimal Contradictions (NCC)
A canon, as the authoritative narrative of a fictional universe, must be coherent to serve as a reliable basis for interpretation and comparison. The No Canon Contradictions (NCC) principle posits that a canon contains no contradictions, as contradictions disrupt narrative integrity. For example:
* In Dragon Ball, if Goku is canonically “universal-level” in one story but struggles with a building-level threat in another without explanation, this contradiction challenges the canon’s coherence.
* Contradictions serve as analytical tools to identify breaks in continuity. A story containing a contradiction (e.g., “Superman is invulnerable and vulnerable in the same narrative”) suggests it belongs to a different continuity or is non-canonical.
NCC ensures that canon remains a unified narrative by minimizing contradictions, allowing powerscalers to rely on consistent feats and statements for comparisons.
Premise 2: Canon Is a Set of Maximally Consistent Stories (CCP)
The Canon Continuity Principle (CCP) defines a canon as a set of stories that are maximally consistent, meaning they contain the largest possible collection of narratives with no contradictions. This principle promotes:
* Consistency: Stories within a canon align without irreconcilable contradictions, ensuring a coherent universe.
* Parsimony: The number of canons is minimized to avoid unnecessary fragmentation, grouping stories into as few continuities as possible while maintaining consistency.
For instance, DC Comics may have multiple continuities (e.g., Silver Age, Post-Crisis), each a canon defined by CCP as a maximally consistent set. Contradictions (e.g., Superman’s power levels varying across stories) are resolved by assigning stories to distinct canons, preserving each canon’s integrity.

* Narrative incoherence: Stories depend on logical consequences. If a character both "died permanently" and "didn't die" in the same sense, narrative stakes and tension disappear completely.
By selectively applying dialetheism, these analysts effectively create an unfalsifiable framework where evidence cannot constrain claims, while still pretending to engage in reasoned analysis.
The fundamental problem is that accepting true contradictions doesn't enhance analysis—it eliminates the possibility of any meaningful analysis at all.
Refutation of the "Lesser vs. Greater Contradictions" Argument
This argument creates an arbitrary distinction between "lesser" and "greater" contradictions to preserve the Tier 0 concept. Here's why this approach fails:
The distinction creates special pleading without logical foundation. By claiming that some contradictions (those convenient for preserving Tier 0 status) are acceptable while others are disqualifying, the argument introduces an arbitrary standard with no objective basis. What makes a contradiction "lesser" versus "greater" other than its impact on the preferred tier assignment?
This approach actually undermines the very concept of Tier 0. If a supposedly ineffable, omnipotent being exhibits any contradiction whatsoever—even a "lesser" one—it immediately fails to meet the criteria of being an undifferentiated unity or truly omnipotent. A genuinely omnipotent being couldn't fail at anything, as failure implies limitation. Once we accept that the being has limitations, it no longer qualifies as Tier 0 by definition.
The "universe level being failing to planet bust" example reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of contradictions in logic. This isn't merely a "lesser" contradiction—it's a direct violation of the transitive property that defines power scaling. If A > B > C, then A cannot be < C. When we observe such contradictions, the proper conclusion is that our categorization of the entity was incorrect to begin with, not that we should selectively ignore contradictions.
The proposed category of "lesser contradictions" ultimately serves as an escape hatch to protect preferred interpretations from logical scrutiny. Either contradictions invalidate claims or they don't—creating a subjective hierarchy of which contradictions "count" destroys the entire system's logical foundation.
Refutation of the "Larger Set" Approach
This approach fundamentally misunderstands how evidence and contradictions function in logical analysis:
Treating contradictions as simply "data points" to be counted rather than logical impossibilities misrepresents their nature. A contradiction isn't just another piece of evidence—it signifies that something is fundamentally wrong with our understanding or categorization. Contradictions don't merely add or subtract from a tally; they invalidate entire interpretations.
The "larger set" approach implies a democratic view of truth where reality is determined by majority vote. This is a classic logical fallacy (argumentum ad populum). If a character shows planetary-level power in 99 instances but universal power in 1 instance, the correct conclusion isn't "mostly planetary with one outlier"—it's "planetary level with one inconsistency requiring explanation."
This approach makes falsifiability impossible. If any number of contradictions can be outweighed by a larger set of consistent data, then essentially any claim becomes unfalsifiable. For example, we could claim a character is omnipotent and simply dismiss all evidence of limitations as the "smaller set of contradicting data."
The suggested methodology lacks any principled decision rule for when the smaller set becomes significant enough to matter. Is it purely numerical? Does context matter? Without clear criteria, this approach devolves into subjective preference rather than logical analysis.
In sound analytical frameworks, contradictions serve as signals that our interpretation requires revision. They aren't merely data points to be weighed against consistent evidence—they indicate conceptual problems that demand resolution through better theories, recognition of outliers, or acknowledgment of authorial inconsistency.
How the "Larger Set" Approach Makes Claims Unfalsifiable
The "larger set" argument suggests we can analyze two contradicting sets of data, selecting the larger set while not dismissing the smaller set as false. This approach fundamentally undermines falsifiability in several critical ways:
The Problem of Cumulative Evidence vs. Disqualifying Evidence
In science and logic, certain evidence doesn't just add to a tally—it can invalidate entire theories regardless of how much supporting evidence exists. Consider the following:
Imagine a character is shown to be vulnerable to ordinary bullets in just one scene. Meanwhile, in twenty other scenes, they're portrayed as invulnerable to all physical attacks. The "larger set" approach would conclude they're "mostly invulnerable" instead of recognizing that vulnerability to ordinary bullets fundamentally contradicts true invulnerability.
This is analogous to physics: if we drop an object 10,000 times and it falls downward, but once it falls upward without explanation, we wouldn't say "gravity works 99.99% of the time." Instead, that single contradiction would demand a complete revision of our understanding of gravity.
The Moving Target Problem
The "larger set" approach creates a moving target where any claim becomes nearly impossible to disprove:
For any character claimed to be omnipotent, advocates can simply treat all limitations as the "smaller set" of data.
As counterexamples accumulate, they can continually reframe what constitutes the "larger" and "smaller" sets by:
* Reinterpreting contradictory evidence ("they were holding back")
* Counting instances differently (treating each page showing power as separate evidence while grouping limitations together)
* Assigning different weights to different types of evidence (valuing statements over shown feats or vice versa)
When contradictions reach a tipping point, they can shift to claiming "most of the time" the character is omnipotent, preserving the essential claim in weakened form.
The Lack of Threshold Criteria
The approach provides no principled criteria for when the "smaller set" becomes significant enough to matter:
* Is it purely numerical? (51% vs 49%)
* Does recency matter? (newer evidence outweighs older)
* Does source authority matter? (creator statements outweigh in-universe demonstrations)
* How are outliers identified versus legitimate contradictions?
Without clear thresholds and criteria, the framework allows unlimited flexibility in interpretation. Any limitation can be dismissed as an outlier, anomaly, or simply belonging to the "smaller set."
The Impossibility of Decisive Counterexamples
In proper scientific and logical reasoning, certain observations can definitively falsify a claim regardless of supporting evidence. The "larger set" approach eliminates this critical feature:
Consider the claim: "This character can destroy universes at will." Under conventional logic, showing the character failing to destroy a planet at full power would be disqualifying evidence. Under the "larger set" approach, this would just be filed under "smaller set of contradicting data" and the claim remains essentially intact.
A Practical Example
Let's apply this to a concrete example to see how it makes claims unfalsifiable:
Claim: "Character X is a true omnipotent being (Tier 0)"
Evidence against:
* Character X is shown being harmed by Character Y
* Character X fails to accomplish something they tried to do
* Character X needs to rest after using powers
* Character X admits they have limitations
Under rigorous logical analysis, any one of these points disqualifies Character X from true omnipotence. But under the "larger set" approach:
"We have 50 instances where Character X displays seemingly unlimited power and only 4 instances of limitations. The larger set suggests Character X is omnipotent, while not dismissing the smaller set as false."
This framework makes the claim of omnipotence essentially unfalsifiable because no amount of contradictory evidence can decisively refute it—it will always just be categorized as the "smaller set."
The False Equivalence Problem
The approach creates a false equivalence between different types of evidence:
A single demonstration of a logical impossibility (like a supposedly omnipotent being failing to do something) is qualitatively different from a thousand demonstrations of power. The former contradicts the definition of omnipotence, while the latter merely supports a claim of great power.
By treating these as merely different "sets of data" to be weighed quantitatively, the approach fundamentally misunderstands how logical definitions and contradictions work.
Conclusion
The "larger set" approach essentially replaces falsifiability with a form of confirmation bias. Instead of asking "What evidence would disprove this claim?" It focuses on "How much supporting evidence can we accumulate?" This shift fundamentally breaks the logical framework needed for meaningful analysis, creating a system where preferred interpretations become immune to contradiction and therefore unfalsifiable.

I'll present the strongest possible counterarguments against the claims that anyone can make any work canon or non-canon to any other, then analyze why these counterarguments ultimately fail.
Counterargument 1: Ownership and Legal Authority
Steelman Position: The distinction between canon and fanfiction is fundamentally a legal and ownership-based distinction, not a logical one. Intellectual property rights give creators and publishers exclusive legal authority to determine what constitutes their fictional universes. This authority isn't arbitrary—it's based on recognized property rights that society has established to protect creative works.
Debunking: This argument confuses legal authority with logical coherence. While copyright holders can legally control official publications, this doesn't resolve the logical problem. The legal right to declare something "official" doesn't magically resolve contradictions or create logical consistency. Consider:
1. Legal ownership frequently transfers between different entities (Disney buying Star Wars)
2. Different legal rights holders can produce contradictory "official" works (different Batman continuities)
3. Legal authority can retroactively declare previously "official" works non-canonical (Star Wars Expanded Universe)
These demonstrate that legal authority itself operates exactly like the "larger set" problem described in the original document—it's an external framework imposed on the fiction rather than a property of the fiction's internal logic. Legal ownership may determine what's "official," but it cannot determine what's logically consistent.
Counterargument 2: Author's Communicative Intent
Steelman Position: Canon is determined by authorial intent and the communication between author and audience. The original creator established a communicative framework that readers entered into when engaging with the work. Fanfiction violates this original communicative contract by introducing elements the author never intended to communicate.
Debunking: This argument fails for several reasons:
1. Authors frequently contradict their own earlier communicative intent (George Lucas with Star Wars special editions)
2. Works with multiple authors have multiple, often conflicting intents (long-running comic series)
3. Dead authors cannot clarify their intent for new situations or interpretations
4. Many works are intentionally created to allow multiple interpretations
More fundamentally, this argument creates a false equivalence between "what the author intended" and "what constitutes a logically consistent universe." The fact that authors frequently create internal contradictions proves that authorial intent itself doesn't guarantee logical consistency.
Counterargument 3: Hierarchical Canon Systems
Steelman Position: Many franchises employ explicit hierarchical canon systems that resolve contradictions. For example, Star Wars had G-canon, C-canon, S-canon, etc. These frameworks provide explicit rules for resolving contradictions and determining what "counts," making the distinction between canon and fanfiction clear and systematic.
Debunking: Hierarchical canon systems actually prove the original point rather than counter it. Their very existence acknowledges that contradictions are inevitable and require external frameworks to resolve. Furthermore:
1. These hierarchies are themselves arbitrary and frequently revised (as happened with Star Wars)
2. They create a "compatibility" spectrum rather than a binary canon/non-canon distinction
3. They implicitly acknowledge that logical consistency alone is insufficient for determining canonicity
If canon requires arbitrary hierarchical systems to function, then the distinction is indeed based on authority rather than logical necessity—exactly as the original argument claimed.
Counterargument 4: Audience Consensus
Steelman Position: Canon is determined by broad audience consensus about what constitutes the "real" version of a fictional universe. While individual interpretations may vary, the collective understanding of a sufficiently large audience creates practical boundaries between canon and fanfiction that aren't arbitrary but emergent from collective interpretation.
Debunking: This argument fails because audience consensus is:
1. Constantly shifting and never truly universal
2. Often split between multiple valid interpretations (Kirk vs. Picard fans)
3. Frequently overruled by publishers/creators despite strong consensus
4. Subject to the same logical problems as individual interpretation
More importantly, consensus doesn't solve logical contradictions—it merely normalizes certain contradictions while rejecting others. This is precisely the "special pleading" problem identified in the original document.
Counterargument 5: Pragmatic Utility
Steelman Position: The distinction between canon and fanfiction has practical utility for discussion and analysis even if it's not philosophically airtight. Setting boundaries around what constitutes the "official" version enables meaningful discussion of fictional worlds without infinite regress into what could be included.
Debunking: This argument openly abandons logical consistency in favor of convenience. It admits that the distinction is arbitrary but defends it on utilitarian grounds. However:
1. The same pragmatic utility could be achieved through other frameworks (like discussing specific works rather than "canon")
2. The utility varies dramatically between different approaches to fiction
3. The pragmatic approach still requires arbitrary decisions about what's "useful" to include
This counterargument essentially concedes the original point that the distinction lacks logical necessity while trying to preserve it on practical grounds.
Conclusion: The Fundamental Logical Problem Remains
All of these counterarguments ultimately fail because they don't address the core logical issue: if internal contradictions are permissible within canon (as they demonstrably are), then there can be no logical basis for excluding works based solely on contradiction. The distinction between canon and fanfiction thus reduces to arbitrary authority rather than logical necessity.
This doesn't mean the distinction has no practical use or legal significance—only that it cannot be justified on purely logical grounds, which was the original claim.

Introduction
It's common on some wikis, those descended from OBD or VSBW, to say that a character is infinitely powerful based on things like feats, scaling, cosmology, and so on. While this is fine by itself, it is a major problem when anti-feats exist. This post will go over the various anti-feats of infinitely powerful characters and why those are disqualifications.
The Fundamental Problem of Portraying Infinity
Visual Limitations and the Statement Dependency
Infinity, by its very nature, cannot be visually portrayed in any medium. This creates a fundamental issue when discussing infinite power levels in fiction:
1. Visual Impossibility: No image, animation, or visual sequence can show something truly infinite. A character destroying a planet, a galaxy, or even multiple universes still only shows a finite level of power. The gap between destroying 100 universes and infinity is still infinite.
2. Statement Dependency: Claims of infinite power in fiction almost exclusively rely on narrative statements, character dialogue, or supplementary materials. A narrator might say "his power was infinite" or a character might claim to possess "infinite strength," but these are statements rather than demonstrated feats.
3. The Feat-Statement Contradiction: When actual feats contradict these statements of infinity, we face an important question: which should take precedence? A character stated to have "infinite power" who then struggles to lift a mountain presents a direct contradiction. The feat (struggling with the mountain) directly contradicts the statement (having infinite power).
4. Feat Primacy: In battleboarding communities, feats typically take precedence over statements when contradictions arise. What a character actually demonstrates carries more weight than what is claimed about them. This is because feats represent what a character can definitively achieve in their fictional reality.
How This Can Be Falsified
This position could be falsified by a genuine visual portrayal of infinity, without relying on statements. However, this appears to be impossible within the constraints of visual media:
1. The Visualization Challenge: To visually portray infinite power would require showing endless capability without limit. No matter how extensive the demonstration, it would always remain finite.
2. Theoretical Requirements: A true visual demonstration of infinity would need to show:
   * A character affecting literally everything that exists simultaneously
   * Performing actions that require unlimited energy with zero effort
   * Existing in all possible states at once
   * Transcending all possible contextual restraints
3. The Paradox: Any attempt to show infinite capability in a finite medium (comic panels, animation frames, novel pages) will necessarily fall short. The very act of portrayal imposes limitations that contradict infinity.
This is why, when examining claims of infinite power, we must be especially attentive to anti-feats—instances where characters demonstrate limitations that would be impossible for a truly infinite being. These anti-feats falsify the statement-based claims of infinity and ground our understanding in what characters actually demonstrate they can do.
Anti-Feats For Infinite Power
1. Powering Up/Powering Down
2. Tiring
3. Exerting Effort/Struggling
4. Not One Shotting Finite Opponents
5. Having Scaling of Any Sort
Why these?
For one, the explanation is simple. Let's say you are infinitely powerful, and power up. You can power up in purely additive amounts, or multiply your power. However, you won't get any stronger if you're infinitely powerful. This is because adding to infinity, multiplying it, dividing it, subtracting it by a finite number, does not increase or decrease infinity at all. Infinity is not a normal number.
For two, the explanation is a little more complex. All physical actions cost stamina. Stamina is like fuel. The more intensive your physical actions are, the more it drains your stamina. The longer you physically move, the more it drains your stamina. An infinitely intensive physical action would drain your stamina infinitely, so you wouldn't be able to fire it off if you had finite stamina. You would die of exhaustion. If you run out of stamina, by definition, you have finite stamina.
For three, the explanation is like so. Exerting effort means you're reaching the limits of your strength. Struggling means you're pushing up against them. Infinite power doesn't have any limits. If you bring in higher infinities into the mix, you still have no leeway. Either the action requires effort of whatever cardinality you have, in which case you do it with 0 effort, or the action requires effort of a higher cardinality, in which case, you can't do it at all no matter how hard you try.
For four, this is again very simple. If you hit infinitely hard, then you will kill anyone in one hit if they have finite durability. The same applies to beings of a lower cardinality. Also, you can't hold back if you have infinite power, as 0.00000000000000000001% of infinity = infinity.
For five, this is again very simple. If you are infinitely powerful, you either have people infinitely below you, infinitely above you, or just as powerful as you. There's no 'slightly stronger' or 'slightly weaker'. Those are the only three options.
Anti-feats for infinite durability
1. Getting harmed.
Why this?
Being harmed suggests that your durability has limits.
Here's an analogy. Let's say you have 10 durability. Attacks that do 1, 2, 3, or 4 damage do nothing to you. 5-6 damage scrapes you, 7-8 damage bruises you, and 9-10-above damage severely injures you. The higher the damage is above your durability, the more hurt you are. The more durability you have, the higher your threshold for all of those things are.
The thing is that this suggests limits on how much damage you can ignore, how much damage is needed to injure you, and how much damage is needed to kill you. Limits are not something infinity has. By definition. I harm someone with infinite durability with 99999999999999 damage, they take no damage. I attack them with infinite damage, they take no damage.
With higher infinities, you are one shot if you have a lower cardinality compared to the attack. Equal or lower cardinality attacks do nothing to you no matter how much they are repeated.
Also if you're not physical you don't have durability in the first place.
Anti-feats for infinite speed
1. Moving in sequential order
2. Get tired
3. Fail to reach certain places in a certain time
4. Being outsped*
Why These?
For one, it's because infinite speed would mean you start and finish all actions instantly. In 0 time. You can slow things down as much as you want. You still won't see any sequence because by definition you take every action at the same time. There's no in-between, no matter how small.
For two, refer to number two of the section about infinite power.
For three, the definition of infinite speed means 'move infinitely fast', which would mean you can visit every place in an infinite universe at once. So you should not be worrying about not having enough time at all.
For four, I don't really know if you can have transfinite speed, but I don't think you do. If transfinite speed is valid, then anyone with a higher cardinality of speed would see you as frozen, just as someone with aleph-null speed would see finitely fast beings as frozen. In both cases, there's no 'race'. You wouldn't be able to see the higher cardinal guy MOVE at all.
The Consequences
Most characters claimed to be infinite get nerfed. Comic characters, anime characters, etc. Since the requirements are extremely stringent, almost no characters qualify, aside from works where action is not the main focus. A story about punching gods in the face means no one is infinite, but a story about distant gods that are like forces of nature could have infinitely powerful gods.

Fiction-Specific Logic and Cross-Series Comparisons
When discussing infinitely powerful characters across different fictional settings, we encounter a fundamental problem. If we allow each fictional universe to define its own rules of how infinity works, these definitions cannot be meaningfully transferred across series.
The Problem of Different Axioms
If fictional works can establish their own axioms regarding infinity, then comparing characters from different works becomes incoherent. Consider the following:
1. Series A: Defines an "infinitely powerful" character who can still tire after extended exertion
2. Series B: Defines an "infinitely powerful" character who is immune to all forms of harm
These two definitions of "infinite power" are inherently contradictory. In Series A, infinity has limits; in Series B, it doesn't. When comparing these characters, whose definition of infinity should prevail?

When settings have contradictory foundational rules, they become mutually exclusive systems:
* Fiction A might state: "A character with infinite power can still be harmed by certain specific attacks"
* Fiction B might state: "A character with infinite power cannot be harmed by any attack whatsoever"
These premises directly contradict each other. If we honor both definitions simultaneously, we create an irresolvable paradox where the same concept has mutually exclusive properties.
The Impossibility of Cross-Series Interaction
Without a neutral framework to judge both settings, characters from these contradictory works cannot meaningfully interact in debates or versus scenarios:
* If we use Series A's rules, then Series B's character isn't truly "infinite" as defined in their own universe
* If we use Series B's rules, then Series A's character isn't truly "infinite" as defined in their own universe
* If we try to create a new neutral framework, we're no longer honoring either original setting

For cross-series comparisons to work at all, we need standardized definitions of concepts like infinite power, durability, and speed. These standards must override any fiction-specific definitions to allow for meaningful comparison.
This is why the anti-feats described earlier are so important. They provide a consistent framework for evaluating claims of infinity across all fictional settings, rather than allowing each setting to define infinity in contradictory ways.
Conclusion on Cross-Series Comparisons
When a character demonstrates any of the anti-feats listed previously, they simply aren't infinite by any standardized definition, regardless of what their specific fiction claims. Accepting fiction-specific definitions of infinity would make cross-series battleboarding fundamentally impossible, as each series could simply define their characters as "infinitely powerful in ways that specifically counter any other series' infinite characters."
For debate to remain coherent, we must either:
1. Apply consistent standards of infinity across all fictions, or
2. Acknowledge that truly different systems of logic between fictions make meaningful comparison impossible
In either case, the anti-feats remain valid disqualifiers for claims of infinite power.
The Problem of Authorial Authority in Cross-Fiction Debates
Introduction
When fans debate which fictional characters would win in hypothetical confrontations across different fictional universes, they face a fundamental problem: how do we compare characters governed by different sets of rules created by different authors? Some argue that author statements should be the ultimate authority, transcending even logic.. This analysis examines the consequences of this view and why it creates insurmountable problems for cross-fiction debates.
1. Reductio ad Absurdum: The Consequences of Unlimited Authorial Authority
If we accept that an author's statements about their creation have absolute authority that transcends any framework of comparison, several absurd consequences follow:
A. The "Unbeatable Character" Problem
If authorial declaration is the highest authority, any author could create an "absolutely unbeatable character" by simply declaring them so. Consider:
* Author A: "My character Alex is unbeatable against all other fictional characters."
* Author B: "My character Beth is unbeatable against all other fictional characters."
These statements create a direct contradiction when comparing Alex and Beth. Both cannot simultaneously be true, yet if authorial authority is supreme, we must accept both. This creates an irresolvable paradox that renders meaningful comparison impossible.
In practice, this would mean any cross-fiction debate could be instantly resolved by simply quoting the author making the most expansive claim about their character's capabilities.
B. The "Universal Canon" Problem
The separation between fictional universes is maintained by recognizing boundaries of ownership and canon. If authorial declaration transcends these boundaries:
* Any author could declare: "My fiction encompasses all other fictions as subordinate realities."
* Any fan-fiction writer could declare their work canonical to the original universe.
* Any interpretation would have equal standing regardless of consistency with the original work.
This would collapse all fictional boundaries, making meaningful distinction between separate works impossible. Dragon Ball and Star Wars would simultaneously be and not be part of the same universe.
C. The "Arbitrariness" Problem
Without constraints on authorial authority, any statement about a character becomes equally valid:
* "Superman is actually vulnerable to bananas."
* "Darth Vader secretly worships Hello Kitty."
* "All Marvel characters are actually aware they're in a comic book."
These statements would carry full authority simply by being declared, regardless of their coherence with established works.
2. The Problem of Incompatible Frameworks
When fictional universes operate under contradictory rules, attempting to compare them creates fundamental inconsistencies:
A. Mutually Exclusive Metaphysics
Different fictions often establish fundamentally incompatible metaphysical rules:
* Universe A: "Magic is the fundamental force that supersedes all physical laws."
* Universe B: "Magic doesn't exist; all seemingly supernatural phenomena have scientific explanations."
When characters from these universes interact, whose metaphysics apply? If Character X's powers depend on magic working, but in Universe B magic doesn't work, there's no neutral framework for comparison.
B. Contradictory Absolute Powers
Many fictions establish characters with supposedly "absolute" powers that create direct contradictions when compared:
* Character X: "Can destroy anything, including indestructible objects."
* Character Y: "Cannot be destroyed by any means."
When these characters meet, the result is a logical impossibility. Either X's power to destroy everything fails, or Y's indestructibility fails. There is no coherent way to resolve this contradiction while preserving both characters' established capabilities.
C. Recursive Reality Levels
Some fictions establish nested levels of reality that create comparison problems:
* Universe A: "All fictional worlds exist as books within our world."
* Universe B: "Our world contains and controls all possible realities."
These create an irresolvable recursive paradox when compared.
3. Steelman Counterarguments and Refutations
Counterargument 1: The "Neutral Framework" Solution
Steelman: We can resolve cross-fiction debates by establishing a neutral framework that harmonizes contradictory elements. Just as different scientific theories can be reconciled within broader paradigms, fictional universes can be compared by finding common elements between them.
Refutation: This approach fails because:
1. Creating a neutral framework necessarily privileges some aspects of each fictional world while discarding others. This undermines the authority of the original works.
2. A truly neutral framework would need to simultaneously accommodate contradictory principles. For example, if Universe A establishes "only divine weapons can harm gods" and Universe B establishes "science can overcome any supernatural protection," a neutral framework cannot preserve both principles.
3. The neutral framework itself becomes a third fictional construct distinct from either original universe, essentially creating fan fiction rather than a valid comparison of the original works.
Counterargument 2: The "Demonstrated Feats" Solution
Steelman: We should ignore author statements and focus only on demonstrated feats. By comparing objective accomplishments shown within the works themselves, we avoid the problems of authorial authority.
Refutation: This approach still faces several problems:
1. Feats occur within specific contextual frameworks. A "universe-destroying" feat in Universe A might operate under different physical laws than Universe B, making direct comparison impossible.
2. Interpreting feats still requires assumptions about how abilities work across universes. If Character X can move faster than light in their universe, does this translate directly to another universe with different physical laws?
3. Many fictional works deliberately leave certain capabilities ambiguous or undefined. Focusing solely on demonstrated feats privileged explicit stories over implicit worldbuilding.
Counterargument 3: The "Multiverse Accommodation" Solution
Steelman: Many fictional universes already contain multiverses with different rules. We can simply treat cross-fiction comparisons as another type of multiversal interaction, where characters retain their capabilities across universal boundaries.
Refutation: This approach fails because:
1. Even fictional multiverses typically maintain consistent meta-rules governing how inter-universal travel and power interaction works. These meta-rules are specific to each fictional setting and often contradict those in other settings.
2. A multiverse approach still doesn't resolve fundamental contradictions. If Universe A's multiverse operates under the principle that "no being can be omnipotent" while Universe B's multiverse includes truly omnipotent beings, the contradiction remains.
3. This approach essentially privileges the most expansive cosmology, favoring settings with broader multiversal structures over more contained narratives.
Counterargument 4: The "Authorial Intent" Solution
Steelman: We should focus on what authors intended about their characters' capabilities rather than their explicit statements. By examining the narrative purpose and consistent portrayal of characters, we can determine their "true" capabilities.
Refutation: This approach remains problematic because:
1. Authorial intent is often ambiguous, contradictory, or evolves over time. Many works have multiple authors with different visions.
2. Intent doesn't resolve contradictions between settings. Two authors may have intended their characters to be "the most powerful being in existence," creating the same fundamental contradiction.
3. This approach privileges certain interpretations of authorial intent over others, creating arbitrary hierarchies of interpretation rather than objective comparison.

Why Author Authority Fails to Justify Canon
Introduction
In literary analysis and powerscaling, canon—the authoritative narrative—must be logically consistent to serve as a stable basis for interpretation and comparison. Author authority, where an author’s declarations define canon, is often invoked but is fundamentally flawed. This analysis demonstrates that there is no good reason to use author authority to justify canon, as it is question-begging, unreliable, destructive to canon boundaries, disruptive to powerscaling, and philosophically arbitrary. Instead, canon should be defined by logical principles like No Canon Contradictions (NCC) and Canon Continuity Principle (CCP), ensuring coherence and analytical rigor.
Arguments Against Author Authority
1. Begging the Question
Defining the author as the sole arbiter of canon assumes their authority without justification, committing the fallacy of begging the question. For example, if J.K. Rowling declares a contradictory Harry Potter story canon (e.g., “Harry is a Muggle”), its validity rests on her say-so, not narrative coherence. This circularity—assuming authority to justify authority—makes canon arbitrary, undermining powerscaling’s need for a consistent evidence base (e.g., feats over statements).
2. Ambiguous or Contradictory Intent
Authors’ intents are often ambiguous, evolving, or contradictory, making them unreliable. George Lucas’s shifting Star Wars canon (e.g., Special Editions, Expanded Universe’s status) shows conflicting “official” narratives. In Marvel Comics, multiple writers produce contradictory Thor portrayals, complicating authority. External pressures (e.g., Disney’s Star Wars decisions) further dilute intent. In powerscaling, contradictory statements (e.g., “The One-Above-All is omnipotent” vs. limitations) disrupt evidence hierarchies, favoring feats for stability.
3. Collapse of Canon/Non-Canon Divide
Relying on author authority creates a fluid canon where contradictory declarations (e.g., “This is canon” and “This is not canon”) coexist, making everything canon and nothing non-canon. If a Dragon Ball author declares fan fiction canon, but another disagrees, both claims are valid, erasing boundaries. In powerscaling, this allows fan claims (e.g., “Goku is omnipotent”) to rival official feats, rendering analysis incoherent. NCC and CCP maintain logical boundaries instead.
4. Disruption of Powerscaling’s Logic
Powerscaling relies on transitivity (A > B > C), hierarchical tiers, and falsifiability (feats > statements). Author authority introduces contradictions (e.g., “Batman > Superman” despite feats) that break these pillars. For instance, accepting an author’s claim that DC’s Batman is stronger than Superman disrupts transitive chains (Batman > Superman > Darkseid), making hierarchies indeterminate. The Powerscaler's Fork demands a universal standard (X), rooted in non-contradiction, over authorial fiat.
5. Philosophical Arbitrariness
Author authority lacks a non-arbitrary basis, privileging one source over narrative logic or reader interpretation without justification. If Akira Toriyama declares Goku omnipotent despite finite feats, prioritizing this over evidence is arbitrary, violating rational norms. Literary analysis treats texts as autonomous, prioritizing internal logic (e.g., feats). Collaborative works (Marvel) and corporate oversight (Disney) further weaken authorial control, making it philosophically tenuous.
6. Legal Rights
Some might say that legal rights are a basis for author authority. But this would be mistaken. Legal rights denote a legal normative claim, that is to say, it should be that someone who has legal rights over a story has the right to alter it. This says nothing of whether their alterations are considered true or not.
Practical Consequences for Powerscaling
Relying on author authority:
* Unfalsifiability: Declarations (e.g., “Character X is unbeatable”) override contradictory feats.
* Inconsistent Hierarchies: Contradictory statements disrupt transitivity and tiers.
* Incomparability: Differing author declarations create incommensurable systems, negating cross-series powerscaling.
NCC and CCP ensure a stable, evidence-based canon instead.

A Philosophical Defense of Abstract Powers
This argument establishes that abstract powers—those rooted in non-physical, non-spatial, mind-independent concepts—exist independently, transcend conventional power hierarchies, cannot be resisted, meet strict definitional criteria, and withstand counterarguments. Each of the five arguments below is presented in its strongest form, forming a cohesive case grounded in formal logic, modal reasoning, and philosophical tradition.
Argument 1: Abstract Concepts Exist Independent of Physical Space-Time
Thesis: Abstract concepts, such as mathematical truths, logical principles, and fundamental laws, exist independently of physical space-time and are not contingent upon physical reality.
Reasoning:
* Philosophical Foundation: Platonism, a well-established tradition, posits that abstract entities (e.g., numbers, geometric forms) inhabit a non-physical realm of forms, existing necessarily across all possible worlds. For instance, the truth of "2 + 2 = 4" holds regardless of physical conditions or observers, suggesting an existence beyond material constraints.
* Modal Necessity: Abstract concepts are necessary truths. In modal logic, a proposition is necessary if it is true in all possible worlds. Consider the law of non-contradiction ((¬ (P \land ¬P))): it governs any coherent reality, as its negation leads to logical collapse. This necessity implies independence from contingent physical frameworks.
* Empirical Invariance: Physical laws (e.g., gravity) depend on abstract principles (e.g., mathematical equations). If physical space-time were altered, these principles would persist, as they are presupposed by any coherent physical system. For example, Einstein’s equations rely on mathematical structures that predate their physical application.
* Countering Nominalism: Nominalism, which denies the independent existence of abstracts, struggles to explain the universality of mathematical truths without invoking physical instantiation, which risks circularity (e.g., numbers exist only as physical marks, but physical marks presuppose numerical concepts). Platonism avoids this by positing a non-physical realm, which, while not empirically verifiable, is philosophically coherent and intuitive.
Strength: This argument is robust due to its alignment with a longstanding philosophical tradition, its use of modal logic to establish necessity, and its intuitive appeal (e.g., mathematical truths feel timeless). Its primary vulnerability—reliance on a metaphysical framework not universally accepted—is mitigated by the incoherence of alternatives like nominalism in explaining universal truths.
Argument 2: Abstract Reality Warping Is Beyond "Power Levels"
Thesis: Abstract powers, which manipulate the foundational rules of reality, transcend conventional power hierarchies, as they operate on a categorically distinct ontological level.
Reasoning:
* Categorical Distinctness: Physical powers (e.g., lifting a planet) operate within reality’s rules, while abstract powers (e.g., altering causality) manipulate those rules. Comparing them is a category error, akin to measuring "loudness" against "color." Abstract powers define the framework; physical powers are bound by it.
* Recursive Priority: Any power-ranking system relies on consistent rules (e.g., energy output, force). Abstract powers can redefine these rules, creating a logical recursion: the system cannot measure what defines the system. For example, if "power" is energy-based, an abstract power could redefine "energy," nullifying the metric.
* Gödelian Limitation: Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems show that no consistent formal system can fully describe itself. Similarly, no power-ranking system can capture powers that manipulate its logical foundations, as it would require self-referential completeness, which is impossible.
* Modal Logic Formalization: In modal terms, physical powers operate within possible worlds (e.g., moving objects in world W1), while abstract powers govern accessibility relations between worlds (e.g., redefining "motion" across all worlds). Formally:
   * Physical power: (∃ w ∈ W: P(w)), where (P) is an action in world (w).
   * Abstract power: (∀ w, w' ∈ W: R(w, w')), where (R) defines the relation between worlds.
   * The latter logically precedes the former, as it determines the structure of possible worlds.
* Refined Analogy: A programmer doesn’t compete with in-program characters because they control the code defining all metrics (e.g., "strength"). If "power level" is coded, rewriting the code overrides any measurement, placing the programmer beyond the system.
Strength: This argument is now robust, grounded in formal logic (category theory, Gödel’s theorems, modal logic) rather than intuition. It addresses objections about effects ("they transcend measurement") and hierarchies ("they operate priorly"), avoiding circularity by demonstrating logical necessity.
Argument 3: Abstract Powers Cannot Be Logically Resisted
Thesis: Entities participating in abstract concepts cannot resist their manipulation, as such resistance is logically incoherent.
Reasoning:
* Logical Dependence: Any entity (E) resisting manipulation of concept (C) (e.g., identity) requires a resistance property (R). However, (R) depends on abstract principles (e.g., logical consistency, existence). Manipulating these principles alters (R), creating an infinite regress: resistance always rests on manipulable abstractions.
* Formal Contradiction: Define:
   * (A(x)): "x is affected by abstract concept manipulation."
   * (P(x,c)): "x participates in concept c."
   * Resistance claim: (∃ x ∃ c [P(x,c) \land ¬ A(x,c)]).
   * Refutation: Participation entails being affected: (∀ x ∀ c [P(x,c) → A(x,c)]). For example, if (x) relies on "identity" ((c)), manipulating "identity" affects (x). The claim is contradictory.
* Non-Classical Logics: Non-classical logics (e.g., paraconsistent) might permit apparent contradictions, but they still rely on meta-logical principles (e.g., coherence of discourse), which are abstract and manipulable. Resistance remains incoherent.
* Practical Example: If an entity resists manipulation of "causality," it must operate causally to resist (e.g., exert force). Altering "causality" undermines the resistance mechanism, as it depends on the very concept being manipulated.
Strength: This argument is logically airtight, using symbolic logic to prove a contradiction and addressing loopholes like non-classical logics. Its dependence on Argument 1 (abstracts exist) is a strength, as it builds on a solid foundation, though it assumes acceptance of that premise.
Argument 4: Debunking Counter-Arguments About Resistance
Thesis: All counterarguments claiming resistance to abstract manipulation fail due to logical impossibilities inherent in their premises.
Reasoning:
* Axiomatic Dependence: Resistance requires a property (R), but (R) depends on abstract principles (e.g., logic). Manipulating these undermines (R), as shown in Argument 3’s regress.
* Property Identity Challenge: Resistance to manipulation of concept (C) requires (C)-independent properties:
   * If (C)-independent, the properties are indefinable within any framework involving (C) (e.g., resisting "logic" with non-logical properties is incoherent).
   * If definable, they involve (C) and are vulnerable to manipulation.
   * Formally: For entity (E) to resist (C), (E) needs property (P) such that (¬P(C)). But all coherent (P) are defined via abstracts, so (∀ P: P(C)), contradicting the premise.
* Meta-Level Dilemma: "Meta-level" resistances (e.g., "resisting all concept manipulation") either:
   * Rely on deeper abstractions (e.g., "resistance" as a concept), making them manipulable.
   * Claim abstraction-independence, rendering them incoherent (a "thing" without properties).
* Engaging Strong Objections: Consider a "steelman" counterargument: an entity with infinite meta-level resistance. This fails because:
   * Infinite regress still depends on abstract principles (e.g., infinity as a concept).
   * Non-abstract resistance (e.g., physical immunity) is irrelevant, as it operates within reality, not on its rules.
Strength: This argument systematically dismantles resistance claims using formal logic and engages robust objections. It complements Argument 3 by addressing speculative counterarguments, though it relies on prior premises, making it slightly less foundational but highly rigorous.
Argument 5: Criteria That Disqualify Powers From Being Truly Abstract
Thesis: Powers failing specific criteria—spatial independence, compositional independence, physical immunity, locational transcendence, and perceptual transcendence—are not truly abstract, as these criteria are logically entailed by the nature of abstractions.
Reasoning:
* Spatial Independence: Abstracts are necessary across all possible worlds. A power tied to spatial dimensions (e.g., "exists only in 3D") varies with spatial properties, contradicting necessity. Formally: If concept (C) depends on dimension (D), then (∃ W: ¬ C(W)), but abstracts require (∀ W: C(W)). (Only use this if abstracts are considered to be necessary in the world)
* Compositional Independence: Abstracts lack physical properties. A power with composition (e.g., "made of energy") has physical attributes, violating non-physicality. Formally: If (X) is abstract, then (¬ ∃ P(X)), where (P) is compositional.
* Physical Immunity: Physical causation operates within abstract frameworks (e.g., laws). The reverse—physical forces altering abstracts—implies circular causation (effects altering their rules), which is incoherent. Formally: Abstracts are causally prior: (∀ E: E → A), not (∃ E: A → E).
* Locational Transcendence: Location presupposes spatial concepts. Locating an abstract (e.g., "where is justice?") leads to a regress: "location" needs a location, akin to Russell’s paradox. Abstracts transcend this framework.
* Perceptual Transcendence: Abstracts are mind-independent; visibility implies physical instantiation, contradicting non-physicality. Formally: If (X) is abstract, then (¬ ∃ V: V(X)), where (V) is visibility.
* Counterexample Handling: A "visible power" claiming abstractness (e.g., a glowing "force of truth") is physical, as visibility requires photons or matter. These criteria ensure conceptual coherence.
Strength: These criteria are derived from the logical essence of abstractions, using modal reasoning, formal definitions, and philosophical principles. They address counterexamples by showing that failures result in contradictions, making the argument practical and robust.

An Argument Against Raw Featism in Powerscaling
Raw featism, the belief that only observable feats matter for determining a character’s power level in fictional works, is a flawed and overly restrictive approach to powerscaling. It dismisses the narrative authority of authors, ignores the role of abstract and infinite powers, and leads to absurd logical consequences when extended to other properties of fictional characters. This argument, structured in three parts, demonstrates why raw featism is untenable.
Part 1: The Impossibility of Visually Depicting Abstract and Infinite Powers
Abstract and infinite powers, by their very nature, cannot be fully captured through visual feats alone. Abstract powers—such as the manipulation of concepts like time, causality, or logic—lack physical form, as they are non-spatial and non-material. Similarly, infinite powers—such as the ability to destroy an infinite universe or wield boundless energy—transcend finite representation. To disprove this, one would need to visually depict an infinite universe or an abstract concept (e.g., "justice" or "infinity") on screen in a way that fully conveys its nature.
* Challenge to Featists: Show an infinite universe visually, capturing its boundless extent, or display an abstract concept like "truth" in a way that undeniably proves its non-physical essence. This is impossible because:
   * Infinity’s Visual Limit: An infinite universe cannot be shown in its entirety, as any visual medium (e.g., a screen) is finite. At best, one can depict a large expanse or use symbolic imagery (e.g., stars stretching endlessly), but this fails to prove infinity—it merely suggests it.
   * Abstracts’ Non-Physicality: Abstract concepts lack physical properties. For example, showing a glowing orb to represent "truth" introduces physical attributes (light, shape), contradicting the non-physical definition of abstracts. Any visual depiction risks conflating the concept with a physical stand-in.
* Necessity of Statements: Only narrative statements can establish abstract or infinite powers. For instance, an author declaring “Character X can rewrite the laws of physics” or “Character Y destroyed an infinite multiverse” conveys what visuals cannot. These statements, grounded in the author’s intent, are the primary evidence for such powers, as feats alone are inherently limited by the medium’s finite nature.
Raw featism’s insistence on visual evidence dismisses the reality that fiction often deals with concepts beyond human perception. Demanding feats for the unshowable is an unreasonable standard that impoverishes the analysis of fictional power.
Part 2: The Author’s Narrative Authority Over Featist Restrictions
Raw featism’s demand that only feats matter implicitly seeks to impose a rule on fiction: nothing infinite or abstract should exist unless visually shown. This restriction lacks legitimacy, as it undermines the author’s sovereign authority over their fictional world. The argument for authorial supremacy is as follows:
* Author as Creator and Sustainer: An author is the sole creator of their fictional universe, defining its rules, characters, and metaphysics. Without the author’s active imagination or recorded work (e.g., text, film), the fiction ceases to exist in any meaningful sense. This makes the author’s declarations the ultimate arbiter of truth within the narrative, subordinate only to logical consistency to avoid incoherence (e.g., a character cannot simultaneously exist and not exist without a coherent explanation).
* Logical Consistency as the Only Constraint: The author’s freedom is boundless provided their declarations avoid logical contradictions. For example, stating “Character X has infinite power” is valid unless contradicted within the narrative (e.g., X fails to perform a finite task without explanation). Raw featism’s demand for visual feats is an arbitrary external rule, not a logical necessity, and thus has no authority over the author’s intent.
* Defenses Against Objections:
   * Objection 1: “Feats ensure objectivity.” Response: Fiction is inherently subjective, rooted in the author’s vision. Feats are one tool among many (e.g., dialogue, narration) to convey power. Prioritizing feats over statements ignores the author’s intent, which may deliberately use non-visual methods to describe abstract or infinite powers (e.g., a character’s power is “beyond comprehension”).
   * Objection 2: “Statements can be exaggerated or unreliable.” Response: Unreliable narration is a valid concern, but it’s addressed by analyzing the narrative context (e.g., is the statement from a trustworthy source like an omniscient narrator?). Feats can also be misleading (e.g., a character’s feat may be downplayed for dramatic effect). Both require interpretation, not a blanket dismissal of statements.
   * Objection 3: “Feats align with real-world evidence standards.” Response: Fiction isn’t bound by real-world epistemology. Unlike science, which demands empirical data, fiction operates on narrative truth. An author’s statement that “Character X transcends time” is true within the story, regardless of visual proof, as fiction doesn’t require real-world verification.
   * Objection 4: “Fans need feats for fair powerscaling debates.” Response: Powerscaling is a fan activity, not a narrative mandate. Fans may prefer feats for simplicity, but this preference doesn’t override the author’s right to define powers through statements. Debates should weigh all evidence—feats, statements, context—rather than arbitrarily excluding non-visual data.
* Authorial Freedom in Practice: Authors routinely use statements to establish abstract or infinite powers. For example, in Marvel Comics, the One-Above-All is described as omnipotent, a claim not fully shown through feats due to its infinite nature but accepted as true based on narrative authority. Raw featism’s attempt to limit this freedom is an overreach, as it seeks to dictate how authors convey their vision.
The author’s role as the creator and sustainer of the fictional world grants them absolute authority, constrained only by logic. Raw featism’s visual-only rule is an unjustified imposition that stifles the richness of fictional storytelling.
Part 3: The Absurd Logical Extension of Raw Featism
If raw featism insists that only shown feats matter for powerscaling, this principle can be extended to any property of a fictional character: anything not explicitly shown in the work doesn’t exist. This leads to absurd and untenable conclusions.
* Extension to Other Properties:
   * Personality: If a character’s kindness is never shown (e.g., no scenes of charity), raw featism implies they aren’t kind, even if the narrative states “X is compassionate.” This erases internal traits unless overtly displayed.
   * Backstory: If a character’s past (e.g., “X trained for 10 years”) is stated but not shown, it doesn’t exist. This nullifies narrative depth, as backstories are often conveyed through dialogue or exposition, not flashbacks.
   * Thoughts and Emotions: If a character’s love for another is never shown (e.g., no romantic gestures), it doesn’t exist, despite statements like “X loves Y.” This flattens characters into mere actions, ignoring their inner lives.
   * Off-Screen Events: If a character’s victory over a foe occurs off-screen but is described, it didn’t happen. This dismisses narrative summaries, a common storytelling device.
* Logical Consequences:
   * Impoverished Characters: Characters become one-dimensional, defined solely by visible actions. A character described as “wise” but never shown giving advice lacks wisdom, reducing them to a caricature.
   * Narrative Collapse: Many stories rely on exposition or implication (e.g., “the war devastated the land”). If only shown events count, entire plot elements vanish, rendering stories incoherent.
   * Inconsistency with Intent: Authors use statements to convey what cannot be shown due to medium constraints (e.g., budget, runtime). Dismissing these statements contradicts the intended narrative, misrepresenting the work.
   * Absurd Reductionism: Taken to its extreme, raw featism implies that only what appears in the “frame” exists. A character who exits a scene ceases to exist until they reappear, as their off-screen actions aren’t shown. This solipsistic view undermines the continuity of fictional worlds.
* Counterexample: In The Lord of the Rings, Gandalf’s power as a Maia is described but rarely shown in full (e.g., his fight with the Balrog is brief). Raw featism would dismiss his cosmic stature because it lacks extensive feats, contradicting Tolkien’s intent. Extending this, Gandalf’s wisdom or courage would vanish if not constantly depicted, flattening a richly layered character.
* Conclusion: Extending raw featism to all properties reveals its absurdity. Fiction thrives on a blend of shown and stated elements. Limiting analysis to feats alone guts the narrative, ignores authorial intent, and produces a shallow, incoherent understanding of characters and worlds.
Conclusion: The Fallacy of Raw Featism
Raw featism fails as a powerscaling methodology because it:
1. Cannot account for abstract or infinite powers, which require statements due to their unshowable nature.
2. Lacks authority to restrict authorial freedom, which is absolute within logical bounds.
3. Leads to absurd consequences when extended, undermining the richness of fictional narratives.
Powerscaling should embrace all narrative evidence—feats, statements, context—to honor the author’s vision and capture the full complexity of fictional characters. Raw featism’s narrow focus is not only philosophically untenable but also detrimental to meaningful analysis, as it strips fiction of its depth and imaginative potential.

Dimensional Tiering and Transfinite Tiering
Dimensional tiering, the idea that higher dimensions are qualitatively superior to lower ones in a way that makes them "infinitely" stronger or more indestructible, has long been debated. However, this argument is fundamentally flawed due to category errors, equivocations on infinity, and a misunderstanding of physics and mathematics. Below, I will break down the core arguments in favor of dimensional tiering and systematically refute them.
The Core Argument for Dimensional Superiority
1. Premise 1 (P1): Higher dimensions are qualitatively different from lower dimensions in that they have more axes of movement.
2. Premise 2 (P2): Even multiplying a lower dimension by an uncountable infinity does not make it a higher dimension.
3. Conclusion (C): Higher dimensions are uncountably infinitely superior to lower dimensions.
Key Issues with This Argument
1. Category Error in the Conclusion
* P1 discusses a geometric property (axes of movement), but the conclusion claims that higher dimensions are physically indestructible and require "infinitely more energy" to destroy.
* There is no necessary link between the number of movement axes and physical indestructibility. Geometry and physics are separate domains of discussion.
2. Misuse of "Uncountably Infinite"
* P2 claims that multiplying lower dimensions by an uncountable infinity does not elevate them to a higher dimension, which is true but irrelevant.
* However, the conclusion asserts that higher dimensions are "uncountably infinitely superior," a vague and unsupported claim. There is no mathematical framework that ranks dimensions in an uncountably infinite hierarchy.
3. Equivocation on "Infinity"
* The argument shifts between different meanings of "infinity":
   * Geometric infinity (axes of movement)
   * Cardinal infinity (counting infinities of universes)
   * Physical infinity (energy required for destruction)
* The failure to distinguish between these concepts results in a misleading and incoherent argument.
Empirical Refutations of Dimensional Tiering Claims
Claim 1: "Lower Dimensions Cannot Affect Higher Dimensions"
Refutation:
* Singularities in Black Holes: Black holes have zero-dimensional singularities that exert massive gravitational influence over 3D space (source).
* Electrons and Quarks: These fundamental zero-dimensional particles influence the structure of our 3D world (source).
* Empirical Evidence: A lower-dimensional being could theoretically generate sufficient force to harm a higher-dimensional being, since energy is not inherently "higher-dimensional"—it follows standard physics laws regardless of dimension.
Claim 2: "Higher Dimensions Govern Lower Dimensions"
Refutation:
* The Holographic Principle: Some theories suggest that our 3D universe is actually a hologram projected from a 2D structure (source).
* This directly contradicts the idea that higher dimensions inherently contain more information than lower ones.
* Lower dimensions can contain all the information necessary to describe higher-dimensional spaces, meaning influence is not necessarily one-directional.
* There are several ways to put the information of higher dimensional structures onto lower dimensional ones (Here is one). But this misses the point. Namely, you can not not have a structure where higher dimensions are dependent on lower ones, but you can not have a structure where lower dimensions are dependent on higher ones. The reason why option 1 is possible while option 2 isn’t is for the same reason why you can’t have an infinite amount of things without having at least one thing.  Before you try to argue against it: ask yourself this question. How many axises of movement does a 4-D space have? 4, by definition? Can 4 exist without 3? Can 3 exist without 2? Can 2 exist without 1? The answer to all of those questions is no, none of those can exist without reference to lower numbers.
Claim 3: "Energy in Higher Dimensions is Qualitatively Different"
Refutation:
* Energy is measured in the same units across all dimensions (source).
* While energy in higher dimensions may be able to move in more directions, this does not make it qualitatively superior—only different in how it is distributed. If 300 joules are taken from 4-D space to 3-D space, it is still 300 joules.
* The assertion that energy is "uncountably infinitely superior" just because it moves in more directions is a non sequitur.
Addressing Counterarguments
Counterargument 1: "Each point in a higher dimension represents multiple lower-dimensional configurations, so lower dimensions cannot affect higher ones."
Refutation:
* This is a logical leap that assumes more configurations translate to invulnerability.
* Just because a higher-dimensional space contains more data does not mean it is impervious to lower-dimensional interactions.
* Example: A 2D shadow can be cast by a 3D object, but changes in the shadow can reflect changes in the 3D world.
* Source:
* https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9905221
* Misner, C. W., Thorne, K. S., & Wheeler, J. A. (1973). Gravitation. W. H. Freeman.

* Counterargument 2: "Higher dimensions can exist without reference to lower ones."
Refutation:
   * The concept of dimensionality is inherently hierarchical.
   * To define a 4D space, we must reference x₁, x₂, x₃, and x₄—removing lower dimensions makes defining higher ones incoherent.
   * Higher dimensions necessarily presuppose the existence of lower ones, making their complete independence impossible.
Counterargument 3: "Higher-dimensional beings need more energy to sustain themselves because the energy can move in more directions, proving their superiority."
Refutation:
   * More axes of movement do not mean energy behaves differently. This is a trait of the higher dimensions, not of the energy itself.
   * A 300-joule object in 4D moving to 3D does not gain or lose energy—it simply has fewer directions to move in.
   * There is no empirical evidence suggesting energy from higher dimensions is inherently superior.
Conclusion
Dimensional tiering arguments rely on category errors, equivocations, and misunderstandings of physics. The belief that higher dimensions are infinitely superior is unfounded both mathematically and empirically. If one wants to argue for strength based on dimensional tiering, they must provide actual physical mechanisms—not just abstract mathematical concepts. The assumption that "higher = better" is a flawed heuristic, not a legitimate scaling method.
1.11 Beyond Dimensions

This section addresses the concept of Old Outerversalism, where a atemporal and aspatial realm is posited to be a tier of its own when destroying it. The problem with this is threefold.

1.112 An Atemporal and Aspatial Realm Cannot Be Changed or Entered.

A ‘3-D’ being can have ‘higher dimensional’ power despite being 3-D themselves, which is to say, they hit with 4-D or more power. Thus, to say a character has 1-A punches is to say that they have 1-A power behind their attacks even if they are dimensioned.

1.13 On Qualitative Superiority Hierarchies

1.131 R>F isn’t how the relationship between reality and fiction works

R>F layers refer to a conceptual framework in which a higher physical reality completely transcends a physical lower reality, perceiving and interacting with it as if it were "fiction." However, this does not mean they regard the lower reality as fiction in the same way we perceive stories or media in real life. Instead, the higher reality's level of control and dominance over the lower one is so absolute that the lower reality appears as subordinate and manipulable as a video game world is to its player.

Due to this, there are some constraints. The lower reality must not be able to access or affect the higher reality in any way. The higher reality must have thew lower reality depend on its existence, not the other way around.
In discussions about the relationship between reality and fiction, a common misconception is the idea that reality is inherently "superior" to fiction in some metaphysical or hierarchical sense. This notion is often reinforced by fictional works that depict "real" entities transcending or overpowering "fictional" ones. However, such portrayals misrepresent the nature of fiction itself and fail to align with real-world ontology.

Fiction, by definition, consists of narratives, characters, and worlds constructed by human minds. These constructs do not possess independent existence, sentience, or agency outside of the frameworks in which they are created. Unlike conscious beings in reality, fictional characters do not have self-awareness, desires, or the capacity to act beyond what is dictated by their creators.
Some fictional works attempt to circumvent this by treating the "fictional world" as a sentient, self-contained system with awareness of its own status. This, however, is still a narrative device—not an actual property of fiction. A fictional character "realizing" they are fictional is merely a scripted event within the fiction, not a demonstration of genuine awareness or autonomy.
When fiction presents the idea of a "real" character transcending a fictional world, it relies on a false premise: that fiction exists as an independent, conscious domain that can be surpassed. In reality, fiction is an abstraction created within reality. There is no separate, sentient world to be transcended. Fiction does not operate by the same rules as reality—it does not "contain" actual beings, only conceptual entities with no independent thought or presence outside of their narratives.
This means that the idea of being "stronger than fiction" is meaningless because there is no sentient force within fiction to compare strength against. Fiction is not a competitor or a parallel reality—it is a product of reality, shaped by human thought, language, and artistic expression.

Rejecting X negates powerscaling itself, presenting a fork: accept the universal standard or abandon the enterprise.
Premises and Logical Development
Premise 1: Powerscaling Requires a Universal Set of Logical Rules (X)
Powerscaling is inherently a comparative enterprise, aiming to establish relative power hierarchies across diverse fictional universes. To achieve this, it relies on three logical pillars:
   * Transitive Relations: If Character A defeats Character B, and B defeats C, then A should defeat C.
   * Hierarchical Classification: Power tiers (e.g., universal, planetary, city-level) assume that higher tiers encompass lower ones.
   * Falsifiability: Evidence hierarchies (feats > statements > implications) allow claims to be tested and contradicted.
These pillars presuppose a universal logical framework (X), typically classical logic, which enforces the law of noncontradiction (a proposition cannot be both true and false). Without X, contradictions (e.g., “A > B and A < B”) disrupt transitivity, tier containment, and falsifiability, rendering comparisons incoherent. For example, if Goku is both stronger and weaker than Superman without resolution, no transitive chain or tier assignment is possible, halting analysis.
Premise 2: Under X, Some Fictions Cannot Be Infinite or Abstract in a Transcendent Sense
Under the universal standard X, all fictional attributes—including claims of infinite or abstract power—must be quantifiable within the framework’s constraints. Classical logic demands that power be measurable through feats or statements that align with transitive and hierarchical principles.
If the character does not fit up to the standards of the claim, the character does not have that property, full stop.
Premise 3: Allowing Fiction-Specific Rules Destroys Powerscaling
If fictions are permitted to define their own rules—such as accepting true contradictions (dialetheism) or unique metaphysical systems (e.g., higher-dimensional superiority)—the universal standard X is abandoned. This leads to several catastrophic outcomes:
   * Loss of Transitivity: If Fiction A allows “Character A > B and A < B” to be true (as in dialetheism), transitive chains (A > B > C) become indeterminate, as contradictory relations cannot be resolved.
   * Collapse of Tiers: If Fiction B claims its characters operate on “infinite layers” unbound by X, tier systems break, as no shared metric exists to compare them to Fiction A’s characters.
   * Unfalsifiability: Fiction-specific rules make claims like “Character X is omnipotent beyond logic” immune to evidence, reducing powerscaling to assertion (e.g., “X wins because I say so”).
Without a universal standard, each fiction becomes a self-contained system, incomparable to others. This destroys powerscaling’s purpose: to establish relative power across narratives.
Conclusion: The Powerscaler's Fork
The Powerscaler's Fork presents a dilemma: either fictions abide by the universal logical standard (X) to be meaningfully powerscaled, or they reject X and forfeit comparative relevance, appearing “weak” in powerscaling terms. Steelman Argument: The Case for Limited Author Authority in Powerscaling
In powerscaling discussions, we need a framework that respects author creativity while maintaining analytical rigor. The strongest version of this position holds that:
Authors have supreme authority over their own fictional universes but this authority operates within certain meta-constraints that enable meaningful cross-fictional analysis. These constraints aren't arbitrary limitations on author freedom but necessary methodological guidelines for comparative analysis.

The key distinctions are:
   * Internal vs. External Authority: Authors have absolute authority over what happens within their fictional universe, but when their work enters the comparative domain of powerscaling, additional analytical frameworks must apply.

   * Descriptive vs. Prescriptive Power: Authors can describe characters of any power level, but they cannot prescribe how these characters compare to entities from other fictional universes they don't control.

   * Logical Consistency Requirements: While fiction can contain internal contradictions (as established by the author), meaningful comparison across fictions requires a common logical framework. The No Limits Fallacy (NLF) is one such framework that prevents unfalsifiable power claims from dominating analysis.

   * Intent vs. Evidence: Author statements provide valuable context but must be weighed against demonstrated feats and internal consistency. This isn't dismissing author intent but recognizing that demonstrated capabilities provide stronger evidence for comparative analysis.

This approach respects author creativity while enabling the systematic analysis that makes powerscaling possible. Without these constraints, powerscaling would devolve into competing claims of author authority with no way to resolve contradictions between them.

Response: Why the Steelman Position Fails
Despite its appeal, the steelman argument for limited author authority in powerscaling contains fatal contradictions:
   * Arbitrary Authority Boundaries: The distinction between "internal universe authority" and "comparative analysis constraints" creates an artificial boundary with no clear justification. If we grant authors the power to establish internal contradictions, by what principle can we deny their ability to make comparative claims?

   * Self-Appointed Meta-Authority: The powerscaling community positions itself as having meta-authority over author statements without establishing the source of this authority. This creates a circular argument: "We can override author claims because our analytical framework requires it, and our analytical framework requires it because otherwise we couldn't override author claims."

   * The Fundamental Paradox: If authors can transcend logic within their works (creating true contradictions), then the entire concept of the No Limits Fallacy becomes incoherent. The NLF is based on logical principles about evidence and burden of proof. If logic itself is optional within fiction, then applying the NLF becomes arbitrary.

   * Inconsistent Application: In practice, this approach inevitably leads to cherry-picking—accepting author statements that align with preferred narratives while rejecting others as "NLFs" without consistent criteria.

   * Unresolvable Disputes: When two authors make contradictory claims about their characters (e.g., "my character can defeat anyone" vs. "my character cannot be defeated"), this framework provides no principled way to resolve the contradiction.

We face an unavoidable choice: either embrace author authority completely (making powerscaling impossible) or establish consistent logical constraints that apply to all fictional works (potentially limiting author authority). The steelman position attempts to avoid this choice by creating artificial boundaries around author authority, but these boundaries collapse under scrutiny.
For powerscaling to function as a coherent analytical practice, it must establish clear philosophical foundations that don't contain these internal contradictions. This likely requires accepting that authors do not have authority to make their characters "omnipotent" or "unbeatable" in a comparative sense, even if they can describe them that way within their own fiction.
A Concise Argument Against Dimensional Tiering
Dimensional tiering, which claims higher-dimensional entities are inherently superior (often infinitely so) to lower-dimensional ones, is flawed. It misuses mathematics to support arbitrary metaphysical assertions and fails logically, as shown below.
1. Metaphysics Means Nothing: Anyone Can Claim Anything
In fictional narratives, metaphysics is arbitrary as long as it is logically coherent. Authors, as the sole creators of their universes, can define any metaphysical framework—e.g., 3D beings defeating 4D ones or dimensions being causally equal—without contradiction. Dimensional tiering’s claim that higher dimensions are uncountably infinitely superior is just one possible metaphysics, with no authority over others. To assert “4D is superior,” I can equally say, “No, it isn’t,” as both are valid if coherent. Fiction prioritizes authorial intent, not fan-imposed hierarchies, rendering tiering’s metaphysics baseless.
2. Misusing Mathematics to Support Metaphysics
Dimensional tiering uses the mathematical premise that “each point in a higher dimension corresponds to multiple configurations in lower-dimensional space” to claim higher-dimensional invulnerability. This is invalid:
   * Category Error: Mathematics describes geometric relationships, not metaphysical power. Higher dimensionality (e.g., 4D vs. 3D) adds coordinates, not inherent superiority. Claiming a 4D entity is “stronger” because it has more axes is like saying a 3D cube is “stronger” than a 2D square—it’s a misuse of a descriptive system for a normative conclusion.
   * Separation of Domains: Mathematical properties don’t dictate metaphysical truths. A 4D point’s relation to 3D configurations is a structural fact, not evidence of causal or power-based supremacy. Metaphysical claims require narrative justification, not mathematical analogies.
3. The Mathematical Claim is Wrong and the Argument Fails
The premise that “each point in a higher dimension equals multiple lower-dimensional configurations” is mathematically valid but misapplied, and the conclusion of invulnerability is logically unsound:
   * Why the Math is Misapplied: In geometry, a 4D point can represent a continuum of 3D configurations (e.g., fixing a fourth coordinate). However, this doesn’t mean a 4D entity is “infinitely greater” or immune to 3D influence. For example, a 2D plane can intersect and affect a 3D sphere (e.g., cutting it), showing lower-dimensional entities can influence higher ones. Similarly, in physics, 3D particles interact with higher-dimensional systems (e.g., string theory’s 10D spaces). The math describes embeddings, not invulnerability.
   * Incorrect Infinity Claim: Tiering often invokes “uncountable infinity” to assert 4D superiority. This is wrong: the cardinality of points in 3D and 4D spaces is the same (continuum, (\aleph_1)), per Cantor’s set theory. Higher dimensions don’t have “more” points in a way that implies power; they differ in structure, not quantity.
   * Logical Failure: The conclusion—“higher dimensions are invulnerable”—doesn’t follow. Interaction implies mutual causality: if a 4D entity affects a 3D one, the 3D one can affect its 4D projection, depending on narrative rules. Invulnerability requires causal isolation, contradicting interaction. Narrative examples (e.g., 3D Superman challenging 5D Mr. Mxyzptlk in DC Comics) show lower-dimensional influence, disproving universal invulnerability.
Conclusion
Dimensional tiering fails because:
   1. Its metaphysical claim of higher-dimensional superiority is arbitrary, as authors can define any coherent metaphysics.
   2. It misuses mathematics, conflating geometric descriptions with power-based conclusions.
   3. The mathematical premise, while valid, doesn’t support invulnerability, and the “infinity” claim is incorrect, with interaction contradicting the conclusion.
Powerscaling must respect logic over arbitrary frameworks. Dimensional tiering’s reliance on flawed mathematics and baseless metaphysics makes it an invalid standard for fictional analysis.

There would be no conflict. You cannot overcome infinity. You cannot decrease infinity. You cannot increase infinity. You cannot gradually wear down infinity.

An infinitely fast character would solve all problems instantly. The story would begin and end in a single panel.

Let’s say a supervillain was abstract.

There would be no conflict. You cannot touch an abstract thing. You cannot see it. You cannot physically interact with it.

Let’s say there was a realm beyond space and time. You cannot enter the realm, you cannot change the realm. No story can be told here.

DC/Marvel wants to tell stories about combat between good and evil and exploration. THe philosophies and religions they borrow from don’t care about entertainment. They care about rigorous consistency.
