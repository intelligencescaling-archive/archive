THE STRUCTURE OF DANTE’S HELL
WRITTEN BY LLA

In the new video series that I have just started, I am using the structural model of hell created by the italian poet Dante Alighieri  in his masterpiece, the Divine Comedy. Hell obviously occupies the first of the three parts of the poem (Inferno; the other two are centered on the other two otherworldly worlds, namely Purgatory and Paradise), Dante crosses it in twenty-four hours guided by his literary and ideal mentor, Virgil (a Latin poet of the era of Augustus, symbol of the human reason within the poem), during the year 1300, when Dante was thirty-five. In this short document I simply intend to explain the various circles into which Dante's Inferno is divided in order to make it more understandable why some characters fall into it and others do not, and in such a way that anyone who wants to replicate the format of the video can even without having read the poem directly (which I would still recommend).

Before continuing, I think it is fundamental to say what Hell is for Dante: it is an enormous funnel dug in the center of the Earth (yes, in the Middle Ages it was known that the Earth was round) by Lucifer's fall from heaven after his defeat against the archangel Michael. Here are destined all the sinful souls of the world, and here they will reside for eternity, without any hope that their punishment will cease. Hell is guarded by many devils and demons, servants of Lucifer, and is the furthest place from God in the universe.
Having said this we can start by mentioning the fact that the various circles are divided into two macro parts of hell: the upper hell, which contains the anti-hell and the first five infernal circles, and the nether hell, or rather the one in which they are punished the worst sinners, guilty of the sins of heresy, violence, fraud and betrayal. The descent into hell in fact involves a gradual worsening of the sins of the damned depending on how much their actions have offended God: the least malignant sinners are those who simply chose neither good nor evil, while betrayal is considered the worst of possible sins. The worsening of sins leads the lost souls to get closer and closer to the prince of hell and the traitor par excellence,  Lucifer, who is placed at the bottom of the last zone of the last circle.

It must also be underlined that although the damned are guilty of horrible actions, it is not necessarily the gravity of the sin that drags them into eternal pain (even if it also counts by a certain point): the element that conditions the future of a man's soul is in fact his earthly conduct and his possible repentance. No matter how terrible the sins committed were, if a man sincerely repents before dying, then God will accept his request for forgiveness and will send his soul to Purgatory, where it will obviously take a long time to redeem himself, but in the end he will be able to reach Heaven. The damned, on the other hand, are too late to repent, their end was decided the moment they took up evil and became its instruments without being able to separate themselves from it.

UPPER HELL (vestibule of hell-VI circle)

VESTIBULE OF HELL (ANTI-HELL): the vestibule of Hell is the first place you reach after crossing the infernal gate and ends at the banks of the Acheron river. There is no demon who guards it and the souls present are called "pusillanimous", those who in life were unable to decide between good and evil, always remaining neutral. Among them stand out the angels who did not take part in the celestial war between God and Lucifer and, perhaps, a pope, Celestine V, who had decided to abdicate the papal throne a few months after his election, effectively leaving the Church in the hands of his corrupt successor, Boniface VIII, enemy of Dante. The pusillanimous are not real sinners, but they still suffer a punishment: they have to run after a constantly moving flag while hornets sting them and the ground beneath them is covered in worms. The punishments used by Dante are never random, but follow the rule of retaliation, so that the eternal punishment and the sin committed are closely related. Obviously, the pusillanimous chase a flag in vain because in life they never knew which side they were on.

FIRST CIRCLE (LIMBO): Limbo is the first of the 9 circles of hell and is the only one where there is no punishment for the damned, who in fact have not committed any sin in life. In the circle reside those who were virtuous in life but who were not baptized and therefore did not know the authentic God, in addition to newborns who died before receiving the sacrament. The circle is divided into two areas, one simply barren, the other more graceful, consisting of a large castle surrounded by green meadows and a stream. Here are the great spirits, the most virtuous among the men of the past. Virgil, Dante's guide, also belongs to this circle, since he died before the birth of Christ. There are many souls mentioned by Dante in this circle, including pagans (Caesar, Aeneas, Homer, Cicero, the great Greek philosophers such as Aristotle and Plato, Saladin, and many others), and although none of them suffer, they are still melancholy and they sigh, making the air tremble due to the absence of God to which they are condemned.

Ayanokoji and Johan were not baptized (we don't have exact information for both but it is very unlikely given that Koji is Japanese and Johan was born from an experiment wanted by the army in the communist Czech Republic).

SECOND CIRCLE (LUST): after the Limbo there is King Minos, ancient ruler of Crete (step-father of the minotaur), who has become an infernal judge and who confirms the sentences decided by divine justice, rounding his tail around the souls as many times as the number of the circle in which they will suffer.
Once past Minos, we enter the circle of the lustful, the first of the circles dedicated to the incontinent. Dante in fact divides the sins into three: the sins of incontinence (linked to the 7 capital sins, which do not follow reason), the sins of violence (linked to the bestial nature of man), and the sins of fraud (those committed with reason, i.e. the worst of all since reason is the most unique gift that God has given to man).
The lustful have subjected rationality to sexual instincts, becoming victims of them. These sinners, just as in life they allowed themselves to be carried away by passion, are now dragged by a storm that never stops, which slams them from one side of the circle to the other. Dante among them identifies (and dialogue) with a specific group of lustful people, that is, those who died violently for love. The most famous characters present here are Francesca da Rimini (the woman Dante speaks to) and her lover Paolo, Cleopatra, Helen, Paris and Achilles.

THIRD CIRCLE (GLUTTONY): going down a circle we reach the place of damnation of the gluttons, who in life have indulged excessively in the pleasures of the table. Just as in life they behaved and satisfied themselves like beasts, now they are forced to remain supine in the mud like pigs, while a rain of snow/hail hits them. The rain, however, is the least of the punishments since their guardian, the hell dog Cerberus (symbol of gluttony), continually flays them. The only character that Dante distinguishes in the circle is Ciacco, a fellow citizen of Florence.

FOURTH CIRCLE (GREED): Dante allocates two complementary sins in the fourth circle: the avaricious (excessively thirsty for wealth and power) and the prodigals (who have squandered their fortunes superficially). Both are forced to push huge boulders with their chests, making them roll halfway around the circle until they collide with the opposite group of sinners, with whom they begin to insult each other, and then start the effort again. The retaliation is not immediately clear, yet it is present: trying to accumulate and squander wealth are tiring actions, but devoid of spiritual usefulness, consequently now such sinners are forced to continually carry out a painful and exhausting effort for eternity. Dante does not recognize any famous sinners among them, although Virgil says that the circle is full of ecclesiastics. The guardian here is Pluto, brother of Zeus (he had been called "the rich" by Cicero, which makes him perfect to be the demon of the miserly and the prodigal), who however does not participate in the punishment of the damned.

FIFTH CIRCLE (WRATH, THE STYNX): The fifth circle is covered by the second infernal river, the Styx, which here forms an extremely muddy swamp. There are immersed the wrathful ones, the worst of the incontinent sinners mentioned directly by Dante, they beat each other and also injure themselves, the luckiest ones near the surface, the others in the dirty waters. On the Styx the demon Phlegias ferries (while on the Acheron it is Charon the ferryman) since he has the task of leading the damned to the infernal city of Dis, home to the next circle. Among the wrathful souls Dante meets one of the most detested fellow citizens of Florence: Filippo Argenti. Some critics have formulated the hypothesis that all the other incontinent people not mentioned by Dante (slothful, envious and proud) are immersed in the Styx, but this seems unlikely given that the system of infernal circles does not have as its basis that of the seven deadly sins (at contrary to the terraces of Purgatory), and it is however possible that Dante considers the remaining deadly sins as derivations of wrath.

NETHER HELL (VI circle-IX circle)

SIXTH CIRCLE (HERESY, THE CITY OF DIS): In the city of Dis, defended by enormous walls and legions of devils, heretics and heresiarchs (i.e. the leaders of heretical and some philosophical sects) are punished. Among them Virgilia directly mentions only the Epicureans, guilty of having believed in the mere materialism of the world and in the mortality of the soul, but we are sure that there are many other heretics in the city. Once beyond the walls (hardly since the devils don't let anyone through, and Virgil had to wait for Heaven to send an angel to convince them to open the gates) the entire city is actually a huge cemetery of continually burning tombs. The retaliation is more cruel than ever and even double: the heretics in Dante's time were punished by burning at the stake, but above all the Epicureans and other philosophers who did not believe in the immortality of the soul are now forced to be dead among the dead, being the only sinners to be buried. Dante in Dite meets Farinata degli Uberti (the greatest florentine ghibelline nobleman), who tells him that together with him many other souls are burning such as that of Emperor Frederick II, and Cavalcante dei Cavalcanti (father of the poet's former best friend and very famous poet himself, Guido dei Cavalcanti).

SEVENTH CIRCLE (VIOLENCE): The last three circles of hell are divided into various areas since the sins committed by these souls are so serious that they require different punishments. The seventh is the circle of the violents, those who have followed the bestial side of man, sometimes even combining it with reason, to carry out evil. They are divided into three rings:
-I ring, violents against others: Dante places here tyrants, robbers, murderers and other violent commoners. They are immersed in the Acheron, a river of boiling blood, and are guarded by centaurs, who strike them every time they try to get out of the boiling blood more than has been decided by divine justice. It is important to say that only those who have used violence deliberately or worse for personal pleasure reside here, therefore not all those who have used it in life. Dante here identifies the Hunnic king Attila, Pyrrhus king of Epirus and perhaps even Alexander the Great.

-II ring, violents against self: this is one of the saddest places in the whole hell, since those who have used violence against their own bodies, killing themselves, are punished here. They were transformed into dry trees without leaves or fruit, horribly changing their shape but not their soul, which still regrets the extreme choice made. In this dead forest there are also squanderers (a more extreme version of the prodigals), who are chased by a group of infernal dogs who tear them apart, and while they try to escape into the forest they destroy the branches of the suicides, practically amputating them. To make the already tragic situation even worse there are the harpies, mythological monsters who continue to hurt the poor soul-trees and perch on them. Suicides are the only sinners who will not receive their earthly bodies back at the time of the Last Judgment, rather they will be hung from the branches of their trees. TWO WORDS ABOUT DANTE’S LIFE
Dante Alighieri was born in Florence in 1265 to a wealthy urban family. Having become an orphan (of his mother when he was a child, of his father when he was a teenager, before his marriage with Gemma Donati) he inherited the large family fortune which allowed him to dedicate himself to his studies, literature (poetry in particular, dedicated to his beloved Beatrice) and above all to political activity. He was in fact one of the main political exponents of the White Guelph party, dominant in Florence at the time, and obtained several prestigious positions such as the priory of the municipality. In 1302, however, the rival party of the White Guelphs, the Black Guelphs, organized a coup d'état thanks to the support of Pope Boniface VIII and exiled all the members of the enemy party from the city, including obviously Dante. From that moment on, for the next nineteen years Dante wandered through the courts of central and northern Italy, working as a diplomat, advisor or simply being hosted by the noblemen. It was while he was in exile that he composed his greatest masterpiece, the Divine Comedy, which guaranteed him a place in the Olympus of writers and poets. He died in 1321 in Ravenna while still in exile.

FOR THE CURIOUS ONES
For those who already had in mind to start reading the Divine Comedy or for those who were intrigued by my videos or by these few lines (I would be immensely proud and flattered) there may be a desire to delve deeper into the subject matter. Immediately the first response to satisfy this idea would be to read the Divine Comedy itself and in this case I would like to make you slow down for a moment and give you some humble advice. First we must state the obvious: the Divine Comedy is a decidedly mammoth text, especially for someone not used to poetry (because yes, the poem as such is written in poetry verses, even though it is narrative and therefore more flowing), and it has a unique complexity. Its size and this complexity of themes, concepts and references make it impossible to read the Comedy without an excellent commentary by an author or an authoritative critic. Also because by reading it "on your own" you would understand, and with difficulty, only a tiny part of the work, perhaps even leading you to consider it overrated. A good comment is therefore essential, but if you decide to follow a similar path it means that your interest is really great (and commendable), but I don't want those who have lit only a small torch of curiosity, but who do not wish to undertake such a reading challenging, turn it off and move on. There are many other ways to approach Dante's masterpiece that do not involve a direct reading (and in my opinion they should not be subject to criticism or underestimation), from summary videos to simplified editions.
Whatever way you come into contact with it, Dante's poetry is a turning point in the life of a reader open to his teachings and his depth, whether Christian or not, because although the literal protagonist of the Divine Comedy is Dante Alighieri, as he himself wrote in a letter (epistle XIII) the symbolic and therefore most important protagonist of the book is the entire human race, in its journey towards its own ascension and self-comprehension.
