Mark Hoffman

Introduction: Mark Hoffman was a respected detective within the SAW verse whose sister was slain by her ex-boyfriend. Heavily disillusioned, Mark created a Jigsaw-style trap to avenge his sister and frame the murder on Jigsaw, though this caught the killer’s attention. John Kramer captured him and blackmailed him with the evidence of Mark’s murder into working with him while also appealing to his dissatisfaction with the justice system. Through a combination of blackmail and genuine ideological realignment, Hoffman worked alongside Kramer for years, but he was unsatisfied merely being the muscle behind Jigsaw and instead wanted to run the games himself.

Note: It’s shown Jigsaw had predicted his entire plan(s) years prior and set up contingencies to test/kill him if need be, though that doesn’t denigrate Marks’s strategic thinking from his end. He simply had the misfortune of facing a very brilliant opponent.

Pendulum Trap

But before ever meeting Kramer, Hoffman had impressed John with his revenge plot against Seth Baxter in 2004. He had been heavily depressed and an alcoholic, but even so he’d managed to, motivated by his burning desire for vengeance and disillusionment with the justice system, learn and almost replicate Jigsaw’s quality in engineering with the pendulum trap, which warrants further analysis.

Seth Baxter wakes up in a dimly lit room chained to a table. A TV switches on and Jigsaw informs him that he has escaped justice for too long, let off with too light of a sentence. A pendulum hangs above him, and it will descend over 60 seconds, cutting him in half when it is finished. The only way to prevent it, the tape says, would be to reach his arms out as far as he could, press a button within a mechanism, and let his hands be crushed to dust. After some hesitation, he does so, but the traps stop for only a moment before bisecting him.

Now the obvious aspect is that the trap involves mechanical (which of course requires heavy usage of mathematics) and electrical engineering. Hoffman had designed a power descending pendulum, and that requires balancing the blade’s weight while minimizing vibration, which could throw off its precise downward path. The structural integrity has to be right to handle the force of the swinging pendulum, which itself must also be carefully chosen to cleanly slice through Seth without losing momentum. Knowledge of electrical engineering would be required in order to rig the pendulum, which is seemingly powered by a motor, to descend at a pace synchronized with the clock and the end of the tape’s message. To be a dick, Hoffman seemingly rigged the machine to even offer respite, a moment of false hope, to Seth after he pressed the buttons, before it continued its downward path. And to top it off, neither the police nor the FBI, who’d studied Jigsaw for years, ever caught onto the trap that killed Seth Baxter being one designed by a copycat. Hoffman had mastered electrical and mechanical engineering to (nearly) replicate the quality of John Kramer himself, sufficient to fool law enforcement. Furthermore, John never rigs his traps, and the police within SAW know this; they even deduce that some traps were built by Amanda based on them being unwinnable. Yet Mark had managed to conceal the machine being rigged against the subject. The only flaw that differentiated the trap from John’s was Mark’s choice of metal within the pendulum; John would have used tempered steel, which he chides Mark over. This mastery is very impressive since Hoffman had achieved it over a short period of time (around a month) and was depressed and heavily alcoholic.

Next, the trap is carefully built to fit Jigsaw’s creative and symbolic MO. Hoffman clearly did his research, designing the trap based on Edgar Allen Poe’s The Pit and the Pendulum, where the narrator finds himself a victim of the Spanish Inquisition and is at the mercy of their torture devices. A pendulum hangs and descends from a ceiling panel with Father Time painted on it, representing the inevitability of death at the hands of time; and the pit itself represents hell, or self damnation within certain Christian theologies. So, Hoffman’s message with the trap, fabricating it as Jigsaw’s lesson, is this: “You have damned yourself through your destruction. And the only way to escape is to be reborn through sacrifice.” This actually aligns well with Jigsaw’s view of “immortality,” as the trap communicates that Seth, like Kramer, can become “immortal” by undergoing a willing sacrifice. Given the tight time frame Seth is subject to, the test aligns extremely well with the inspiration.

More obviously, the tape that informs Seth of his situation delivers information with a voice nearly indistinguishable from John Kramer. (Meta note: Tobin Bell actually provided most of the voice for the tape. It is really him.) This is impressive since John, as shown in a flashback involving his wife, actually distorts his own voice to mask it and as a calling card of sorts. Hoffman was able to record his own voice, modify it to sound almost exactly like John Kramer, further modify it to sound like “Jigsaw,” and he even threw in the signature warbles present in John’s tapes. This shows a mastery of audio engineering.

And finally, we see that Hoffman accurately replicated Jigsaw’s speech patterns, replicating the formula of Kramer’s actual tapes - which typically chide the listener(s) for their sins, explains why they’re in the trap, what they have to do to get out, and makes use of double entendres all with John’s articulate, philosophical vernacular. Again, the police never pick up on this, and even John doesn’t chide him for anything but the metal quality/revenge motive.

The planning process for this likely involved discreetly ordering the components of the traps, or building them himself; acquiring the Billy puppet, or building one himself; acquiring the tapes from the police department; creating the tape; and stalking and subduing Seth in a secure place.

Killing Kramer
Goals: Hoffman’s holistic goal is simply to continue as Jigsaw and be free of the leverage Kramer has on him. As a corollary, this requires killing Jigsaw’s other protege Amanda Young, since she was loyal to John and was being trained to carry on his work.

(Note: In italics is theory, but it serves to make sense of Amanda’s 180 turn around from SAW II and X from being loyal to Kramer and brainwashed to being disillusioned.)

Hoffman began his scheme by targeting Amanda. He manipulated her out of her brainwashing, convincing her subtly that Jigsaw’s philosophy was wrong, that the subjects they captured simply needed to be put down. Now if he just outright began arguing against the “will to live” tests, then Amanda would warn John, so Mark had to be sneaky about it. And it worked. Amanda began rigging the traps John had her build. Mark knew that John would be smart enough to catch on and that, per his philosophy, would be inclined to test her again in a situation where she could either “live or die.” Thus, he would have an opportunity to rig the game so that she dies. To do this, he investigated Amanda off-screen and discovered that she was connected to Cecil, the man responsible for killing John’s unborn son.

The actual test involving Amanda bears fruition in SAW III/IV. It’s a mere part of an incredibly complex chain of events with dozens of players that include the FBI, the police force, the several victims involved, Amanda, Dr. Lynn, John, and himself. Lynn was forced via an explosive collar around her neck to operate on John’s brain tumor, and Amanda was being tested in her temperament in keeping Lynn alive and refraining from killing her when John showed affection to Lynn. Thus, the fail condition would be Amanda killing her. Not only that, Amanda’s test’s fail condition was also a secret, one that John only reveals as she’s dying from the punishment that John had orchestrated should she fail, that is, Lynn’s husband Jeff would arrive at the exact moment Amanda shoots Lynn and kill Amanda in a rage. To put it simply, Hoffman had deduced not only that the final game was a secret test of character for Amanda, but also that she would die if she failed. Mark ensured that she failed by blackmailing her with the information he acquired. In another document, I went over just how impressive John’s prediction of the events surrounding his death was: if Jeff was a little faster, Amanda wouldn’t have shot Lynn since Lynn would reunite with the armed Jeff; if Jeff was slower, he would arrive later and possibly die in a shootout with Amanda after she regains her bearings after killing Lynn; if the judge Jeff saved happened to not be standing in front of the shotgun when Jeff was trying to save another subject, he might be able to stop Jeff from killing John; if Jill was a bit quicker to release details about John, Strahm would’ve discovered the facility sooner (canonically, he arrives at the scene moments after Jeff killed John) and prevented the execution of John and likely Amanda; and so on. John was able to predict all of this. But Mark’s feat is even more impressive than John’s in this case, because John is consistently parsimonious with the information he reveals to his disciples. As mentioned, he didn’t reveal to Amanda that she was being tested until after she had failed, he didn’t reveal to Hoffman that Gordon was a fellow disciple, etc, so Hoffman had made the same prediction with less information than John, not only showing his exceptional reasoning ability but also his ability to “anticipate the human mind” as John had taught him. Given the precision, he also would’ve had to simulate  the events and players of the game in his mind, juggling all the details involved such as Jeff’s temperament, Strahm’s reasoning skills, Rigg’s progress through his own set of games, etc, visualizing the events as they played throughout Gideon’s meatpacking facility, Riggs’ path as he worked through his trials through town, and the police station, an area comprising perhaps miles. More generally speaking, very temporally precise predictions require one to be able to make logical inferences by taking into account as much relevant information as possible, which is especially difficult since predicting the behavior of human beings is an “intractable problem,” a problem for which there exists no algorithm or computer capable of solving given the unpredictability of human behavior as we are influenced by innumerable variables - our past experiences, immediate sensory stimuli, priming from recent experiences, and so on.

  How much information did he have? The films are loose with their flashbacks, but we’re consistently shown that Hoffman helped build traps, gather info on victims, kidnap victims, and leave false clues for the law as part of Kramer’s plans. It’s likely he was able to make the prediction based just off this:

        1. The layout of the game area
2. The traps involved
        3. The victims and their background
        4. The FBI’s involvement (it’s likely Hoffman was the one instructed
        to lure them to the final game via a key
        5. John telling him that Amanda would be tested

Events proceed, and Amanda dies after shooting Lynn, Jeff avenges Lynn by killing Amanda, Jeff kills John, who tells him that Jeff’s daughter is a part of the game, and Strahm kills Jeff after the latter threatens him while enraged. Hoffman proceeds with John’s plan. Strahm gets locked in the room where John and Amanda’s bodies are, but Strahm proceeds to a secret door, where John had left a tape instructing him not to proceed. In position, Hoffman subdues Strahm when the latter disobeys and situates him in a trap meant to be his execution - his head trapped in a glass box that slowly fills with water. But Strahm survives through an improvised tracheostomy using his pen, and that brings us to SAW V.

Analysis: It goes without saying Hoffman’s strategy is effective. Through his reasoning skills, manipulation, and simple blackmail, he’d killed two of his biggest opponents. But we can see it’s also well shielded. You could argue that the plan falls apart if Amanda doesn’t kill Lynn, but Hoffman had reasoned to a certainty that she would. (1) She doesn’t know the consequences of doing so. (2) The test John has for her directly preys on her feelings, testing her loyalty to him. Even without the blackmail, John’s affectionate displays towards Lynn clearly enrage Amanda. (3) This is further compounded by Hoffman having already broken her agreement with Jigsaw’s philosophy beforehand. (4) Hoffman had blackmailed her with information about her past - which is that Amanda was involved with the death of John’s son - so that she would kill Lynn.

Thus, based on his understanding of her character and situation within the game Hoffman knew Amanda would certainly shoot Lynn.

But we can imagine some other scenarios. Strahm’s timing in arrival doesn’t really confound Hoffman in any sense. If he arrives sooner and prevents Jeff from killing Amanda or Amanda from killing Lynn, Hoffman could’ve simply activated the lockdown system built into the room to trap the subjects, which he canonically did as part of Strahm’s test, and gunned down the survivors (as he proves to be a very effective marksman later on) from the secret door. Since Strahm had arrived there alone without warning anyone, Hoffman would be free to reveal the game to the police at his discretion. Depending on Amanda’s mental state, he could even count on her to cause a gunfight with Strahm in her highly shattered mental state. He could even leave the survivors to starve since Kramer rigged the area so that Strahm couldn’t get a proper phone signal (shown in the beginning of SAW V).

Strahm Framing

Goal: To frame Strahm as the second apprentice. But this is not the full strategy; it’s only half of his strategy to deal with the FBI, the other half of which is seen in SAW VI.

And that brings us to SAW V. Most likely, Hoffman hadn’t planned on Strahm surviving. It’s shown during his scene that Hoffman had taken care to remove his gun and phone, and Hoffman is visibly shocked when Strahm is discovered to be alive right after asserting that “nobody made it.” This forces him to adapt his strategy from just becoming Jigsaw to also getting rid of the FBI, Strahm in particular. This is because Agent Strahm had proven exceptionally clever prior to the events of SAW V. He was the one to catch on that Jigsaw had a second apprentice besides Amanda, and his survival of the water box only further proved that. Hoffman knew that Strahm would put the pieces together between his second apprentice theory, the last thing he saw before being subdued being a large man in a pig mask who subdued him, and Hoffman being the only other survivor. With further research into his troubled background and research into Seth Baxter, Strahm and the FBI would close in on him. A letter he receives in his office marked “I know who you are” leads him to believe Strahm has already made the connection, forcing him to come up with a plan. He goes to the evidence room and grabs Strahm’s phone. This marks the first step of his plan, which will become crucial later on. He visits Strahm after being told that Strahm’s partner Perez had died (this was actually a scheme by Erickson). Strahm makes it very clear that he thinks the whole department is crooked, but Hoffman manipulates him by making himself look suspicious, telling him to “back the fuck off,” raising his suspicion. Erickson arrives shortly, Strahm asks for another talk with Jill Tuck, and Erickson informs Strahm that he’s off the case due to his recklessness in arriving at the game without telling anyone else. At the same time as all this, Hoffman has put forth into motion another set of subjects John had wanted him to test - the Fatal Five.

Now Hoffman already had a good grasp on Strahm’s situation and personality. Per his prediction of Strahm shooting Jeff, he knew that (1) Strahm would be forced to kill an innocent, and (2) he would arrive alone. Strahm didn’t really save anyone in the process, so it would be natural for Erickson to put him on leave due to his fruitless and reckless actions. This also indicates that Hoffman had profiled Strahm well in the short time he worked alongside him. He knew Strahm was passionate in his career to a reckless extent and very intelligent. Furthermore, he knew that the “death” of his partner Perez only made it more personal for Strahm, which both gave Strahm motive to continue his hunt for Jigsaw would also further give Erickson good reason to keep Strahm from the case. Thus, Hoffman reasoned that Erickson would put Strahm on leave, and Strahm would certainly continue his investigation into Jigsaw and the second apprentice theory no matter what.

Hoffman makes a call to the FBI asking if he could speak to Strahm about something urgent, sounding somewhat nervous. (He already knows Strahm is off the case.) Meanwhile, Strahm covertly sneaks back into his old office to look for information on Hoffman, stealing files since he can’t find anything on their internet database because Hoffman has no criminal records. He discovers the death of Hoffman’s sister and the death of Seth at the hands of “Jigsaw.” Erickson catches Strahm on the way out, and Strahm lies that he was just grabbing some stuff before his time off, leaving Erickson befuddled.

With the files and photographs, Strahm revisits the site of Seth Baxter’s death. He notes how a small hole in a wall gave “Jigsaw” a perfect view of Seth’s face as he died. This confirms that Hoffman is an apprentice for him, as the hole indicates that the killer wanted to personally see Seth’s face as he died.

Hoffman receives another “I know who you are” letter. Strahm continues sneaking back into his office, and Erickson spots him. This leads Erickson to call Hoffman back. Hoffman explains that Strahm wasn’t in his office, that Strahm had theorized about a second accomplice, and that he suspected the accomplice was someone within law enforcement. Due to Hoffman’s manipulation, Strahm had been manipulated into acting heavily suspicious in the eyes of Erickson, and Erickson even discovers that all files on previous Jigsaw victims have been taken by him.

Later, Jill informs Erickson that she thinks she’s being followed. She thinks it’s Strahm, who meanwhile visits the site of Jigsaw’s death and figures out Hoffman’s plan was for them all (John, Amanda, Jeff, Lynn, Riggs, him, etc) to die, and Hoffman was supposed to come out as the hero and sole survivor. Meanwhile, Hoffman uses Strahm’s phone to call Erickson before immediately hanging up. Erickson gets freaked out and has Strahm’s phone tracked, but Hoffman had taken the precaution to make sure Strahm’s couldn’t be tracked yet. He only allows it to be tracked after returning to the control room of the Fatal Five’s game. Hoffman returns to his office at the police station, where Strahm had been waiting outside in his car, allowing the latter to tail him to the renovated Nerve Gas House. Erickson acquires the phone signal from his team just then and heads to its location. There, he finds Strahm’s phone, pictures of the Fatal Five, and a folder with pictures of himself. Thus, Strahm is now the #1 culprit for Jigsaw’s successor.

Meanwhile, Hoffman has lured Strahm to a room with a large glass coffin filled with ice, and a tape instructs Strahm to enter the box - a test of whether Strahm has learned to trust him. Strahm ambushes Hoffman after he emerges from a hiding spot with a syringe and throws him into the coffin, triggering the trap as the walls close in on Strahm. He tries desperately to escape the trap, shouts “I know who you are!” but is ultimately crushed to death, leaving only a hand.

Analysis

First, let’s go over the plan:
1. Set up the Fatal Five’s games
2. Steal Strahm’s phone
3. Make a call to Strahm’s office pretending to be nervous
4. Inform Erickson about Strahm’s “second apprentice” theory
5. Have Jill tell Erickson that she thinks Strahm is stalking her
6. Call Erickson from Strahm’s phone
7. Leave Strahm’s phone at the Fatal Five’s game
8. Lure Strahm to the glass coffin trap
9. Kill/Manipulate him

With this, Hoffman outsmarts not one but two FBI agents, predicting every move both of them make He predicted Strahm would get fired by Erickson for being reckless and too emotionally invested in the case, he predicted Strahm would continue investigating on his own, he predicted that Strahm would come off as suspicious when he returned to the station to get the files to do this, he predicted that Erickson would be spooked enough by then to track Strahm’s phone after he received a call from it, and he did all this to such a precise degree that Strahm’s death and Erickson’s discovery of the Fatal Five happened simultaneously, showing that he even grasped Strahm’s psyche so thoroughly that he knew exactly how fast he would catch onto Hoffman.

From Erickson’s POV, all evidence does indeed point to Agent Strahm:

* The apprentice would have to be someone physically strong, so that excludes Amanda and John
* The apprentice would have to be someone smart. Strahm proved to be intelligent several times.
* Strahm had previously been notably aggressive with Jill during his interrogation of her in SAW IV, he asked if they could reopen the investigation into her, and, as far as he knew, it’s entirely possible that John Kramer wanted revenge on his ex-wife after she left him. So Hoffman manages to connect the dots in Erickson’s mind by having Jill report Strahm.
* The apprentice would have to be someone with a law enforcement background since many of victims were chosen based off information in the police database
* Strahm was clearly deceptive in his first encounter with Erickson at the station
* And the photos, phone, and Fatal Five game just seals the deal

From a strategic point of view, this plan is brilliant since Hoffman structures it in such a way that it’s a lose-lose situation no matter what Strahm does. If Strahm chooses to not investigate, Hoffman simply carries on as Jigsaw or waits for the heat to die down. If Strahm does investigate, then he incriminates himself as described above. By the time of the finale, Strahm has lost no matter what he does. If he escapes the trap, he’s still successfully framed by Hoffman’s actions. If Strahm escapes the trap after throwing Hoffman in there, then it’d be hard to explain it in any way that doesn’t make it look like Hoffman had been forced into another trial by Strahm. The worst case scenario for Hoffman is that he dies in the struggle with Strahm, but that it’s only a loss for him, not a win for Strahm, since it would be heavily suspicious for Strahm at that point to kill another officer; Hoffman had further made this clear by framing it such that it looks like (from Erickson’s POV) that Hoffman had been looking into Strahm and his theory, so if Strahm killed him it would look like Hoffman simply got too close to the truth. And if Strahm entered the coffin, Hoffman would have easily incapacitated him. We see in the next film that the coffin is moved mechanically to a room just down the hall, Hoffman would’ve hidden and ambushed the disorientated Strahm. Judging by the syringe, it’s also inferrable that Hoffman planned to perhaps recruit him as an apprentice, hence his tape asking whether Strahm trusted him, cut his hand off for the plan in the next film, or perhaps both.

Another strategically brilliant aspect is that it aligns well with John’s prior plan in SAW IV, where Strahm was lured to the game site and forced to shoot Jeff. Hoffman took advantage of that and engineered a situation based on that where Strahm was inherently forced to chase him alone, due to how big of a mistake he made from a bureaucratic POV, thus isolating the smartest person bent on catching him. John’s plan had also been to plant the seed in the minds of investigators that he was training a new apprentice (Riggs) within law enforcement. While Riggs did die later on, Strahm formed a theory about there being a second apprentice from the get go based on Kerry’s trap design. Hoffman knew that this would inevitably be vindicated later on, so he managed to turn this theory against Strahm by implicating him as the new apprentice.

Though Hoffman did have reason to believe events would play out as they did with Strahm not entering the coffin. (1)  Hoffman had seen how Strahm had previously flagrantly disobeyed Jigsaw’s instructions. (2) Strahm in general was a hotheaded person, disobeying his superior’s order to drop the case. (3) Strahm notably has a glass motif throughout his appearances. A transition to his introductory scene involves glass shattering,his first trap involves a glass box, and the coffin is made of glass with broken glass shards thrown in for good measure. Furthermore, via metacognition he can know that his interactions with Strahm up to that point had been inherently deceptive, and Strahm from his point of view had made progress entirely by NOT trusting Hoffman. For example, Hoffman tried to debunk Strahm’s second apprentice theory by bringing up how the heavier victims could’ve been set up by Amanda, John, or  machines, but Strahm was vindicated when he directly encountered the second apprentice after seeing John and Amanda dead in the secret tunnel at the beginning of SAW V. It’s likely Strahm would be further inclined not to enter the claustrophobic glass coffin based off his traumatic experience with the glass box.

What about the letters? It is revealed in SAW VII that Gordon had sent those letters. The entire Hoffman vs Strahm plot had been foreseen by John, and Gordon was instructed to send those letters to set Hoffman against Strahm. Strahm saying “I know who you are!” confirms this, as it shows John’s trademark ability to predict people’s behaviors. He had Gordon taunt Hoffman with Strahm’s dying words before Hoffman ever put the pieces in play.

Analysis

His plan in VI is primarily to build on his work in V and make it look like Strahm was framing him, i.e. Hoffman framed Strahm to make it look like Strahm was Jigsaw and intent on framing Hoffman, a very complex scheme. How he does this revolves around victims of the cold opening. As mentioned, he cut a piece from them using the same knife he used on Seth Baxter several years prior, and he didn’t leave the tape behind to break Jigsaw’s pattern of leaving tapes behind. They’d notice the knife, be reminded of Seth Baxter, and catch onto the missing tape. This would incriminate Hoffman, but with the evidence against Strahm, it would look like Strahm was incriminating Hoffman.

(Though the tape used in the cold open also very accurately replicates John’s voice, ensuring the survivor wouldn’t be able to distinguish it from John’s real tapes if the police showed one to them.)

His plan A was to simply fool them with this set up. If this worked, he had a number of ways to play it: he could continue working as an investigator alongside them, using his inside knowledge of the case to mislead them; he could disappear, letting the police think Strahm-Saw had kidnapped/killed him (this would be especially strong since Jigsaw had “kidnapped” Hoffman before in IV), since Mark’s plan had already established Strahm-Saw has reason to go after him; he could test them one by one; or he could retire.

His plan B was in case the FBI did crack the forensic holes in the case, and it’s what we see play out on film. He slaughters the FBI and concludes his framing of Strahm by burning all evidence, destroying the decrypted Baxter tape, and leaves prints behind that would be much harder to see through due to fire damage. We know he had actually anticipated this since he had the hand, a portable freezer for it, and gasoline; a huge risk not worth taking unless he specifically anticipated having to kill the FBI and destroy the site of the crime. We even see in flashbacks that he has a larger cooler unit in his base where he put the hand back after setting up his earlier crimes, so he had to have grabbed it out of there after receiving the call from Ericson. And finally, we see that he had gloves in his car which he takes off after destroying the lab.

While the riskier and less desirable outcome, plan B still leaves him with options. He could disappear as previously mentioned. With the FBI and their work destroyed, Hoffman’s framing of Strahm would be solidified. He was their last public prime suspect, with an APB out on him to boot, existing evidence implicates him, and the police would have to start from scratch. He could disappear in a similar manner explained above, retire, or continue Jigsaw’s work in the same city.

A brief note. While Easton’s game is largely disconnected from the events of Hoffman’s plotline unlike in SAW V, it does have the effect of dividing police attention. Rather than the police and FBI synergizing on the cold opening case, they’re forced to divide manpower among the missing members of Easton’s insurance company, the missing journalist Patty Jenkins, and the cold opening game, thus shielding his strategy. Another layer of shielding to consider is that by letting the FBI catch on to the existence of an accomplice within law enforcement, they’d inherently be set against the local police force, limiting the information they can share with them and even amongst themselves (hence them faking Perez’s death). This is actually fairly similar to Light Yagami’s own strategy against L and the police, and it likewise has the effect of heavily isolating his most dangerous living opponents - the FBI.

It’s a common misinterpretation that Hoffman was sort of just bumbling his way through this film and the FBI cornered him, with Hoffman only escaping due to his combat skills. How do we know Hoffman isn’t in fact bumbling his way through this?

1. Hoffman would have to go out of his way to re-use the exact same knife he used years ago. Hoffman had worked alongside John for years, conducted several tests for him after he became wheelchair-bound, likely making the cuts with the knife John did use, so he would know what knife to use.

2. The cold open victims were the first trials solely set up by him, not on John’s orders. The timing isn't a coincidence. It happens soon after Strahm’s death as part of his plan. If Hoffman had simply begun going after his own victims, he would’ve done so after the events of IV, where there was a time gap for him to pick new victims.

3. The missing tape is deliberate. Hoffman knew his encryption skills could fool forensics, hence him not taking the tape when he killed Seth, but drawing attention to it breaks the pattern established up to that point and leads the FBI to fastidiously review the cases they know, acting as a contingency in case the different knife went unnoticed.

4. When Ericson is pressuring Hoffman during the sound lab scene, he actually plays along with Hoffman’s plan, explaining how it is possible that Strahm was trying to frame Hoffman. Hoffman relaxes at this and sets down his cup of coffee, thinking he might not have to resort to his plan B since plan A had them fooled, before Ericson explains the forensics evidence that disproves that, causing Hoffman to rearm himself with the coffee.

5. As explained above, the hand, freezer, gasoline, and gloves he brought to the FBI’s lab.

One might be fooled by Hoffman’s acting. It’s easy to take it at face value as him genuinely getting flustered when the FBI increasingly close in on him, but the SAW films are very layered, and they establish well that he plays very well into the local bumpkin detective act while in fact being a mastermind second only to Kramer. We see this in IV, where Hoffman acts like a fool when Strahm deduces the second apprentice’s existence, IQmogging the local detectives; V, where he makes a seemingly overly suspicious move in telling Strahm to fuck off, and also when he makes the call to Ericson; and VI, where he acts dumbfounded at the idea that an autopsist could notice a difference in the blades used to make a cut. Hoffman had merely played into their pride as big hotshot federal agents, which was shown in IV with Strahm and further in VI where they had the audacity to fake Perez’s death, and made them underestimate him as a brute muscle detective/second-rank serial killer.

This scheme achieves several things:
1. Gets rid of Agent Strahm

3. Frames Agent Strahm in a double layer scheme that makes it seem like he’s after Hoffman

His plan was so good that it was only Jill’s betrayal that stopped him, as she trapped him and ran to the police to confess everything, sending Hoffman into hiding in the next film.

His plan, which he impressively seems to have thought up while in excruciating pain from his torn cheek and mangled hand, centered around controlling the movements of Gibson and Jill, isolating the latter, dividing police attention as much as possible, and concealing his intentions and whereabouts.

First, he begins by targeting the skinheads. This serves to show that Jigsaw’s work isn’t finished. Gibson

Thus ends one aspect of his strategy in VII, which fulfills his old promise to get revenge on Gibson.

Jill

His manipulation of Jill’s movements is much simpler. She had already had reservations about staying at the safehouse, believing that Hoffman knew its location, and Hoffman proved that seconds later with the CD sent there. To fortify the place, they even completely lock the place down, which turns out to play very well into Mark’s favor.

This might seem odd, but with the tape seemingly negotiating for a deal and by merely sending a CD there instead of directly attacking the safehouse, Hoffman had planted the idea in Gibson’s head that he wouldn’t go for any head-on assault. This makes Gibson think he’s playing defense and trying to exchange hostages for Jill when he’s really on the offense.

The Police

It’s subtle, but this is the strongest aspect of Hoffman’s strategy. By dividing the police’s attention between three different targets Jill was left with only a few officers guarding her. Again, they didn’t think he would risk a direct assault, so they thought it was safe to send more officers out to deal with the junkyard threat or Bobby’s game.

Gibson orders the bomb squad to rush down there in case there are more bombs. (3) It serves to conceal Hoffman’s secret location inside the junkyard. Gibson ordered that officers retreat and play it safe, lest they set off another explosion or a trap. Jigsaw had used boobytraps against the police on several occasions, so this was a natural reaction. And (4) when Gibson finally bites the bullet in a hail of gunfire, this would naturally keep police fixed on the site even if Gibson managed to give out a panicked order for every officer to go back to the station, an order which likely wouldn’t have worked anyways since it’d be difficult to explain the plan he had deduced and there were seemingly more pertinent problems at the junkyard and hospital.

Second, Bobby Dagen. Firstly, Bobby was kidnapped in broad nightlight, footage of which was captured on camera (as Hoffman wanted). Even prior to Gibson’s discovery, that would already mean a few officers were focused on finding the famous author. Once they discover the hospital, that draws several heavily armed police out, away from Jill to deal with the more immediate threat. Ultimately, it’s no surprise Hoffman had only a few officers to deal with at the station.

Feat Framework

While Hoffman did lose, it’s very important to note that Hoffman was not in an easy situation at all. This is most clear when we consider the External Challenges and the Quality of Opponents Hoffman had to deal with.

        One external challenge Hoffman faces is that he daylights as a detective. He’s surrounded by people trained to spot liars, colleagues who are hunting him down, and yet Hoffman maintains his cool 24/7 despite  his highly stressful situation. Another obvious one is the logistics involved in setting up the traps. Kramer had designed every trap, but Hoffman still had to discretely assemble them in semi-public locations. Before any complex schemes can begin, Hoffman has to be extremely careful to not get caught by a mundane mistake like someone stumbling upon the location of one of his games or leaving DNA behind at one of the traps. As Strahm states, “Jigsaw doesn’t make mistakes!” and, logistics-wise, Hoffman managed to maintain Kramer’s flawless record.

        Hoffman had an extremely high quality of opponents. To start with, even the FBI and police he outmaneuvers are, unlike in many other films and shows, very competent, and his schemes largely rely on them being able to pick up on the clues he allows them to gain. We can see this in his SAW V plan, where he trusts Erickson to follow the false breadcrumbs that lead him to conclude Strahm is the new Jigsaw. Agent Strahm in particular is exceptionally clever. As mentioned, he was the first to pick up on there being another disciple besides Amanda, he improvised his way out of the box trap designed to execute him, he was able to discover the site of John’s game in SAW III/IV, and he was able to deduce Hoffman’s history with Kramer largely through intuition. But the big one is, of course, John Kramer, the main antagonist of SAW who foresaw and planned out most of the events of SAW I-VII, a time frame encompassing years, with an uncanny ability to pick up on and model the behavior of the people he has in his games. While John beat him, Hoffman has the honor of being one of the few people to boast a genuine victory over Kramer, as he managed to kill John’s wife, Jill Tuck, despite the numerous challenges and contingencies John had thrown him: Agent Strahm and the FBI, Jill Tuck and the police, and only finally losing to Dr. Gordon, John’s seemingly final posthumous ace up the sleeve who completely blindsided him.

Hoffman’s Crystallized Intellect:

Mechanical Engineering - Obviously. He was already proficient prior to working with John, and he should have only gotten better.
Electrical Engineering - ditto.
Audio Engineering - His fake Jigsaw voice was well encrypted enough to fool the police and FBI the first time, and it seriously impressed the FBI technician later on.
Forensic Science - Hoffman was a detective prior to working with Jigsaw. His precise foresight in his VI plan would also likely require him to know the flaws in his usage of the deceased Strahm’s hand.
Chemistry -  Many SAW traps utilize acids and toxins. By the time of SAW II, John was wheelchair-bound, so it was likely up to Hoffman and Amanda to synthesize the poisons he had invented and set up traps that utilize acids. We see that Hoffman also quickly scrambled together bombs in VI.
Toxicology - ditto.
Pharmacology - ditto.
Botany and Animal Biology - Likely. We see John studying botany in Rebirth, and there’s diagrams around his deathbed of humans and animals. He likely discovered the toxins he utilizes through his knowledge in these fields, and for Hoffman to help out he likely had to pick up a few things.
Explosives Engineering - ditto. SAW V also makes use of a bomb trap that destroys a room.
Medical Skills - Many traps require precise biomechanical knowledge and skills. The Steam Maze trap in VI revealed that Easton had had a key implanted in him well enough that he hadn’t noticed prior to it being revealed to him. In VII, we see in his hideout diagrams of the human body, which he likely used in that film’s circle of silence trap, which involved a key implanted inside a woman’s stomach attached to a hook.
Hacking - Hoffman had hacked into the police station’s cameras in VII
Firearms - Obvious since he’s a trained cop and very proficient killer. He also utilized a machine gun in a trap.
Psychology - Whether intuitive or learned, he’s very proficient in this based off his foresight. Though it is likely he has some formal training based on his career as a detective and having a Bachelor of Arts degree.
