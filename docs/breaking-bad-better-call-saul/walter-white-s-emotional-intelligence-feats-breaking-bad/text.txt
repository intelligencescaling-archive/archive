Walter White's emotional intelligence feats

I've been meaning to make a post on Walter for a while, and in the end I decided to make it on this specific category, which while not his best by a long shot, he's still extremely good in and better than a lot of people give him credit for.

The feats in this post will be summarized down to the essentials, if you want to read a more detailed breakdown for any one of them, check out my full doc: [https://docs.google.com/document/d/1tWb3sqRBlub1UxVV8PfoJZmMuq872dKI2n-v-g-lYkk/edit?usp=sharing](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1tWb3sqRBlub1UxVV8PfoJZmMuq872dKI2n-v-g-lYkk/edit?usp=sharing)

This post will go over Walter's best feats in every commonly used EI subcategory.

(This turned out longer than I thought it would)

EMOTIONAL PERCEPTION

Reading Gus Fring (S2E11): This is arguably Walter's best feat in EP. Earlier in the episode he went to a Los Pollos Hermanos restaurant, where Saul told him he would be approached by a potential drug distributor for a meeting. This did not happen, but Walter decided to make another attempt by showing up at the restaurant again. After a few hours, Walter catches the restaurant owner, Gus Fring, looking at him by watching him through a reflection. While there were obviously other clues Walter must've considered when identifying Gus as the distributor, it was this one glance alone that made decide to confront Gus, and he's shown to be quite certain of his conclusion despite very little actual evidence (none at all, I'd argue).
So to break down this feat:
\- Walter had to discern a specific, extremely subtle emotion from Gus's glance. Keep in mind that Gus simply showing an interest means absolutely nothing since Walter was the last customer, waiting there for hours for no apparent reason, so simply looking at him wouldn't be suspicious at all. What we're talking about is likely some kind of consideration, not a strong emotion at all, which makes it difficult to read, especially since it lasted such a short amount of time.
\- Walter had to perceive the emotions not directly, but their reflection on a glossy surface (you can see from the scene itself that Walter's view isn't completely clear) and at a certain distance.
Following this, Gus keeps up his act, trying to conceal the truth, but Walter keeps seeing through him. Given how little proof Walter had here, had Gus's act convinced him, he would've likely left. The fact that he got more certain of his conclusion the more the conversation continued, shows that Walter was able to read his body language and discern his actual thoughts from the act. This is also very impressive because Gus was able to fool the likes of the police and the DEA for years without a single suspicion, and similarly with the Cartel, who would be a lot less trusting.

Gretchen & Elliot Judgment (S5E16): This one comes late, but it's also quite good. Walter sets up a bluff to coerce Gretchen and Elliot into giving his money to his family. The official script for the episode reports that Walter is able to determine that they believe him and will comply by "studying them closely". Moreover, he determines that the "lesson taught" is "never to be forgotten", showing that his determining the long term of these emotions as well (giving this an EU edge as well, in my opinion). Now, it is technically true that we never saw this prediction come true, but I can safely say that it's narratively supposed to be accurate, so I'd count this.

Miscellaneous (minor feats, still worth mentioning):
\- (S2E7) Walter is being handed the drug money from members of his gang (Jesse's friends) and seeing them insist that they're loyal, he perceives their fear and realizes they believe Jesse to have killed a man during a recent occasion.
\- (S3E13) Walter can tell Gale is trying to perfect his formula so that Gus may replace him.
\- (S4E1) Walter can tell from Jesse's and Victor's expressions that Jesse managed to successfully kill Gale, despite thinking he had failed immediately before seeing their faces.
\- (S4E4) Walter realizes that Hank is still puzzled by the W.W. inscription on Gale's notebook and needs to be given an explanation to stop him from continuing to think about it, despite the thing being sort of treated as a joke.
\- (S4E8) Jesse is giving signs of trying to avoid going through with the plan to kill Gus, and Walter picks up on this, making him suspicious and leading him to investigating further.
\- (S4E9) Walter notices signs of someone having cooked inside Gus's lab. From a subtle smirk by Tyrus, who dislikes him, Walter infers that Jesse has officially aligned with Gus, meaning Walter lost his leverage.
\- (S4E12) During the Lily of the Valley strategy (more on this later), Walter's tell to go from trying to convince Jesse that it was Gus to telling him to shoot him is Jesse's expression, from which Walter can tell he's starting to believe the fake narrative.
\- (S5E8) Walter is able to profile Jack as prideful in order to manipulate him, likely based on his Neo-Nazi history and how the carried himself with his crew during their brief interaction.

EMOTIONAL UNDERSTANDING

Note - I excluded a lot of feats here since there are just so many. Most of said excluded feats is better than the average miscellaneous feat in the EP section above.

Lily of the Valley (S4E12): Walter's biggest gamble is based on an extremely accurate understanding of Jesse's mind and very impressive predictive depth. He intends to poison the son of Jesse's girlfriend to get him to eventually blame Gus for it and help him kill him.
Here's the full extent of Walter's prediction:
Andrea, upon seeing Brock ill, calls Jesse for support (this is someone Walter doesn't know personally, yet he's still able to predict her actions) > Jesse connects the sudden, unexplained illness to the ricin he's been hiding in a cigarette > Jesse then immediately blames Walter, thinking he's trying to punish him for changing sides (the last two points show granular understanding of precise thought processes, not just emotional states) > Jesse, now furious, decides to go to Walter's house to get justice personally > when Walter leaves a gun on his sofa, Jesse is compelled to take it to kill him > Walter's story is then able to plant doubt in Jesse's mind > when presented with the choice to either believe Walter or kill him, Jesse finally believes him > Jesse then immediately turns all his rage against Gus and wants to kill him > Jesse accepts to do this with Walter, now also feeling guilty for what happened.
All of these points are indispensable for the strategy to work and dependent on each other, yet are completely believable in the context of the show because this is how Jesse would think and act in a situation like this, and Walter understands it with incredible precision. Something to add is also that Jesse is a very volatile individual, which makes predicting his behavior even harder. That being said, Walter bases his predictions on actual data:
\- Jesse's observed care for kids and loved ones.
\- Jesse's strong sense of justice and emotional reaction to things he perceives as immoral, which have caused him in the past to take rash decisions, commit violence (or attempt to), and put himself in danger.
\- The current fractured relationship between Jesse and Walter that would filter his thought process.
\- Jesse's underlying care for Walter as a father figure and his reluctance to kill when it comes to it.
\- Probably more.

Extrapolating Walter's and Gus's past (S4E13): Walter is told by Saul that Jesse once saw Gus visiting Hector, that "it was like he was torturing the guy", and that apparently Hector once killed someone close to Gus. From this alone, Walter is able to extrapolate the basis for his plan to kill Gus. This is the information he works with:
\- "Someone close to Gus" is notable as Gus is typically very reserved, cold, emotionally distanced and detached.
\- Gus going out of his way to break his facade in order to torture Hector is also notable due to his typical caution.
\- Gus revels in Hector's suffering in a very personal way, which is the only way to explain his behavior. The fact that he's keeping him alive in order to do this when he could easily kill him any time he wants is also telling.
\- Gus caused the deaths of several Salamancas, once with his strategy to let them attack Hank, another time in Mexico when the Cartel was slaughtered.
\-> Starting from this, Walter can tell Gus wouldn't let any of his underlings kill Hector, should it come to it, and would instead want to do it personally.
\- The opposite is also true, Hector, in his current crippled state, powerless and connectionless, tortured by his worst enemy, with a revenge-oriented mind, would accept any help to finally kill Gus, even from Walter, whom he also hates, and would be willing to die for this.
\- The point above is something Gus must've thought about too, though clearly he was relying on Hector's Cartel honor keeping him quiet, which makes the narrative of snitching as a last desperate move believable.
\-> So Walter puts Gus in a situation where, no matter what, Hector should die, knowing that he'd show up personally to revel one last time and shame Hector for snitching, and relieve a hatred that's been brewing for years. If Gus is to think that Hector already dealt enough damage to destroy his operation, that would only make his desire to kill him personally strong so as not to allow him to effectively "win".

Manipulating Jesse into breaking up with his girlfriend (S5E3): This is one I've never seen mentioned but probably some of the most insidious emotional manipulation I've ever seen. Walter brings up the notion that secrets create barriers between people and asks Jesse if he's going to tell Andrea the entire truth. He does this while talking about the relationship in positive terms and reinforcing that Jesse has earned the right to make his decision in order to come off as a supportive father figure. What he's actually doing is manipulating him into breaking up with her in order to make him more emotionally dependent on him and making his drug life his only real satisfactory avenue in life.
What this prediction entails:
Walter knows Jesse won't be able to tell Andrea the truth because he doesn't want her to see him as a monster. But now that Walter has pointed out the contrast, Jesse won't help to think about it any time he's with Andrea, leaving him filled with more and more negative thoughts and emotions which will damage the happiness he feels with her. Moreover, he will start weighing all the bad things he brings to them the more negatively he thinks. These thoughts could range from thinking he's not worthy of them, to thinking he's putting them in danger, to being simply uncomfortable with the lies.

Calling Skyler's bluff (S3E3): Skyler has shown as much disdain towards Walter as possible after finding out about his being a drug manufacturer and has kicked him out of her house. Despite this, Walter predicts that if he breaks in (which isn't illegal since they aren't divorced), she still won't be able to tell the truth to the police due to not wanting to emotionally scar he son by letting him find out, so Walter exploits this in a scheme to make his way back into his house.

Miscellaneous:
\- (S1E3) Walter knows how Skyler's emotional state due to Walter's lies could risk jeopardizing his drug operation, so he tells her about his cancer, knowing that'll occupy her mind.
\- (S2E7) Walter understands how a metaphor could help Jesse get through some trauma and motivate him towards a goal (more on this in the EF section).
\- (S2E13) Walter predicts how Jesse's relationship with Jane will lead him into a destructive path and out of Walter's control.
\- (S3E6) Walter exploits Hank's love for Marie to lure him away from almost discovering him in the RV with Jesse, knowing she's the only thing he would care more about than the case.
\- (S3E12) Walter immediately anticipates that Jesse would go trying to personally gun down some dealers when he sees on the news that a kid he knew was killed by them.
\- (S5E5) Walter manages to get Hank to leave him alone in his office by feigning an emotional breakdown in front of him. This, due to Hank's nature, makes him feel uncomfortable, and thus leads to him finding an excuse to leave while also shutting the blinds to "protect" Walter's masculinity by the stares of others. It's a very precise prediction for what it is.
\- (S5E13) Walter lures a trap for Jesse by making Andrea call him and mention that Walter reached out to her, expecting Jesse to think he was going to hurt her and to thus show up at her house. This would've likely worked had Jesse's cellphone not been in Hank's possession.
\- (S5E14) Walter destroys what's left of Jesse's will by revealing he watched Jane die. It's hard to explain how impactful this is, but on top of reopening the old wound of Jane's death, it also reframes the entire relationship Walter and Jesse had in his mind, making Jesse feel manipulated to an insane extent. For example, when Jesse killed Gale, despite the immense trauma, he could rationalize it by telling himself he did it for Walter, but after what Walter said he won't be able to see it as a positive anymore.
\- (S5E16) Walter is able to predict much of Lydia's behavior, from her routine-oriented actions, to her plotting to kill Walter if given the chance.
\- (S5E16) Walter predicts that Jack, due to his pride, would stall killing him in order to show what state Jesse is in should Walter suggest that he team him with him, considering Jack sees Jesse as a rat.

EMOTIONAL FACILITATION

\- (S2E7) Jesse had just witnessed a brutal murder after being sent by Walter to threaten some junkies who stole their meth. He saw a wife crashing his husband's head by letting an ATM machine fall on it, and both the sound and sight of this left a great impact on him. For this reason, he was unwilling to continue working with Walter and spent his entire day smoking weed in his apartment. Even opening the door took him several minutes, and it's likely he wouldn't even have done it if not to keep Walter from bothering Jane who was dealing with him. Later, after Walter realizes that everyone in the city thinks Jesse was the murderer and sees this as a positive, he goes to tell Jesse, who's initially less than happy about this. Nothing seems to convince him to take part in Walter's plan to expand their territory, until Walter gets the idea to use the metaphor of the blowfish. This gets across the image of a harmless fish who can be someone "no one messes with" through an illusion, by simply puffing up. This likely plays on Jesse's imaginative mind on top of getting across Walter's meaning better than his previous attempts. The metaphor, together with Walter's enthusiastic delivery, are enough to not only get Jesse out of his unresponsive and traumatized state, but to highly motivate him towards the plan for a period that lasts until Combo's death, even filling him with high levels of confidence. The contrast here is very high which makes this an impressive feat in my eyes.
\- (S2E8) Similarly, Hank also recently went through some trauma, in his case even worse. First of all, he committed what's implied to be his first murder when killing Tuco, which already had its effect. More notably, after being sent to El Paso, he watched a severed head placed on a tortoise explode, killing one of his colleagues and crippling or injuring several others. On a deeper level, these two events forced Hank to grapple with a sense of vulnerability that he's not used to and struggles to accept. Due to this, Hank called in sick, not going to work and spending his days in his bed. Not even his wife was able to get him to talk. However, Walter is able to get him to go to work with a single conversation by talking about his experience after discovering his cancer and how that made him lose his fear to act, motivating him to get out in the real world and "kicking the bastard in the teeth". Hank doesn't get over his trauma, but he's able to go back to work and function normally there thanks to Walter's speech.
\- (S2E2) Walter is able to manage Tuco's notoriously violent and volatile behavior for some time.
\- Miscellaneous: see above the EU explanations, some of which contain a strong EF element (for example, Lily of the Valley facilitating Jesse's hatred against Gus).

EMOTIONAL MANAGEMENT

This is the one Walter is seen as bad in, and while he has his occasional antifeats here, I don't think it's enough to discount what he actually has.

\- (S1E1) Grappling with the discovery of his cancer and concealing his emotions from his loved ones.
\- (S1E1) Keeping a level head while being held at gunpoint for the first time, even being able to come up with a strategy to escape despite the stress.
\- (S1E6) Maintaining composure and even a threatening demeanor during the whole confrontation with Tuco, who has just hospitalized Jesse by beating him up.
\- (S2E11) Choosing to miss the birth of his baby for the sake of his mission.
\- (S3E12) Voluntarily align against Gus, killing his dealers, in order to save Jesse.
\- (S3E13) Keeping a level head while being about to be executed by Mike, enough to come up with a fairly complex improvised strategy.
\- (S5E14) Maintaining a threatening and ruthless act during the phone call to save Skyler from being implicated in his crimes by the police despite actually being in tears. During the phone call, among other things, he's admitting guilt for the death of Hank, despite actually being willing to give away 80 million dollars to avoid just that.
\- (S5E15) Going through months of complete isolation and physical illness while maintaining his self intact and somewhat motivated.
\- (S5E15) Getting past a feeling of complete failure, having just been told by his own son that he should die, and having lost everything to get on his final mission.
\- (S5E16) Maintaining complete composure in the face of death, while also having an M60 bullet in his side. These things are very large and strong enough to shoot through light walls.

Actually, there's a lot more. The bottom line is that Walter is very good at managing stress and fear, less so with other emotions.

EMOTIONAL REALIZATION

Again, people assume Walter only got good at this during the Mr. Lambert phase, but this isn't entirely true.

\- (S2E3) Walter spews all of his emotional baggage on a therapist to create an excuse for having faked his fugue state, among which is the fact that he watched all his friends and colleagues surpass him "in every way imaginable" and that he ended up as an extremely overqualified high school teacher.
\- (S2E8) Walter's speech to Hank shows him recognizing how he feels his fear was holding him back and how he got past it following the diagnosis, which obviously also relates to his drug operation.
\- (S4E8) Walter gives a similar speech to another patient who found out about having cancer. This one is about control, but it also relates to how he feels about his Heisenberg persona.
\- (S3E5) Walter is able to recognize that Gus is using his feeling of pride towards his meth recipe to manipulate him. He doesn't admit to it, but that could only be to try and retain an advantage by telling Gus that he's mistaken.
This is to show that Walter was already very much aware of how his drug life fulfilled him personally, he had simply deluded himself into thinking his family was still his priority and that he could balance the two things. His feeling for his family absolutely exist, so the distinction here is even thinner.

\- (S5E16) This makes it even more impressive how he's able to finally make said distinction and accept the truth that he did it for himself. The script calls this "some real self-knowledge".

EMOTIONAL ENGAGEMENT

I put this last despite this being arguably his best subcat because the ER section outlines more or less what emotions he channeled and the EM section what he went through for them, but to summarized, Walter channeled his feelings of unfulfillment and the love for his family to get through his negative emotions from the cancer, which would've left many others depressed and unmotivated, embark in an extremely dangerous life of crime, face off the biggest local kingpin, almost die several times, go through a lot of emotional turmoil, and more. This is basically what the entire show is.

\- (S5E16) More impressively, after going through all that, he straight up fails, he gets his brother-in-law killed, he destroys his family, he loses everything he built and achieved, he loses the little reputation he has, spends months in complete isolation, grappling with his feelings of guilt and failure, fails once again his last attempt to give any money to his family, also being told to die by his own son, and after all of this, simply hearing Gretchen and Elliot deny his contribution to the company they created with his research, is able to stir up enough pettiness to get through all of his feelings of depression and build up immense dedication to his final mission to tie up his loose ends (giving the money to his family and killing Jack and his gang). This is probably his single best feat.
