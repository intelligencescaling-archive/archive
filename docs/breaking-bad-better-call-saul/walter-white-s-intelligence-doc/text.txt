WALTER WHITE

Introduction

Walter White was an unassuming chemistry teacher when he was diagnosed with cancer. In order to save money for his family, he began a life of crime, in which he got more and more involved, eventually becoming one of the most infamous and dangerous drug kingpins in the world: Heisenberg.

In this doc, I’ll go over his feats from the show Breaking Bad, looking at the thought processes and machinations he employed throughout.

Note that this doc aims to scale the character as high as possible, which means I’ll sometimes be interpreting the source material, but keep in mind that the validity of any feat can always be contended in a debate referring to the doc. I don’t consider docs to be the be-all end-all of debating so cases like these are not me trying to wank the character but simply me getting the most juice out of what I’m given. I’ll make sure to be clear through my wording when I’m talking about fully confirmed segments and when I’m basing myself on implications or interpretations.

Keep in mind that I don’t think every subcat needs a thorough explanation, especially fluid/crystallized intelligence and certain thinking subcats (for example I don’t think that there’s a need to mention that Walter had to come up with an idea every time I write down divergent thinking), but I will give an explanation every time the use of one of these categories is notable.

Throughout the doc, I highlighted a few of Walter’s plans. My criterion was to do this only with plans that had a clear execution process and that were at least somewhat major. Strategies are trickier as they usually involve multiple episodes and get intertwined with other feats, so I didn’t do the same for those.

IMPORTANT NOTE: I left a summary in the end which you can find in the tabs. This is because the doc exceeds 260k characters and I have a feeling not many people will be willing to go through it all. The summary includes short descriptions of Walter’s most notable feats for each episode, so that if you are interested in reading the details on said feat, you can go back to the mentioned episode’s analysis and read the related section. If possible, I would still recommend reading the whole doc in chronological order as otherwise you might miss some important context or some feats that might not be notable enough to put in the summary but still deserve some attention.

Season 1 Feats

S1E1 —

Walter finds out that he has lung cancer. Under-paid and under-achieving, he decides to take up the offer that his brother-in-law, Hank, who is a DEA agent, gave him for a ride-along during a raid on a meth lab.
This is the start of Walter’s Meth-Cooking Strategy, which he will be developing during the course of the series. The final goal is to leave money to his family. The idea came to him, after seeing an interview with Hank on TV, that the drug world can be very profitable even for simple criminals, so considering Walter’s expertise in chemistry, he should be able to earn quite a lot. Walter’s goal for the ride-along is to get to look around the lab to familiarize himself with the equipment, as well as probably decide whether he’s actually going through with the strategy or not. To think of this strategy, Walter had to consider the unusual idea of diving into the world of crime (he’s an honest and straightforward person at this point), then assess his goals in relation to the information he has by connecting his knowledge of chemistry and what he learned on TV. During the ride-along, he had to hide his intentions not only from Hank but also from Steve Gomez, another DEA agent who, unlike Hank, shouldn’t have much judgment bias when it comes to Walter. To note, every feat Walter achieves early on in his meth-cooking career is partly achieved by engaging with his negative emotions from the cancer diagnosis, with the main goal of making money for his family. In this terrible situation that would cause most people to go depressed or otherwise feel powerless or unmotivated, Walter gives himself a goal and goes through with it. The idea to enter the crime world shows how much he’s willing to risk for his family (and deep down, for the desire to achieve something in life) and flies in the face of the idea that everyone in his life has for him. * Fulminated Mercury Plan

In an attempt to sell more meth, Walter tells Jesse to find a distributor. Jesse manages to meet with Tuco Salamanca, but it backfires, and he ends up being beaten up and hospitalized. As soon as Walter finds out about this, he asks Jesse’s friend, Skinny Pete, who knows Tuco and was supposed to vouch for Jesse, to tell him everything he knows about Tuco. Keep in mind, he’s looking at Jesse, hospitalized and unconscious, having just dealt with a dangerous criminal, and he immediately starts planning to confront the man who did that, showing no fear.
Walter’s goal now is to establish himself as someone to respect and worth doing business with and selling his meth to Tuco for a high price.
Here's what Walter knows or what he's likely to know:
* His meth, the bag that Tuco stole from Jesse, has been selling very well.
* Everyone gets searched before a meeting with Tuco.
* Tuco accepts meth when he's given it without a second thought. This point is also further strengthened if Tuco figures out that Walter's visit is related to what happened to Jesse due to point one. This doesn't happen, but still.
Now, based on this, Walter connects his expertise in chemistry to find a solution. Knowing he can't get any weapon through, he intends to hide his weapon inside the "meth", which is the only thing he has any shot at getting inside the room with Tuco. Walter makes himself a bag of fulminated mercury, a material that explodes with violent movement. It’s implied that Walter modified it (“a little tweak of chemistry”), probably to look more like meth or simply to be more powerful. Due to the aforementioned things, Walter predicts that Tuco will either let him in with the meth or have the meth taken to him and then consent to let Walter in (the latter happened with Jesse). Tuco is very confrontational, so it’s unlikely he’d have Walter sent away or killed without meeting him first. Walter’s sudden appearance and demands would also fuel Tuco’s curiosity.
Walter shows up at Tuco's place and demands that a thug let him in to talk to Tuco. The thug silently glares at Walter in the eye, perhaps in an attempt at intimidation, but when Walter doesn't show weakness, he's let in. This is something he keeps throughout the meeting with Tuco, not showing fear despite knowing that the individual is very violent and erratic. That's because Walter knows that he can't show weakness in this criminal world if he wants a shot at respect. On top of that, there's his pride that fuels this desire to show dominance.
After Tuco's men search Walter, who's acting like he just wants to sell, they hand Walter's bag to Tuco, thinking it's meth, as planned. Walter comes in and states his demands, more dollars than Jesse asked for, for the meth Tuco stole, as well as extra money for Jesse’s recovery. Walter is obviously being met with mockery by Tuco. Walter then grabs one crystal of fulminated mercury and throws it across the room, causing an explosion. Walter obviously had to ensure no one got actually hurt by the explosion, especially himself or Tuco, and given how powerful the explosion winds up being, a slight mistake could have been fatal. It’s likely that Walter took into consideration factors like distance, an estimation of the explosion, and possible obstacles that would shield against it, all in the short time he was in the room, and he had to aim properly on top of that. Walter takes advantage of the shock to grab the whole bag, so as to make sure the thugs don’t just shoot him for fear that his crystals might explode. Tuco gives Walter the money and agrees to do business with him on Walter’s terms. This is convenient for Tuco both because Walter has proven to be dangerous and unpredictable and because, as mentioned, his meth sells very well. This event starts the legend of Heisenberg and gives Walter his first real influence and reputation. And now, despite the setback, not only did Walter solve the feud, but his meth business is in its best form yet, having now a proper distributor. This is Walter’s first planned confrontation with a criminal. Walter quickly calculates the money he’d need to leave his family for a comfortable life and how many meth trades would be needed to reach that sum.
* A good state college, accounting for inflation, is 45,000 dollars a year. Walter needs to provide for 4 years and two kids. 45,000 * 2 * 4 = 360,000 dollars.
* The remaining mortgage for the home is 107,000 dollars; home equity line is 30,000. That’s a total of 137,000 dollars.
* The approximate cost of living for Skyler and the children is 2,000 dollars a month, so for ten years it’s: 2,000 * 12 * 10 = 240,000.
* Everything combined is: 360,000 + 137,000 + 240,000 = 737,000 dollars.
* The weekly income from dealing with Tuco is 70,000 dollars, which means: 737,000 / 70,000 = 10.5 weeks of drug manufacturing.

Common Misconceptions

Now that I’m finished with the feats, I’d like to tackle some notions commonly believed about Walter that paint him as less intelligent or more incompetent than he actually is.

1. Walter is just lucky.

This is arguably the most common and most damaging belief about Walter’s achievements.
I have seen quite a lot of people claiming that Walter’s success was always due to luck, rather than skill or intelligence.
Now, there are instances of Walter having some good luck, but he’s not inherently luckier than your average character. In fact, I’d argue that for any example of good luck on Walter’s part, you could give two examples of bad luck.
Here are a few examples:
1. Tuco’s sudden outrage at No Doze indirectly leading to his lair being raided and thus his kidnapping of Walter.
2. Walter’s mention of having a second cellphone to Skyler while anesthetized for his surgery.
3. Jesse, by complete chance, getting in a relationship with the older sister of the kid that the dealers working with Gus use for their business, leading to the conflict with Gus.
4. Skyler giving the drug money to cover Ted’s tax evasion.
5. Jesse making Gus suspicious during the execution of the hospital trap.
6. Hank deciding to use the master bathroom and finding Gale’s book.
7. Jesse having his meth lifted off him by Huell, leading to his realization of Walter’s lily of the valley scheme.
8. Hank happening to be following Jesse to Walter’s house, leading to their alliance.
These are only some examples, but they paint the picture. While Walter might’ve had some good luck from time to time, more often he has been quite unlucky, forcing him to adapt to unforeseen situations.
Aside from that, a character being lucky at times doesn’t invalidate all of their feats by default.

2. Walter is inferior to Gus and only beat him through luck.

This is a sister argument to the previous one, and there are a few things to talk about.
1. Walter’s operation was not a long-term one like Gus’s. His goal was to make enough money throughout the two years he had left to live. Gus had twenty years to build up a rock-solid operation paired with a public enterprise in Los Pollos Hermanos acting as a front, not to mention vital help from Madrigal.
2. This is a continuation of my previous point, but I think it’s unfair to say Walter’s victory over Gus is just luck. What’s lucky about it is simply Hector’s existence as an emotional weakness of Gus’s. Walter very likely wouldn’t have been able to kill Gus, but this is not because Gus was smarter. Gus had simply a lot more influence and material power than Walter, making the task of killing him almost impossible. On top of that, the opportunity to kill Gus isn’t served on a silver platter. Walter discovered this connection through Jesse’s intel, which was a deliberate part of his strategy. Even then, someone as smart as Saul thought the information to be useless, while Walter intuited its importance and was able to infer a whole hidden aspect of Gus’s personality from it. And still, this only provided a vague opportunity. Walter still had to persuade Hector, adapt his strategy, and misdirect Gus to achieve his goal. To make an example from another work (spoilers for Death Note, highlight the next portion of text to see): A similar argument would be to say that Light’s victory over L was completely luck-based because of the mere existence of the Shinigami Eye power. If that ability wasn’t a thing, L’s secret identity would’ve made it nearly impossible for Light to kill him, just as Gus’s power and protection made it nearly impossible for Walter to kill him. Both L and Gus had inherent qualities that made them hard targets for Light and Walter, respectively, and only the existence of a way to bypass said qualities (Shinigami Eyes and Hector) made them possible to defeat. Sure, there’s a degree of luck working in Light and Walter’s favor here, providing them with opportunities, but it’s minor compared to the initial bad odds they had, and even then, it took a lot of cunning and effort for each to capitalize on said opportunities. In all honesty, I’d even consider Light’s luck to be greater than Walter’s when comparing the two situations, but that’s beyond the scope of this example.
3. Besides that specific situation, I find the notion of Gus being smarter than Walter to be generally untrue. Gus is superior to Walter in some regards, no doubt, but in terms of sheer intellect, Walter is a man who was able to see right through Gus’s strategies basically every time. From Gus’s one minute strategy (the Twins vs. Hank), to his use of Gale as a substitute, to his manipulation of Jesse.

3. Walter is only capable of manipulating junkies.

Yes, I’ve heard this argument. Let’s get it out of the way.
Walter does indeed manipulate a junkie. Walter’s manipulation of Jesse is very advanced and shows how capable and shrewd he is. Just because Jesse is a “junkie” doesn’t mean he can be played with a few words. The methods Walter uses are clearly very thought-out and deserve analysis and consideration, and even then, they don’t always work. Is this because Walter is a poor manipulator? Far from it. Jesse, while not the brightest, has his moments of brilliance, some of which are even mentioned in this doc, though not in detail. Walter is not just using minor persuasion, either. More often than not, he’s actively pushing Jesse to go against his deep-rooted beliefs and morals, and for a big chunk of the show, Jesse was also being counter-manipulated by someone as cunning as Gus. All this is to say, manipulating Jesse is not as meaningless a feat as some make it out to be.
Besides that, the notion is also untrue. Walter, during the show, has successfully manipulated Skyler, Hank, Gus, Jack, Lydia, and more. So no, I’d say he can manipulate more than just junkies.

4. Walter couldn’t even keep his family from discovering his secret.

Technically true, but as I’ve already discussed at length, a lot of bad luck went into this. I also think it would be a disservice to Skyler to say that deceiving her is easy. She has solid reasoning, and she understands manipulative tactics enough to use them herself at times. Yet Walter was able to hide his secrets from her until he was literally sedated. Hank finding out is much more of a mistake on Walter’s part, but it’s still unlucky, and Walter would’ve been able to control the damage if not for Jesse. Something to mention about Hank, though, is that he’s a full-on detective with some very solid feats, like uncovering Gus’s operation, and someone who I could even consider low-tier SCD material.

5. Walter is sloppy and makes a lot of mistakes.

Most of Walter’s anti-feats are contextual and don’t invalidate his feats. For anyone reading this before the bulk of the doc, I talk more about this at the initial bit of the S5E16 analysis, and there are explanations for most major anti-feats throughout the doc.
