A NORMAL CHILD: EARLY LIFE OF OCTAVIUS
(63 BC-50 BC)

Born to the niece of Gaius Julius Caesar (daughter of his sister Julia Minor) Atia, the family situation of the young Gaius Octavius ​​was not easy during his childhood, due to the mismanagement of his father's estate (he died when Gaius was four years old) by some tutors, and the support of his mother alone.
Atia is described as a shrewd, politically skilled and determined woman, ambitious for her son, whom she subjected to a continuous education and strict discipline1.1.
(Adversity Capacity: Discipline-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
After his mother remarried with Lucius Marcius Philippus the little Octavius ​​was not entrusted to the care of his stepfather, but to that of his grandmother, the prestigious Julia Minor, who started him on advanced formation and study paths, especially in rhetoric and oratory. Certainly Julia wrote to her brother Julius Caesar about Octavius' capacities as a new pride for their gens1.2.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability/Cognitive Capacity-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
When he was 12 his grandmother died (51 BC) and since Caesar was in Gaul during his campaigns Octavius ​​was chosen as the male member of the gens to deliver the funeral oration (laudatio funebris)2. The laudatio involved the use of various encomiastic topoi towards the deceased and required solid oratory skills since it was delivered in the forum towards the funeral, indicating how Augustus had evidently already reached a remarkable level by that age.

It was natural that he would seek an heir since he had no legitimate male children and for this reason he had looked among his relatives. Despite having older cousins, Octavius ​​stood out in Caesar's eyes: even for his age he was handsome, charming, communicative and intelligent1. Although war and civil conflicts interrupted his linear education, Octavius ​​continued to be an avid reader and to practice oratory daily. CHAPTER III
THE SON OF GOD: IN WAR AGAINST MARK ANTONY AND THE SECOND TRIUMVIRATE (44 BC-42 BC)

Despite the contrary opinion of his mother and stepfather Octavius ​​(whom I will refer to from now on as Caesar or Octavian) decided to accept his great-uncle's (now his adoptive father) inheritance and took the name Gaius Julius Caesar Octavian. In addition to the name and political legacy of his father, Octavian also acquired two thirds of his enormous estate.
While still in Epirus he had thought of launching a first attack on some garrisons stationed in the region, but he put a stop to this rash decision. He also refused the advice of some soldiers to take refuge in Macedonia because of the danger of assassination against him1, realizing that only timely action would avoid his exclusion from the division of power in Rome.
(Adversity Capacity: Impulse Management-Reasoning: Deductive)
Quickly returning to Rome, Octavian found himself in a difficult situation as the city was teeming with enemies: power was now mainly in the hands of Mark Antony, who looked down on him and considered him a usurper of his position as Caesar's heir, and his father's assassins were still at large and were reorganizing their forces.
From this moment on, one of the cornerstones of Octavian's strategy was to use Caesar's name to attract as many veterans as possible. To pay them, he also appropriated public funds, but subsequent senatorial investigations into the matter deemed him not punishable for this, since part of the troops were used against Antony, at the time an enemy of the Senate. Given his already weak and unstable political situation, supported only by the prestige of his name, it is unlikely that Octavian would have moved in this direction without first foreseeing, also through his subsequent moves, the antagonism of Antony himself among a considerable part of the Senate. In 44 he also appropriated without permission some eastern tributes, a crime for which he will not suffer consequences in the future.

At the end of the year (November) he appeared in the forum where, after having given further proof of his oratory skills with a speech, he publicly demanded that he be granted the glory that had been his father's, with the acclamation of the crowd2. During the same period he began to organize games and spectacles for the people and in honor of Caesar, thus increasing his popularity among the citizens.3

In any case, young Caesar's position was still weak and for this reason he tied himself to the only figure available at the time: Cicero, the greatest statesman alive. Cicero had formally supported his father after the death of Pompey, but in reality he had applauded his death and sided with the Caesaricides. He was the head of the conservative party within the Senate and on a collision course with Mark Antony, whom he considered a threat for the Republic. An alliance with the young Caesar would have allowed him to obtain legitimacy from a part of the populares party and although he was aware that Octavian was gifted and ambitious, his young age made him appear malleable or at least less dangerous compared to Antony. At the same time, the son of Caesar was the main architect of the deception into which Cicero fell, acting in a meek manner and showing ambition, but above all loyalty to the Republic. During these battles, of which I will explain better later, he gave proof of great courage, personally participating in the clashes and heroically carrying Rome’s eagle when he saw that the soldier in charge had been wounded.6 His displays of ardour made him even more appreciated by the armies loyal to his father.

We also know that in these months he attempted to assassinate Antony through some hitmen, but the attempt failed7.
The causus belli had been Antony's desire to appropriate the province of Cisalpine Gaul despite it having been assigned to one of Caesar's assassins. The Senate had ordered that the fighting in the region cease, but Antony had disobeyed its orders. Octavian then, also thanks to Cicero's influence, was chosen as the sword of the senatorial will, and to this end, knowing that the Senate could not count directly on any army at the time, he had amassed a personal army.

In January 43 BC, thanks again to Cicero's support, he was appointed senator despite his very young age. At the same time, his military command over the troops he had recruited was legalized, as he had foreseen.
Octavian was then sent by the Senate to confront Antony, whose forces were besieging Mutina (Modena). Octavian's position in command of the army, however, was subordinate to the two consuls elected that year: Hirtius and Pansa. In addition to the three of them, there was also the Caesar-killer Decimus Brutus, theoretically the legal governor of the province of Cisalpine Gaul. Octavian's actions during this military campaign appear to us particularly Machiavellian and ruthless. Despite having quickly obtained an official military role and the senatorial office, he was aware that the main credit for the victory would had go to the consuls and he would have remained a mere prestigious pawn in the hands of Cicero, without being able to directly confront Antony as a true rival to Caesar's political legacy. Octavian was ambitious and wanted to regain Caesar's political power immediately; he had no intention of embarking on a long and gradual political career.

The joint army of the two consuls and Caesar arrived separately in the north of the peninsula and Antony soon realized that he would be surrounded by the enemy if he did not act quickly. For this reason he decided to attack first the army of Pansa, who was late in the march (Octavian and Hirtius were waiting for him). Antony's attack was victorious and in the armed clash Pansa himself was wounded, although not mortally8. However, it was also more difficult than expected since some support contingents had arrived without Antony's knowledge. While he was reversing to put Mutina back under siege Hirtius fell on him with fresh troops, defeating him and forcing him to retreat and turning his victory into a great loss. On this occasion Octavian limited himself to remaining on guard at the camp, but the wound inflicted on Pansa would be useful to him later.
After hearing of the victory at Forum Gallorum, Cicero delivered his fourteenth Philippics in the Senate, launching harsh attacks on Antony, but above all praising the work of Hirtius and Pansa. As Octavian had foreseen, little praise came his way, demonstrating that for Cicero his growth had to be put limited.

After the defeat, Antony withdrew his forces to Muntia and redesigned his strategy on the defensive, aware that a pitched battle would be difficult for his tired troops. Octavian and Hirtius, however, left him no chance: their ever-increasing approach forced Antony to leave the field to repel them. The battle that followed was bloody and saw Antony interested in containing the enemy's attacks as much as possible. At one point Hirtius personally charged against Antony's camp, in a maneuver that initially seemed to be successful. In the middle of the clash, however, Hirtius was pierced and his legion seemed to momentarily disband, until Octavian took over the leadership with vigor, fighting on the front lines to recover the body of the fallen consul (the episode of the eagle previously narrated referred to these moments). Antony, outnumbered by the enemy and whose troops were already tired, was forced to abandon the battle and the region itself, leaving Octavian the sole victor on the field. On this occasion the young man proved that although he could not be called a skilled commander, he had both the mettle and the ability to lead an army. After the victory, Octavian also made his hostility towards Decimius Brutus known, sending the Caesar assassin fleeing towards the East.
In the following years, Augustan propaganda was committed to portraying the Octavian of Muntia as a hero of the Republic, ready to risk his own life to bring back the body of the fallen consul. Decades after the death of the Princeps, however, Roman historiography began to revise the events surrounding the battle: Caesar would have actually stabbed Hirtius himself in the midst of the battle9 (we know that he was probably also in the offensive against Antony's camp), just as two days later he would have poisoned Pansa's wound10, leading to his death and causing the accusations to fall on his doctor after an investigation.
By the death of Hirtius and Pansa Octavian was not only presented as the only victor over Antony, but it made him the leading figure of the party, enabling him now to gain sufficient strength to deal with Antony as an equal.

Obtaining this status was enough for Octavian to consider his alliance with Cicero and the conservative factions of the Senate obsolete. Their influence had allowed him to position himself as a defender of the nation, but their attachment to the republican institutions would not have allowed him to carry out his plan of domination over Rome11.1. It was for this reason that he disobeyed orders to pursue Antony and his army, who had fled to Gaul and was busy raising new legions in the province. Antony was a better ally for the time being, so Caesar's strategy changed11.2 and he began negotiations to ally himself with him, who in the meantime had re-established relations with Marcus Lepidus, another high-ranking figure of the populares.

In the meantime he sent a centurion to Rome to obtain from the Senate the office of consul, which could not be acquired before the age of forty. The senators, frightened, initially accepted, but then took his mother Atia and his sister Octavia as hostages, threatening them. However, the two women managed to escape thanks to the help of the Vestals and Octavian at that point marched on Rome himself, forcing the Senate to surrender and obtaining the consulship through extortion. At this point Cicero's scheme over the young man could be said to be completely broken. Shortly thereafter, Atia died and Caesar forced the Senate to give her a public funeral, the first ever held for a woman in Rome.
Having become master of the city, he called Antony and Lepidus to sign a peace. In the meantime, he made clear his will to open a war against the Caesaricides: the amnesty for them issued by the Senate in 44 BC was cancelled and instead proscription lists were drawn up to persecute both the Caesaricides and their allies. Over 2,000 members of the Roman ruling class were condemned to death (300 were killed, the rest managed to escape), their assets expropriated and integrated into the resources of the new alliance. After having crushed the conservative resistance and expelled its members, the triumvirs set about reshaping the Senate. Furthermore, very high bounties were placed on the heads of the Caesars' assassins.

SUMMARY: THE FALL OF CICERO
As we have seen, the relationship between Octavian and Cicero began the day after Caesar's death. Octavian was then simply the prestigious son of the deceased dictator, but had no real power (he was building his own personal army at that time) while Cicero was the leader of the conservative party of the senate, former consul and the most influential politician of the republic together with Antony. The superiority of his position, the young age of the counterpart and the gentle ways of Octavian persuaded Cicero that he could be a tool to obtain legitimacy and consensus from parts of Caesar's units, ensuring military protection to the senate against Antony. Cicero was aware that it was a gamble, but an alliance with Antony was impossible and Caesar's assassinations were not a valid option due to their unstable situation. Octavian, however, never saw the alliance with the optimates as anything other than a way to acquire power, and the murders of the consuls confirm that his plan included from the beginning an alliance with Antony in case he failed to kill him. Having obtained the necessary military strength, Octavian forced Cicero and his faction to submit, ignoring the death of his supposed mentor. With this move he showed that he had already outsmarted the elderly orator in political cunning. As ruthless as he proved to be with Cicero, it must be remembered that the latter had no real intention of strongly promoting Octavian's career, but rather of using him for his own plan to save the republic and the young man, having understood this, never fell for his flattery. In the last meeting between the two, which took place shortly after Octavian's march on Rome, the orator was forced to ask the young man to accept the consulate with him as a colleague, but by now Caesar's son was answering him with irony13.

CHAPTER IV
CAESAR’S AVENGER: THE  VICTORY OF PHILIPPI AND  THE PARTITION OF ROME (42 BC-36 BC)

It was decided that Lepidus would remain to guard Rome while Octavian and Antony directly confronted Brutus and Cassius, the two leaders of the Caesar-killing faction that had taken control of the eastern territories of the Republic. The events at Philippi, the culmination of this civil war, were undoubtedly the lowest point of Octavian's career. From the moment of his departure, he was struck by a serious illness, which also delayed his arrival in Macedonia, where the battle was to be fought. Philippi demonstrated that the new Caesar was certainly not a thoroughbred general: his camp was attacked by surprise by Brutus' army, although he was not found inside, having managed to escape in time. Even during the battle, he had succumbed to illness, and this is primarily to blame for his inability to stem the Caesar-killing offensive. Thanks to Antony, however, the two battles of Philippi were ultimately won by the triumvirs, and Octavian nevertheless obtained the honors of victory.
He was certainly more brilliant in sending a signal of dominance to the few survivors of Philippi: while Antony had Brutus's lifeless body dressed and arranged as a sign of respect, Octavian then cut off his head and sent it to Rome, where he ordered it displayed before Caesar's statue. This clear message must have impressed the Roman citizens and terrified the enemies of the triumvir: in the last bloody decades of civil war, no one had ever dared to inflict such humiliation on a former Roman senator.
(Psychological Warfare: Demoralization/Emotional Inducement-Emotional Intelligence: Facilitation)
After the victory at Philippi, the triumvirate divided the Republic's territory into three zones, each of which was entrusted to a triumvir. Octavian chose to take Italy and Iberia for himself, the regions of operation of Sextus Pompeius. He was the youngest son (the only survivor) of Gnaeus Pompeius the Great, and he had survived Caesar's wars in Iberia (46-45 BC), managing to carve out a remarkable slice of power after the Ides of March, taking advantage of Rome's political instability. His strength lay primarily at sea: his fleet, composed, among others, of pirates his own father had fought, was the most powerful in the Mediterranean and had several legions under his control, with which he had taken control of Sicily. Octavian was aware that taming Sextus was necessary and useful for several reasons: it would further strengthen his role as Caesar's successor as it would end the civil war launched by his father against the Pompeian gens, it would guarantee absolute control of the Italian macro-region (essential for the stability of his dominion over Rome), it would have allowed his naval superiority and it would be an opportunity to unbalance the ties of power that linked him to Antony in the future, as we will see.
(Planning: Aim-Strategy: Building-Foresight: Depth)
Before he could focus on Sextus Pompeius, Caesar was forced to address two other conflicts, one a product of the other.
The first was the redistribution of lands promised to war veterans after the Battle of Philippi: approximately 170,000 men demanded as compensation sufficient land to cultivate their own crops, a classic reward for the conclusion of a legionary's career, but never managed on such a scale. Octavian arranged for a series of plots of land in central and northern Italy, but lacking sufficient terreins, he had to resort to a series of expropriations from local inhabitants, which created discontent (well narrated by Virgil in the Georgiche). Furthermore, he clearly favored his own veterans over those of Antony.
At the same time, he had just ended his first marriage with Claudia, Antony's stepdaughter (daughter from the first marriage of his influential wife, Fulvia), declaring that it had never been consummated, publicly humiliating Fulvia and, indirectly, Antony's family. Fulvia then, taking advantage of the fact that the distribution of land to the soldiers had been unequal, together with Lucius Antony (brother of the triumvir) caused a military riot to break out in central Italy. Antony's role in this drama was wisely neutral, just as Octavian preferred to limit the conflict to his colleague's relatives: both were still in too unstable situations and needed the other's formal support. For Octavian, this brief civil war served primarily to impose tight control over Italy, on which he could always count, and also to eliminate Fulvia, a woman overly ambitious and hostile towards him, from the political arena.
In addition to a disgruntled army, Lucius Antony leveraged some anti-Octavian senators, convincing them to legalize his operation. The son of God crushed the revolt without too many problems yet: not to mention that many of the armies present in Italy had not revolted because they were loyal to him, he cut off payments to the rest of the legions and gradually surrounded Lucius and Fulvia's positions, forcing them to retreat to Perugia.
(Strategy: Castling)
Throughout the winter of 41–40 BC, Perugia resisted, but at its conclusion, Fulvia and Lucius Antony were forced to surrender due to starvation. To avoid worsening relations with Antony, whose fleet he needed to fight Sextus Pompeius, Octavian spared both, although he ordered Fulvia sent into exile in Sycyon (Lucius, evidently less dangerous, was assigned to a governorship in Iberia). The same clemency was not granted to the city of Perugia, which had supported the rebellion, nor to the senators and equestrians who had taken refuge there. On the anniversary of the Ides of March, Caesar ordered the killing of 300 aristocrats (including senators and equestrians), ravaged Perugia, and exterminated the city's nobility. Besides sending a warning to his enemies, the sack of Perugia would also serve to calm the army's discontent, keeping his popularity high among them. To anyone who begged him for clemency, Caesar would have replied: "You must die"1.
(Psychological Warfare: Emotional Inducement/Demoralization/Reinforcements)
In the aftermath of Perugia, the political situation was still extremely tense: in recent months, Antony had begun his relationship with queen Cleopatra and had established even closer ties with the East, but above all, he had established contact with Sextus Pompeius with the intention of marginalizing Octavian. The two reached an agreement by which, in the event of war, they would take sides. Octavian, who was aware of Antony's movements, was undaunted, even when five of his colleague's legions landed in Southern Italy. He knew that an alliance with Pompey and a new war would be of no real benefit to Antony, both because Sextus was a self-appointed republican and his faction, which gathered the survivors of Philippi and the Pompeians, was completely opposed to Antony's support base (the old-Caesarians), and because Antony had no chance of starting a war against him: on the eastern border, in the area under his control, the threat of the Parthians was too insidious, and he was preparing a campaign against them2. Seeing the stalemate created (even the armies of the two triumvirs expressed their disagreement regarding a new war), Antony's bluffing was withdrawn, the entourages of the two leaders made peace and he retreated towards Syria.
(Reasoning: Deductive-Thinking: Critical/Analytic-Adversity Capacity: Impulse Management/Stress Tolerance-Counteraction: Trap Evasion-Field Crafting: Risk Calculations)
The reconciliation between the two triumvirs also allowed them to marginalize Lepidus, who was definitively relegated to a secondary role. To seal their ties, since Fulvia had recently died, Antony married Octavia, Octavian's sister (despite, as mentioned, being already in a relationship with Cleopatra). Octavian, who had recognized Sextus Pompeius's rule over Sicily for the moment (caused by the grain blockade imposed by Sextus Pompey over Rome, which led to popular revolts and discontent), also forced himself into a political marriage with Scribonia, Sextus's niece. However, the relationship with the woman was short-lived, because in 39 BC he already met Livia. Although both the women were married (Livia was also pregnant, as was Strebonia), Octavian and Livia immediately bonded and as soon as his wife gave birth (she will give the only child of Caesar, the daughter Julia) he divorced Scribonia, forced the breakup of Livia's marriage and married her, when she was also still 6 months pregnant. The break with Strebonia was the prelude to the reopening of hostilities with Sextus. The reopening was also caused by the breaking of the peace treaties by the leader of the pirates, who realized that they had been established by the triumvirs to cage him.
During this period, Octavian discovered that one of his associates, Salvidienus Rufus, had attempted to change his master and was about to ally himself with Antony. For this reason, he had him arrested, convicted of high treason, and forced him to commit suicide3.

Octavian's naval forces were initially unfortunate: they were twice overwhelmed by storms4, and on the few occasions they did engage in battle, either Octavian or his admirals were defeated by the young Pompey on the sea. Given the enemy's naval strength, Octavian asked Antony to send him a fleet, in exchange for which he would then send twenty thousand men to his colleague's Parthian campaigns. This lie would later become a hoped-for source of tension in the years to come.

SUMMARIZE: FIRST STEPS OF THE MASTER OF PROPAGANDA
In the popular imagination, Mark Antony is remembered as a man devoted to the pleasures of life, sensitive to women, and a betrayer of Roman values ​​in exchange for Eastern ones. This image of the character is actually the product of Octavian's (and later Augustus') propaganda machine, which operated with such force and precision that it destroyed the great merits Mark Antony had in life, both as a military commander and as a politician.
The erosion of Mark Antony (and Cleopatra) was the first test to which Caesar's son's propaganda was subjected, which over the course of 45 years of absolute rule over Rome would be refined to the point of becoming one of the most powerful ever employed in History.
Antony started from a better position than Octavian: he was Caesar's best general, having fought alongside him in Gaul and in the civil wars, and assuming a leading role during the years of his dictatorship. During the same period, Antony's actions against the Senate had also been a source of criticism from the young Caesar, the same young Caesar who on several occasions entered the Senate with his soldiers and armed to impose his authority by force (as in the months preceding Actium).

THE BIRTH OF AUGUSTUS: RISE OF THE EMPIRE (30-23 BC)

EMOTIONAL INTELLIGENCE
* Octavian's use of vengeance and clemency demonstrates how much he had learned from the experience of his great-uncle Caesar, who had excessively pardoned his enemies after Pompey's defeat. Octavian was generally firm and ruthless with his enemies, as evidenced by the numerous victims he eliminated in his rise to power. However, this bloodthirsty streak was only used at the time of his rise to power, not during his reign as emperor, leaving the common people and even the middle classes with the memory of a moderate and benevolent princeps. Indeed, starting with the victory at Actium, feeling politically secure and sensing the need to build a solid base of consensus that could unite all social classes and the major parties, Octavian began to combine (covert) repression with greater leniency toward his adversaries or critics, unless they were actually offensive. Facilitation-Realization-Management
* Although the young Caesar had always tended to be calm and cold, on one occasion he showed that even he could be guided by strong emotions, namely his divorce from Scribonia and his marriage to Livia. While it's true that his estrangement from Scribonia (who was the only woman to bear him a child) was inevitable given the impending conflict with his uncle Sextus Pompeius, his marriage to Livia was not without strong criticism from Roman society: Livia was already married (and Octavian forced the divorce), and more importantly, she was six months pregnant, making it completely scandalous for her to be married under those conditions. Octavian's love for her, however, was clearly too strong, and their union would always remain solid and vital to the empire. Engagement-Understanding
* Octavian's understanding of the masses was one of his finest qualities and played a key role in maintaining his popularity among the plebs. From the very beginning of his career, the Son of God placed the consensus and support of the people as one of his main strengths, thanks to his acquired surname and name. Caesar was a man of the people (symbolically, he was also a patrician), and Octavian presented himself as such. Throughout the various crises that beset his years of civil wars and government, he was always able to perceive and allay the populace's discontent and address their concerns and needs. And to do so, he did not rely solely on agents, officials, or collaborators; as already mentioned, both in his public walks and in his judicial hearings, Augustus was constantly in contact with the people. ADVERSITY CAPACITY
* Beyond specific episodes, Octavian's life was filled with grief, exceptionally so even considering the context of antiquity. He lost his father at four, his grandmother at twelve, his stepfather at sixteen, his mother at nineteen, his adoptive father at eighteen. He also lost a nephew, two grandsons, a son-in-law (and best friend), his other best friend (Maecenas), his sister, a stepson and even a son (Livia gave birth to an already dead child). He was also betrayed by his only daughter and forced to banish two other grandchildren. The only family members to survive him were his beloved Livia and the unappreciated Tiberius. Augustus always bore these losses and sorrows with fortitude, and nothing transpired in his continued work. Mental Fortitude
* The discipline instilled in him in childhood by Atia had an impact on his entire life. Octavian, like Caesar, despised luxury and ostentation (his palace was decidedly modest compared to those of his immediate successors) and avoided excess. He ate a simple diet (which perhaps allowed him to live so long despite his health’s frailty) and maintained a very disciplined routine. The only thing he seemed to lack in moderation was lust. MANIPULATION
* It is unclear to what extent the distance between Cleopatra and Antony grew after the defeat at Actium. Sources differ on this point (and we have to remember that they were buried together in their mausoleum), although almost all agree that Cleopatra actually attempted to independently approach Octavian. According to Suetonius, the general's suicide was caused by the queen herself, who spread the false news of his death, convinced by Octavian that this would spare her and her kingdom. While we have no certainty that this is true—indeed, it is an unlikely reconstruction—the young Caesar was undoubtedly skilled in keeping alive, but indefinite, relations and possible promises to Cleopatra after the Battle of Actium, with the aim of capturing her alive. DECEPTION
* In building his empire, Octavian did not limit himself to simply annexing conquered territories (Egypt, Asturias, Cilicia, Noricum, Germany and Illyricum), but, especially in certain complex areas, he employed client kings, local puppet rulers who answered to him and whose territories were under Roman military and administrative control. This approach greatly aided his control of the areas, which were made less turbulent by the presence of local symbolic figures, and helped him avoid appearing as an unjust conqueror in the eyes of the Romans, for whom the concept of "rightful war" was very important. Client kingdoms subjected to Octavian included Judea, Armenia, Mauretania, Cappadocia, and Thrace. Even the Ethiopian Empire fell under the influence of Rome. Sophistry

PLANNING
* I will limit myself here to summarizing the most important plans executed by Octavian. a| First part of the plan to reconquer absolute power belonging to Julius Caesar (from 44 BC to 42 BC) b| Plan to eliminate Mark Antony (from 38 BC to 32 BC) c| Plan to eliminate Lepidus (parallel to the war with Sextus Pompeius) d| Propaganda plan designed to legitimize the empire (follows the political movements from the Battle of Actium to 23 BC). Added to these are a series of minor plans in the military, political, administrative and propagandistic fields.

REASONING
* Observing and realizing that the Roman people had been too tired in the last century of civil war, Octavian understood that although his starting faction (the populares) was generally more progressive in the cultural field with a rapprochement with the Hellenistic world (as Caesar and Antony had been), it was more useful to resume and protect the ancient Italic-Roman values on which Roman civilization was based, in order to deliver a greater and strengthened identity that could stand comparison with the great political transformation of the Principate. Inductive
* Octavian was able to waive certain military objectives that could have been important to his expansionist plans. First and foremost, the Parthian Kingdom, Caesar's ultimate dream and the necessary path to achieving the glory of Alexander the Great, a widespread trope among all Roman generations from the time of Pompey to that of Septimius Severus. Ironically, Parthia would have been the site of Octavian's first great military campaign had Caesar not died, since he intended to appoint him commander of the cavalry alongside Lepidus. However, given the numerous theaters of war already open and, above all, realizing that Rome would find it excessively difficult and costly to maintain the occupation of a distant and rich territory like Persia (having just annexed Egypt), he decided to maintain the status quo imposed by Mark Antony's two eastern campaigns, limiting himself to tasking Tiberius with bringing Armenia, conquered by force and maintained by the diplomatic skill of Augustus himself, under his control. Likewise, Britain, another place of interest to Caesar, had been coveted for a long time, but it had been decided that it was too poor compared to Germany, the territory on which he instead wanted to focus more.
