The Impulses, Logic, and Convictions of Juan Pujol.-
Pujol makes a distinction between the Nazi leaders who promote this reprehensible ideology and the ordinary German citizens exposed to propaganda. He perceives that the latter do not carry the same responsibility as the leaders. He associates Nazi ideology with a distorted version of socialism. Instead of directly taking the people's resources, it depletes them through continuous demands and propaganda.

Pujol demonstrates critical thinking, as he does not merely accept ideologies uncritically. He interrogates their motivations and how they are used to exercise control over people. By recognizing parallels between the Spanish Civil War and the strategies of Nazi Germany, Pujol gains an understanding of the inherent risks of Nazi ideology.

Pujol learns about the Nazi extermination camps and the systematic elimination of Jews, Gypsies, and Slavs, which confirms his predictions about extremist ideals. Additionally, this cruelty deeply impacts him. His humanistic convictions prevent him from overlooking these atrocities. He experiences a profound sense of justice and a fervent desire to intervene.
Initially, he feels powerless. He cannot speak openly about the horrors, which causes him frustration. However, from this frustration emerges a plan. He decides not to be a mere spectator. He wants to take "practical" steps to contribute to the good of humanity.
Based on these motivations, Juan Pujol becomes one of the most notable spies of World War II, playing a crucial role in the Allied victory. Despite lacking previous experience and having limited knowledge, he embarks on this journey relying solely on his skills in deception and strategy. These motivations provide him with the mental fortitude necessary to carry out his actions.

Entering Espionage.-
Juan Pujol's original plan was to become a spy to serve the cause without involving himself in military realms. However, after being rejected by the British embassy in Spain, he decided to change his approach. Instead of working for the British, he chose to offer his services to the Germans, with the secret goal of helping the Allies and sabotaging the Nazis.
Juan Pujol followed a series of steps to become a spy for the Germans:
STEP 1: He contacted the German embassy in Madrid with the purpose of communicating with the military attaché, expressing his interest in offering his collaboration and support to the Nazi cause.

Encounter: Before meeting with one of the representatives, he thoroughly studied Nazi doctrines and ideologies to fully adopt the persona, perspective, and speech of a fervent supporter of the movement.
STEP 2:  During the first meeting at Café Alcalá with the military attaché, later identified as "Federico," Pujol presented his "supposed" Nazi and Francoist convictions, beginning to boast about them. Although Federico did not openly display skepticism towards Juan Pujol, Pujol perceived it and adjusted his approach upon realizing that he was making a poor impression. Pujol began to highlight Nazi victories, as he had noticed Federico's clear pride in German achievements during their conversations.

This approach helped to reduce Federico's suspicions.
Once Federico was at ease, Pujol seized the opportunity to present his ideas and offer his potential assistance to the Nazis. He claimed to have contacts and access to valuable information that could benefit the Germans, mentioning his connections in diplomacy and political circles. After this presentation, Juan Pujol managed to gain Federico’s trust, who promised to discuss his proposal with his superiors.
Pujol demonstrated convincing behavior as a committed follower of Nazi ideas, even when Federico showed skepticism. In the end, he managed to become a potential spy by earning Federico’s trust.

The Death of William Gerbers
At one point, Juan Pujol demonstrated an astonishing ability to adapt, as he was forced to eliminate one of his fictitious agents.
The problem arose with Gerbers, who was being prepared for the Allied invasion of North Africa, known as Operation Torch, in November 1942. The specific situation is described as follows:
“Gerbers’ failure to notice or comment on the evident military buildup at the Liverpool docks before embarkation would stretch German credibility.”
This indicates that Gerbers’ credibility with the Germans was compromised by not reporting the clear military activity and troop buildup at the docks before the invasion. Since Gerbers was supposedly living near these docks, the Germans would expect him to detect and report information about the military preparations.
If Gerbers failed to report this activity, it would reveal his fictitious identity and endanger the entire disinformation operation of his supposed controller, ARABEL (Juan Pujol). To avoid this risk, Pujol decided to "eliminate" him.
Thus, for Juan Pujol, the excuse for Gerbers' absence was carried out through a narrative claiming that Gerbers had been moved to a hospital.
Recall that the last time Gerbers was mentioned, "—Now let’s see how Juan Pujol executed his last notable deception while in Lisbon.” Pujol had stated that he had fallen ill. Using this fact, Juan Pujol adapted and incorporated it into his narrative by claiming that Gerbers eventually succumbed, after a long illness, on November 19.
This example demonstrates an incredible strategy of adaptability and efficiency on Juan Pujol's part. Not only does he manage to adapt his lies, but he also uses a pre-existing fact in his previous narrative to lend coherence to the situation.
It is a prime example of "strategy efficiency."

In addition to all this, Juan Pujol, with the help of British intelligence, published a fitting obituary in the Liverpool Daily Post on Tuesday, November 4, 1942: "GERBERS - November 19 in Bootle, after a long illness, at the age of fifty-nine, WILLIAM MAXIMILIAN. Private funeral. (No flowers)."
The newspaper clipping was quickly sent to Lisbon and then to the Germans, who completely believed the lie. With the coherent narrative that Juan Pujol had constructed and this fabrication of false evidence, it was clear that Pujol was telling the "truth."

Radio Strategy
Let’s talk about the radio communication strategy.
This strategy emerged out of the need when Juan Pujol (GARBO) and the British intelligence services (MIS) faced the slow communication with the Germans through existing channels. It became evident to Harris and MIS that Pujol's letters to Lisbon took at least a week to arrive, and often longer.
Therefore, Pujol and MIS decided to develop a new covert wireless communication method to speed up the transmission of false intelligence to the Germans. It was recognized that faster communication offered more opportunities, particularly in acquiring valuable signals intelligence. To execute this strategy, Pujol deceived and manipulated the Germans. Firstly, he informed them that he had recruited a "new agent" named CHAMILLUS, who was supposedly a Gibraltar waiter sympathetic to the Spanish Republican party. He explained that CHAMILLUS had befriended a fervent Spanish Republican, classified as a conscientious objector, who had a radio amateur license before the war. However, Pujol did not reveal to Agent 4(1) that CHAMILLUS was actually a German spy. Instead, he narrated a story about a group of Spanish Republicans in London, led by Juan Negrín, who supposedly wanted to stay in touch with the clandestine communist movement in Spain.
This was done to make it more credible and to offer a safer alternative for the Germans.
Pujol highlighted the advantage of this deception, as if the British discovered the transmitter and shut it down, no other member of his network would be at risk, since the police would likely accept the cover story as true.
Additionally, Pujol proposed to supply only encrypted messages to Agent 4(1), so he would never know the actual content of the messages.

OPERATION FORTITUDE.-

It is important to clarify that Operation Fortitude is a complex topic to explain. There are times when British intelligence is in charge of planning, while at other times Pujol or Harris take the lead. Essentially, Operation Fortitude is a collaborative effort. To avoid confusion, I will focus specifically on highlighting the sections where Pujol is directly involved in the planning.
Operation Fortitude represented the largest deception effort carried out during World War II. Essentially, this operation facilitated the Normandy landings by diverting German attention away from the true invasion area.
Stage 1: Diversion
To understand the initial steps of this operation, it is crucial to know the objective of British intelligence: to convince the enemy to place their troops as far away from the target area (Normandy) as possible. It was necessary to confuse the Germans about the exact time and place of the invasion so that even after the Normandy landings began, the Germans would continue to believe these were merely a diversion and that the main attack would take place elsewhere, further north.
Thus, Juan Pujol’s goal to ensure the operation’s success was to spread the following key elements:
      * The invasion would not occur until at least July 1944.
      * The invasion would be preceded by an attack on Norway (Operation Fortitude North).
      * The main attack in France would focus on Pas-de-Calais, although some diversionary actions would take place in other locations (Operation Fortitude South).

These diversions not only ensured that German forces would move away from the real target but also made them believe the invasion would occur much later than actually planned.
To accomplish this, Pujol did the following:
      * Step 1: Divert the timing of the invasion.
Pujol pretended to have conducted a tour of the southern coast of England and sent a report mentioning that he had observed some American soldiers near the city. He also described the distinctive shoulder insignia of these soldiers: "the number '1' in red on a khaki background."
This reference was deliberately aimed at a real unit, the 1st Infantry Division of the U.S., which had been relocated to England from the Italian front.

Pujol’s real objective was to provide credible information to maintain the illusion that preparations for the invasion were not advancing. To support this idea, AND SHIELD IT, other agents also corroborated Pujol’s observations.
      * Step 2: Divert the location of the invasion.
Although Pujol had already manipulated the Germans’ perception of the timing of the invasion, the task remained to divert their attention from the actual place where the Allies planned to attack. For this, Pujol sent another report in which he claimed to have observed “balanced” forces in England, ready to occupy any part of northwestern Europe if the Germans withdrew or collapsed.
Pujol argued that no great analytical ability was needed to conclude that if the Germans left the way open, the Allies would seize the opportunity without hesitation.

What did he achieve with this?
With this maneuver, Pujol managed to make the Germans focus on northwestern Europe, diverting their forces to that region and away from Normandy, the true target of the Allied invasion.
      * Step 3: Confirm the invasion.
The third step in diverting attention literally involved informing the Germans that an invasion was on its way.
Although this may seem confusing, it is important to remember the planning of the “first step.” In summary, the Germans already suspected that there might be an invasion and had an estimate of when and where it might occur, based on the information provided by Pujol. However, the invasion was not yet completely confirmed.
This is where Juan Pujol made a masterful move.
Pujol sent a report to the Germans claiming he had stolen some important pamphlets. He asserted that the purpose of the pamphlets was to be distributed among French civilians after a German withdrawal. The pamphlet encouraged civilians to cooperate with the new occupiers (the Allies, in case the invasion succeeded). Pujol presented these pamphlets to the Germans as proof that the Allies were preparing an invasion plan.
At that moment, the Germans would be completely convinced that the Allies were planning an invasion. However, the place and timing they believed, as it is obvious, were incorrect and manipulated.

(Fabrication, Indirect Manipulation, Strategy Shielding, Misdirection, Coverage, Intricacies)
Stage 2: Psychological Manipulation of the Abwehr
After completing the initial steps, Juan Pujol sought to gain the complete trust of the German intelligence agency (Abwehr). To achieve this, he devised a strategy with two main objectives:
      1. Improve his control over information regarding a possible invasion.
      2. Develop a plan for psychological manipulation of the Abwehr.
This strategy aimed to consolidate his position and manipulate the perception of German intelligence. Pujol's plan sought to leverage psychological elements to achieve his intelligence goals.
      * Improving Control Over Invasion Information
Pujol devised a strategy to enhance his control over the possible Allied invasion, using the radio to report to the Abwehr about a fictitious agent named Chamillus. According to the report, Chamillus had left his job as a waiter in Chislehurst to head to a supposed secret embarkation point. This invented agent allegedly observed that the 3rd Infantry Division was concentrated and ready to depart, with other troops prepared at nearby camps. The report included specific details such as the distribution of cold rations, sea sickness bags, and life vests—typical items for a sea voyage—thus reinforcing the idea of an imminent invasion.
Pujol's strategy had two main objectives:
      * Strategic Positioning of the Fictitious Agent: By placing Chamillus in Hiltingbury, Pujol created the illusion that this agent was in an ideal location to provide crucial information directly to him. This allowed Pujol to control what "reached" the Germans and when, effectively manipulating their perceptions and decisions.
      * Consolidation of GARBO's Reputation: Pujol also wanted to strengthen the reputation of his alias, GARBO, among the Germans by demonstrating his value as a reliable agent.
This strategy, effective on its own, was ingeniously modified by Pujol to also allow him to develop a plan to manipulate the Abwehr, which will be analyzed below…
      * Developing a Plan for Psychological Manipulation of the Abwehr (His Best Feat in Manipulation)
Juan Pujol’s plan to manipulate the Abwehr might seem confusing and contradictory, but it unfolded as follows:
The first step involved sending two types of information to the Abwehr from two fictitious spies.
      1. The first fictitious spy, J(5), reported that an imminent Allied invasion was impossible, arguing that the Allies were merely conducting maneuvers.
      2. The second fictitious spy, Chamillus, reported that the 3rd Canadian Division had left Hiltingbury and was heading to embark at Southampton, contradicting the information previously provided by J(5).
Pujol used this contradiction to "prove" that J(5) was mistaken, suggesting that the troops were not engaged in exercises but were likely preparing for a major military operation.

The second step was to contradict the previous contradiction, proving that Chamillus, not J(5), was the one who was wrong.

Although it sounds confusing, pay attention.
In the May 7 broadcast, Pujol expressed dissatisfaction with Chamillus, calling him "simple-minded" for misinterpreting military movements. Pujol argued that Chamillus had thought that the returning troops were second-line units, but they were actually the same 3rd Canadian Division that had left earlier. Pujol feigned anger at Chamillus and then admitted that J(5)'s information was correct, blaming himself for being influenced by Chamillus.

This was important because Pujol used this mistake to manipulate the Abwehr’s perception of him, presenting himself as an agent who acknowledges his errors and is capable of obtaining more accurate information through his ministerial contacts.
Result of the Manipulation:
When all this occurred, the Abwehr responded quickly, showing appreciation for Garbo's (Pujol’s) skill in managing and presenting information. Through his criticism of Chamillus and his reassessment of J(5), Pujol managed to strengthen his own credibility.
Resilience of the Strategy:
Although Juan Pujol sabotaged his own Chamillus strategy, this did not mean he would abandon it. The strategy had a particular resilience, as Pujol ensured that Chamillus was not perceived as an enemy or traitor, but merely as someone who had made a mistake. Thus, Juan Pujol managed to convince the German intelligence that they should not dismiss Chamillus but rather encourage him and not punish his mistake. He argued that this could cause problems if the true invasion was not reported due to excessive caution.

It is remarkable how Juan Pujol not only managed to reintegrate his strategy but also made the Abwehr encourage a fictitious spy. Surely, Pujol must have enjoyed manipulating German intelligence.
(Info Control, Fabrication, Flexibility, Strategy Resilience, Emotional Manipulation)
Stage 4: Preparations
In this phase, there isn’t much direct action from Pujol. It is a period marked more by the instructions given to him by British intelligence and by other activities carried out without his direct involvement.
In summary, British intelligence continues to fabricate false evidence to convince the Germans that the main invasion would occur in Norway, not Normandy, whether or not Pujol is involved.
However, there is an interesting and noteworthy aspect in the collaboration between Pujol and British intelligence.
Context: The British plan was for Pujol to inform the Germans about the imminent invasion in Normandy just about 3 hours before D-Day. At first glance, this might seem contradictory, but the goal of Operation Fortitude was always to convince the Germans that the invasion in Norway would be the real one and that Normandy was merely a diversion. If the Germans received the alert about Normandy just before it happened, they would think it was a distraction maneuver, reinforcing the idea that the real invasion would be in Norway, as Pujol had previously led them to believe. Additionally, this action was not as risky since, thanks to ISOS interceptions, the British knew that the Abwehr would take about three hours to transmit the warning to the relevant sectors through Berlin. This delay ensured that the warning would not compromise the Allied operation.
What’s the problem here?
The issue is that the D-Day invasion was scheduled for 6:30 AM, but Pujol had to send his warning at 3:00 AM. This posed a problem because the Abwehr usually closed its transmissions between 11:00 PM and 7:00 AM, meaning there would be no one available to receive the warning at that time.

However, Pujol, ever astute, devised a clever lie to navigate this difficulty.
He invented a story in which his supposed Greek agent, tasked with monitoring a fleet in Scotland, made the mistake of personally coming to London to see him, rather than sending an encrypted message as agreed to report on significant movements. Pujol argued to the Abwehr that this confusion was due to something extremely crucial happening and advised them to stay alert, active, and conduct nighttime surveillance.

This forced the Abwehr to stay awake and vigilant, allowing British intelligence's strategy to be successfully executed.
(Coverage, Fabrication)
Stage 5: D-Day
When D-Day arrived, some complications arose, and not everything went as planned. However, due to the detailed planning carried out by Pujol and British intelligence in "Operation Fortitude North," the operation was quite successful. The Allies managed to land in Normandy, surprising the Germans and causing nearly 6,500 enemy casualties. Nonetheless, this did not mark the end of the plan.
Pujol and British intelligence then decided to focus their efforts on supporting "Operation Fortitude South," which aimed to convince the Germans that the real invasion would occur at Pas-de-Calais, and that the Normandy landing was merely a diversion.

The False Invasion at Pas-de-Calais:
An unforeseen issue arose when, in a speech given on the afternoon of June 6, 1944, Winston Churchill made a careless statement about the landing. He suggested that the Normandy landing was only the beginning of a series of assaults, contradicting the carefully crafted deception that the main invasion would take place at Pas-de-Calais.
However, Juan Pujol was not deterred by this situation. With his ingenuity, he leveraged this problem to further reinforce the idea that the main invasion would be at Pas-de-Calais.
Pujol justified Churchill’s statement by arguing that his speech was influenced by his political position. According to Pujol, Churchill felt the need to be transparent and not distort reality, even if it compromised the deception.

This explanation aimed to minimize the impact of Churchill’s mistake and reassure his German contacts that the deception was still intact.
(Adaptability, Planning Versatility, Fabrication)
Manipulating the Abwehr:
To gain more credibility and reinforce the idea that the Normandy invasion was a distraction and that the main attack would occur at Pas-de-Calais, Pujol once again manipulated the Abwehr. His tactic involved feigning outrage over a supposed delay in receiving messages, claiming that the problem was due to a lack of seriousness and responsibility on the part of the Abwehr. He used a desperate and frustrated tone to exaggerate the severity of the issue and maintain his credibility. Pujol went so far as to say:
“I cannot accept the idea of jeopardizing the service without any benefit. If it weren’t for my ideals and faith, I would abandon this job for having proven to be a failure.”

The Abwehr responded to Pujol’s complaints, justifying that the delay in receiving his messages might be due to adverse conditions, although they acknowledged his effort and dedication. With this tactic, Pujol managed to put pressure on the Abwehr, making them believe that the failures were on their part while he remained committed to the operation.

This led the Abwehr to worry about maintaining contact with Pujol, without considering the risks or questioning the situation.
(Emotional Manipulation, Strategy Shielding, Psycho-Warfare: Abusing Insecurities)
Outcome:
Thanks to the deceptions Pujol had implemented to bolster "Operation Fortitude South," a devastating impact was made on the German high command. This led to the cancellation of a major armored counterattack in Normandy. The troops heading to Normandy from Pas-de-Calais were ordered to return to their bases, thus preventing a feared armored attack. This result represented a significant success for GARBO and Operation FORTITUDE, as it helped make the landings in Normandy more effective by reducing German resistance in that crucial area.
