EARLY LIFE:
Richelieu's life was difficult from the start. His birth was a struggle that nearly cost his mother her life (some even speculate that she may have been traveling while pregnant). From birth, it seemed fate had given him a fragile body, which was sensitive, frail, sickly, thin and weak, prone to fevers and prone to headaches. At the same time, he grew up during the French Wars of Religion, when disease, plague, and violence were simply the norm. The situation worsened further after his father's death, which left the family in financial instability, and the family lived with the pressure to restore their status. Yet Richelieu pushed through, as even though he was deeply emotional, he trained himself to cover it with discipline and reason.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 23-26.
(Adversity Capacity)
Richelieu's education began under a man named Hardy Guillot, who taught him Latin. He then studied at the Collège de Navarre, where he delved deeply into religion, grammar, the humanities, rhetoric, mathematics, philosophy, and natural science. Along the way, he also learned Greek, Italian, and Spanish, though not as fluently as Latin, he was still fairly proficient in them. One notable trait was his strong grounding in debate. After Navarre, he studied philosophy at either the Collège de Lisieux or the Collège de Calvi, where he learned scholastic controversy, the formal art of debate involving making distinctions, defending theses, raising objections, and arguing logically. He also attended the Pluvinel Academy, where he received the education of a nobleman, learning court and military skills such as weapon handling, horseback riding, fencing, dancing, music, vaulting, wrestling, and posture training that prepared him for both military service and social circles. So beyond his intellectual education, he was also physically fit and prepared for war, making him dangerous. Hildesheimer notes that by 1602–1603, the young Richelieu seemed well-prepared to make a strong impression both at court and in the army.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 27-28.
(Crystalized Intelligence)
Interestingly, Hildesheimer notes that part of his education involved training his memory; he had to memorize lengthy texts, rules, speeches, and arguments. From this, we can infer that he possessed a remarkably keen memory.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 27.
(Memory)
Context: Despite the hardships the family faced, they retained the right to nominate the bishop of the Diocese of Luçon. This privilege was first given to Richelieu's brother Alphonse, but he abandoned the position to become a monk, leaving it vacant.
To resolve this, Richelieu stepped in to fill the role. Though he had been raised and trained for a noble, military-oriented future, he took on the position willingly and without resistance. It marked a complete reversal of the life he'd expected, yet he adjusted to it remarkably quickly. Once he accepted the role, Richelieu, still only 17, devoted himself to study.  Rather than attending regular classes, he studied theology alone, bypassing the traditional path (bachelor, licentiate, and later doctor). Hildesheimer notes that despite this, he was still accepted, Henri IV nominated him as bishop, and later, when Richelieu traveled to Rome to argue his case, Rome itself accepted him as a consecrated bishop, even though the minimum age was 26 (he was still only 21 at the time). Richelieu's Fluid Intelligence is evident here, as he seems to have possessed knowledge comparable to that of a licentiate or doctor, through his means.
Richelieu was even admitted among the members of the Collège de Sorbonne. Gaining academic recognition in theology, even though, as mentioned before, he did not complete the full normal route to licentiate or doctorate.
Though this was also the product of his hard work. Burckhardt notes that people said his lamp burned continuously until morning. He quickly mastered the required knowledge and sought to demonstrate his abilities.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 37.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 33-35.

Even though he was now bishop, Richelieu did not go to his diocese; he remained in Paris for a while. In that time, he would build valuable relationships and even win the king's favor and trust. Henri IV reportedly referred to him as "my bishop."
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 37.
(Influence Building>Charisma)
Context: Richelieu arrived at Luçon and found a poor, muddy, damaged diocese. The episcopal palace was ruined, the cathedral had been devastated by the Wars of Religion, and the region was still divided between Catholics and Protestants.
To fix all this Richelieu would:
* Negotiate with the canons and make a financial agreement for the cathedral’s restoration.
* Impose stronger episcopal authority over the diocese, basically a strong bishop-centered control.
* Repair relations with the cathedral, negotiate over finances, and obtain cooperation from nobles and abbeys that had competing authority inside the diocese.
* He also brought in religious reformers to help him strengthen Catholic life.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 38-40.
(Leadership)
Richelieu helped a woman who was asking for advice, and in the letters we have from that discussion, we can see his strong emotional intelligence. He had recognized that the woman’s problem was not simple weakness, but an inner conflict. He saw that she was torn between sacrifice and God on one side, and comfort and worldly attachment on the other. Instead of merely commanding her, he tried to redirect her toward self-control, telling her that the outcome depended on her will.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 41.
(Overall Emotional Intelligence)
Richelieu would step away from Luçon and go to Paris. During that time, he built a network of friends, domestic loyalists, clerical allies, religious reformers, and court contacts. To give some example; Sébastien Bouthillier, son of a lawyer, was someone who he would entrust with important missions. Pierre de Bérulle was a major Catholic reformer and founder of the Oratory of France, linking Richelieu to religious circles. Then there was Father Joseph, who was Provincial of Touraine, meaning he held a great deal of influence (this was the man who would see potential in Richelieu and later become his collaborator, known as the "Grey Eminence.") Richelieu also established a friendship with Duvergier de Hauranne, the future Abbé de Saint-Cyran, who was then Grand Vicar of the diocese.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 47-48.

RICHELIEU'S RISE TO POWER:

Context: After the assassination of Henry IV of France, France plunged into instability. Marie de’ Medici struggled to manage the aftermath and sought an alliance with France’s rival, Spain. This caused unease among the Protestants, and numerous revolts were plotted, one nearly succeeded. At this time, France was extremely unstable, with many nobles vying for power. It was in this chaotic environment that Richelieu would later rise to prominence.
Richelieu at the Estates General
During the meeting of the 3 Estates, in which Richelieu took part, a serious dispute broke out between the nobility and the representatives of the Third Estate. The conflict began when one speaker strongly criticized the nobles, accusing them of trying to protect their own wealth while seeking to reduce the resources available to the royal treasury. The nobles reacted angrily, claiming that their honor had been insulted, and even threatened to boycott  the assembly. At this moment, the clergy intervened to calm the situation. Richelieu helped manage the situation by persuading the speaker to apologize to the nobles. This revealed his early ability to manage tense political conflicts, negotiate between opposing groups, and act as a mediator in moments of instability. Later, Richelieu delivered the closing speech of the assembly on behalf of the clergy. He would argue that priests should play an important role in government. His reasoning was that religious figures had often served as trusted advisors to rulers throughout history. He also believed that members of the clergy were especially suited for administrative positions because they were educated, disciplined, had no children to enrich, and were expected to place the public good above private ambition. For Richelieu, these qualities made clergymen capable officials and, in theory, less vulnerable to corruption. Richelieu also used the speech to criticize major problems within the French state. He condemned abuses such as hereditary offices, where positions of authority could be passed down like family property, and the venality of office, a system in which government posts could be bought and sold. He argued that these practices weakened and corrupted the state. Beyond these criticisms, Richelieu suggested that, through reform, finances could improve, corruption could be reduced and the nobility could be redirected toward loyal service to the king, rather than private rivalries and internal conflict.
Hildesheimer later noted that the ideas Richelieu expressed at this meeting were consistent with the political principles he would later develop in his Political Testament. Overall, the speech was great, it captured the audience’s attention, strengthened Richelieu’s reputation, and, as Burckhardt emphasizes, made a powerful impression on Marie de’ Medici.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 59-61.
(Communication>Persuasion>Inductive Reasoning>Relationship Management>Influence Building)
What is interesting about Richelieu during this period is that, although he was deeply ambitious and eager to enter the political arena, he carefully concealed it. While privately preparing and planning his rise, he presented a completely different public image, one of calm indifference. He immersed himself in obscure theological studies and wrote to colleagues in the tone of a contented, aging bishop satisfied with his quiet diocese, even allowing friends to urge him to pursue greater ambitions. Showing too much eagerness could make him appear desperate. By letting powerful figures seek him out instead, Richelieu increased his leverage. Also this protected him, as if political events turned against him, he could claim that he had been drawn into affairs reluctantly rather than seeking power himself.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 61-62.
(Acting Skills>Concealment>Contingency)
Richelieu sought to build influence by attaching himself to the Concini faction. Although Concini was widely hated in France, he had the favor of Marie de’ Medici and had been made Marshal of France. Richelieu therefore worked to gain his trust, befriending people such as Claude Barbin, who in turn introduced him to Eleonora Galigaï, a woman who wielded some influence over the queen mother (Marie). Richelieu cultivated a close relationship with her, and she eventually helped recommend him to the queen mother-regent. All while, he wrote highly flattering letters to Marie, declaring in one of them that he would dedicate his entire life to her service, and that God should shorten his own life to lengthen hers.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 63.
(Influence Building>Charisma)
Richelieu would be assigned important roles, such as when Condé rebelled and Richelieu was sent to Bourges to negotiate his return. Hildesheimer describes his persuasion as charming, saying that he used the beautiful words he knew how to handle so well. In this way, Richelieu began to gain a reputation as a mediator and negotiator.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 64.
(Persuasion)
Context: On 25 November 1616, Richelieu received a royal patent making him Secretary of State.
Context: The Habsburgs were divided into two branches.
* Madrid branch: Spain, Spanish Italy, and the Spanish Netherlands.
* Vienna branch: Austria and the Holy Roman Empire.
The danger for France was that these two branches could cooperate and surround her. And even if they were allies, Spain would still encircle France, even if it were non-intentional. As Hildesheimer notes, Spain wanted a secure road connecting its Italian territories to the Spanish Netherlands because it was fighting the Dutch rebels in the Low Countries. This meant Spain needed to move soldiers and supplies from Italy toward the Netherlands.
This route was basically Milan → the Alpine passes → Tyrol / the Empire → the Spanish Netherlands.
So for France, the situation was still dangerous. Habsburg ambitions would make encirclement imminent. This was the reasoning Richelieu probably came to. As he would face this dilemma; should France return to the more aggressive anti-Habsburg policy of Henry IV by protecting its allies in Italy and resisting Spain? As we know, Richelieu would eventually pursue this anti-Spanish policy.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 73-74.
(Deductive Reasoning>Decision Making)
Then we get to Richelieu’s first use of propaganda. Richelieu would later have many feats in this category, as he used propaganda for many purposes. Here, it is interesting because we see it for the first time. He wrote royal proclamations against the rebellious princes, presenting the king as merciful and the rebel princes as ungrateful and disobedient. Another interesting detail is that he did this very quickly, the Declaration of the King on the New Disturbances in the Kingdom took him only 3 days to finish.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 74.
(Propaganda>Mass Manipulation)
Why Did Richelieu Side with France’s Most Hated Faction?
We then come to the question ( though I already responded to it before, I did so vaguely, here I will go into detail): why did Richelieu side with France’s most hated faction, especially since this faction would later end terribly? Was it a blunder? I would argue no. Richelieu knew precisely what he was doing. His ambition was to shape French foreign policy, and rather than waiting for ideal conditions, he used the existing political landscape as his entry point into power.
* Richelieu’s Contingency:
But he did not do this blindly. While he worked with the Concini faction, Richelieu was already trying to loosen his dependence on them. That is why he acted indifferent, as if he were merely a theologian, a normal bishop, and nothing more. Even a few days before Concini was murdered, Richelieu quietly turned his attention to a figure whom most people at court dismissed entirely, Charles d’Albert, Duke of Luynes. Few recognized Luynes as someone worth watching, but Richelieu did. This is important because, when the young king (Louis XIII) eventually moved against Concini and his mother, Luynes would rise to prominence almost overnight. Richelieu wanted to place himself at the disposal of the situation. In other words, caution. Richelieu seems to have already sensed danger and may have predicted that something would go wrong. Not necessarily that Concini would be assassinated, but that the political situation could collapse, and that allying with Luynes was way more beneficial, foreseeing his future rise.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), pp. 75-76.
(Contingency>Preclusion>Prediction)
Context: Eventually, the Concini faction collapsed when Luynes and Louis XIII had Concini killed in a coup. The mob even stole Concini's body and tore it apart with a ferocity that revealed just how violently despised he had been. Even Concini's wife was not spared; she was later executed on charges of witchcraft. Eventually, nearly everyone associated with the Concini faction was arrested and treated harshly, except Richelieu. His earlier actions had made him appear only marginally involved and largely innocent. As a result, although he was humiliated and forced to resign from office, losing his influence for the time being, he avoided arrest.
Richelieu would actually later claim in his Memoirs that Luynes offered him the chance to remain in the Council with his salary, but that he refused out of honor and loyalty to the fallen queen mother.
(Trap Evasion)
There is an interesting anecdote that entails that, when Richelieu was face to face with Concini's mutilated body, Richelieu realizes the danger of his position in an instant, if the crowd recognized him as one of Concini's allies, he could easily share the same fate. Without hesitation, he shouted "long live the king!" The crowd took up the cry with enthusiasm, and he was allowed to pass unharmed.
Françoise Hildesheimer, Richelieu (Paris: Flammarion, 2021), p. 84.

Abandoning Marie de Medici Strategy
Context: After Concini’s downfall, Richelieu survived politically by repositioning himself as a mediator between Marie de Medici and the new royal government. He followed Marie to Blois as the head of her council, but this placed him in a dangerous position. On one side, he had to advise, restrain, and manage the queen mother. On the other, he also had to maintain communication with Luynes and Déageant, ensuring that the royal government did not view him as an outright enemy. This made Richelieu useful to both sides, but also deeply suspicious to each.
Caught between Marie’s demands and the distrust of the king’s ministers, Richelieu eventually decided to leave Blois on 11 June 1617 after hearing that the king’s council had supposedly resolved to disgrace him. The warning was false, but Richelieu still would use his withdrawal to his advantage, and turn it into a political maneuver. As Bergin later notes:
“he seems to have wished to force the hand of both Marie and Luynes, and to elicit firm expressions of support from them.”
In other words, Richelieu hoped that by removing himself from Blois, he could compel both Marie and Luynes to clarify their positions toward him. If Marie still needed his counsel, and if Luynes still found him useful as a channel of communication with the queen mother, then both sides would be forced to acknowledge his value. By refusing to return immediately, Richelieu seems to have hoped that an official order recalling him would not only protect him, but also strengthen his authority and make his political role harder to ignore. At first, however, the maneuver failed. As Richelieu would later find himself pushed even farther away from power. As he was exiled to Avignon, and the consequences of his disgrace even affected members of his family. This period was one of the darkest moments of his career, and Richelieu himself appears to have experienced suicidal thoughts. Yet the strategy was never truly foiled, as Richelieu’s strategy depended less on what Luynes could do to him, and more on Marie de Medici and the instability of her faction. Luynes could humiliate Richelieu, isolate him, and push him away from court, but Marie herself remained far more difficult to control. Her resentment, ambition, and circle of discontented allies created a political problem that the royal government could not easily solve through force alone. Eventually, this opened the door for Richelieu’s return. When Marie escaped from Blois and threatened civil war in 1619, Richelieu was recalled because he was seen as the only man capable of solving the crisis.
* Richelieu’s Planning Coverage:
During his exile, Hildesheimer notes that Richelieu had already begun preparing a defense of himself, known as the Caput apologeticum. He still feared that he might be implicated during Barbin’s trial, even though he was not. Still, the danger was possible, and enough for him to prepare arguments in advance.

During Marie’s exile at Blois, while Richelieu stood apart, Luynes was busy consolidating, filling the court with allies, building networks, entrenching his position. Richelieu may not have viewed Luynes as an outright enemy. Luynes however once his government was becoming unpopular, he recognized a threatening possibility: if opposition began to coalesce, it would naturally gravitate toward Marie de Medici as its figurehead and Richelieu as its strategist. That combination had to be broken up. Which is why Luynes began severing Richelieu's connections at court, his brother the Marquis de Richelieu and his brother-in-law Pont-de-Courlay were both removed. Then came the trap. Luynes arranged for prisoners like Claude Barbin to be released and permitted to communicate with the queen, while intercepting the letters that followed. Once sufficient material had accumulated, he laid it before Louis XIII, presenting it as proof of a dangerous conspiracy. The crackdown that followed was swift, the governor of the Bastille was arrested, pamphleteers who had backed the queen were publicly punished, and certain political writings were burned at the Place de Grève. Richelieu had no actual involvement in any of it, but he was the intended target from the beginning/
Context: As mentioned before, Marie escaped from exile with the help of Rucellai and Épernon, then launched a propaganda campaign against her son. Luynes had no choice but to seek negotiations. His attempts to push Richelieu out of power failed, as he was forced to invite him back into the political field to help manage the situation.
Once Richelieu arrived at Angoulême, he analyzed the situation and noticed that Marie’s circle was divided between two factions: one around Rucellai and the other around Épernon. He then asked himself who truly held power. Richelieu concluded that it was Épernon. Rucellai may have been the mastermind behind Marie’s escape and her closest confidant, but Épernon had far greater influence, and military power. Winning him over meant giving Richelieu a political base within Marie’s court. Therefore, Richelieu visited Épernon first.

(Abductive Reasoning>Analytical Thinking)
Richelieu also wrote a short strategic memorandum for Marie, in which he advised her to pursue two objectives simultaneously.
1. In the short term, she needed to secure a residence, genuine freedom, and political safety. She should not rush back to court, as doing so would be dangerous because of the mistrust surrounding her. She needed political independence. Richelieu believed returning immediately to court would simply place Marie back under Luynes' control.
2. In the long term, she should work toward reconciliation with the king.
Beyond these two aims, Richelieu also advised her not to let her emotions drive her into protecting all her followers unconditionally. He seems to have foreseen that more factions would emerge over time, so he warned her that, if reconciliation required sacrificing some political allies, she should be willing to do so.

(Strategy Building>Strategy Foresight>Preclusion)
During the negotiations that led to the Treaty of Angoulême, Richelieu was not initially the leading figure, but he eventually became the most important and valuable one. Due to his Abandoning Marie de Medici Strategy, he had placed himself in a neutral position. The royal government was willing to cooperate with him, unlike Épernon and Rucellai, who had both helped Marie escape from Blois. Richelieu took advantage of this by becoming the bridge between Louis XIII and Marie. He also built a communication network with the Jesuits Arnoux and Suffren, who were the confessors of Louis XIII and Marie. This gave Richelieu direct access to their spiritual advisers, allowing him to gradually place himself at the center of both sides. During the negotiations, Richelieu was the one who kept them alive, even when different advisers changed positions or argued against compromise. He stood firm and sought the most favorable settlement, though he also knew when to compromise, since he did not want to risk a civil war that could ruin his strategy. In the end, even though he had to struggle with both sides and avoid civil war, he eventually helped secure the Treaty of Angoulême.
The Treaty of Angoulême in 1619 gave Marie de’ Medici a secure and politically independent residence by granting her the governorship of Anjou, along with the important towns of Angers, Chinon, and Ponts-de-Cé. It also restored control of her household, granted amnesty to her supporters, reinstated their offices and pensions, and had the Crown pay her debts. In return, she agreed to make peace with Louis XIII and suspend open conflict, though full reconciliation remained incomplete.
Joseph Bergin, The Rise of Richelieu (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1997), pp. 185-187.

Even the papal nuncio, who had previously noted that Richelieu had "a hot head", now concluded that Richelieu was the man to support against Rucellai. That is a remarkable change in only a few months.

Richelieu then began to build his own faction. He started promoting his supporters and surrounding himself with relatives, close friends, in-laws, and loyal servants. The Angoulême Negotiations 1619 (Explanation)
The strategy Richelieu had detailed to Marie in his memorandum is interesting, especially when one considers its results and Richelieu’s masterful control over its execution. First, it is important to note that Richelieu’s strategy was not accepted at once. Marie initially became even more aggressive, but Bergin says that Richelieu’s advice eventually grew on her and began to influence her. In the end, Richelieu’s strategy unfolded largely as he had intended, and its execution came increasingly under his control. The short-term objective was achieved first. Marie did not return to court. Instead, the settlement gave her a safer political base: she received Anjou, along with important towns such as Angers, Chinon, and Ponts-de-Cé. Her household was also restored, her supporters were granted amnesty, and her debts were paid. Richelieu was central to the execution of this strategy, since he eventually became the mastermind behind the Treaty of Angoulême, which gave these concessions to Marie. The execution was difficult, and if Richelieu had not compromised strategically, he could have risked causing a civil war. As for the long-term aim, matters were still not fully settled, but they were moving in the right direction. Richelieu would write letters to Luynes and Marie would eventually reconcile with her son, though this had not yet been fully achieved at the time. For now, the foundations had been laid, and Richelieu would continue working toward that future reconciliation. Then there is Richelieu’s advice that Marie should be willing to sacrifice certain allies if reconciliation required it. This unfolded almost exactly as he had advised and foreseen. Marie began to loosen her attachment to political allies who trapped her in difficult dilemmas, such as Rucellai, and she gradually came to trust Richelieu more. Rucellai himself was effectively sacrificed when Marie blamed a politically damaging letter on him instead of taking responsibility herself. His influence steadily declined after this. Whether or not this was directly part of the original policy, Richelieu then sought to control Marie’s court by reducing the influence of his rivals and pushing them aside. In this way, he reshaped the broader political field so that it better served his larger aim of reconciliation with Louis XIII.
(Strategy Logistics)
The reason why Richelieu sought for Angers, Chinon, Ponts-de-Cé and Anjou in general, was because these towns were strategic crossings along the Loire River. Richelieu would have realized that whoever had control over these places could control movement, communication, and military operations across the river. Thus Marie and Richelieu could defend themselves and resist pressure from the royal government if conflict resumed, conflicts which will resume at the 1620 revolt (Contingency).
Joseph Bergin, The Rise of Richelieu (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1997), pp. 193.
(Contingency>Preclusion>Abductive Reasoning)
There was still a problem even after the Treaty though, Luynes wanted Marie back at court so he could control her, and he therefore tried to pressure her into returning. Richelieu, however, still wanted proof of genuine reconciliation and evidence that Luynes could be trusted. Marie understood this, so she rejected these proposals. Soon, however, rumors began to spread that Richelieu was deliberately trying to delay the reconciliation and keep control over the queen. Even the papal nuncio suggested this. Richelieu was now in a dangerous position: he could lose everything.
To avoid being trapped, Richelieu acted carefully. When his uncle, Amador de La Porte, went to court to become governor of Angers, Richelieu instructed him on what to say. If anyone asked whether Marie should return to court, La Porte was to answer yes, BUT only if the queen herself freely decided to do so. In this way, Richelieu avoided openly opposing reconciliation while also avoiding direct support for a return that might place Marie under Luynes’ control.
Joseph Bergin, The Rise of Richelieu (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1997), p. 195.
(Trap Evasion>Logical Manipulation)
Luynes, on the other hand, would not give up. He continued trying to poison the well by taking actions that weakened Marie’s position. For example, he helped release people who held resentment against her, such as Henri II, Prince of Condé. The crown also delayed Marie’s payments, rewards, and promised concessions, while rejecting her demand for equal rehabilitation. While this was happening, Luynes continued accumulating offices in order to strengthen his own power. He became a duke, peer, and Marshal of France; secured duchies for his brothers; and took control of several important fortresses on the northern frontier near Richelieu’s and Marie’s powerbase.
The 1620 Revolt
Context: Beyond the earlier maneuvers Luynes had already carried out against Marie and Richelieu, he also seems to have recognized what Richelieu wanted most, the cardinalate. He used this ambition against him with calculated cruelty. When Richelieu pursued the red hat, Luynes offered only vague encouragement, suggesting that Richelieu’s services in reconciling the king and queen mother would eventually be rewarded. Then, without warning, he withdrew his support and proposed that the honor should instead go to Louis de Nogaret de La Valette. To make the insult worse, he even asked Richelieu himself to compose the letter recommending his rival to Rome.
From that point on, Richelieu’s hatred of Luynes became much deeper. He no longer saw him merely as a personal rival, but as a dangerous favorite who was using his influence over the king for private advancement. In Richelieu’s eyes, Luynes was not only blocking his own rise, but also weakening France by indirectly serving the interests of the House of Austria.
At the same time, opposition to Luynes was already gathering around Marie de’ Medici because of his previous maneuvers. Several great nobles and political figures moved into her orbit, including:
* Soissons
* Mayenne
* The Vendômes
* D’Epernon
* Lesdiguières
* The Protestant leader Henri de Rohan.
These were among the most powerful men in the kingdom, and their support gave Marie’s movement real political weight. However, they were still no match for the royal army.
Richelieu did not create this coalition, and at first he did not necessarily see it as useful. His position was extremely dangerous: he was opposed by the king’s favorite while remaining dependent on the queen mother. Yet he quickly understood the opportunity before him. Thus his Strategy for the situation was to instead of resisting the movement, he would work inside it, impose order on it, organize it, and eventually guide it toward an outcome that ended on his terms.
* Organizing the Revolt
Richelieu helped plan and organize the revolt because the coalition had almost no unity.
* He relied on trusted agents, including men such as Sébastien Bouthillier and Michel de Marillac, who constantly carried information, instructions, and negotiations between Paris and Angers. Richelieu wanted to understand the entire political situation for himself. For that reason, he built his own network instead of depending on others. This network allowed him both to gather intelligence and to negotiate with different members of the coalition in an effort to hold them together.
Even then, this was extremely difficult, since many of the participants were ambitious, selfish, and interested mainly in their own advantage.
* Richelieu also helped finance the rebellion. He seized royal revenues, collected taxes, raised loans, and worked to fund the army. By July 1620, these efforts had produced more than 2.3 million livres and, at least on paper, an army of over 50,000 men. This was a remarkable feat of Strategy logistics.

   * Did Richelieu Want the Revolt to Succeed?
The answer is no, at least not in the sense of Marie decisively defeating the king. This may seem strange, but there is strong evidence for it, and it helps explain what is meant by saying that Richelieu tried to shape the direction of the revolt. Blanchard gives a good example, with Richelieu’s response to Henri de Rohan on a military topic. At one point, Rohan suggested that Marie withdraw south of the Loire, where she would be safer militarily. Richelieu opposed this idea. Such a move might have strengthened Marie’s defensive position, but it also risked turning the conflict into something far more dangerous: a rival government, a wider rebellion, or even a broader Protestant war. That was not Richelieu’s goal. He preferred to keep Marie at Angers. His aim was not for Marie to overthrow or defeat her son, nor for the king to crush her completely. Instead, he wanted to keep the conflict limited. Marie had to remain strong enough to force negotiations, but not so strong that the revolt became uncontrollable. In other words, Richelieu wanted the crisis to end through negotiation, not destruction.
Burckhardt later explains that remaining at Angers also had strategic value because it helped make the king appear to be the aggressor.
   * Richelieu’s “Alibi”
While taking part in these efforts, Richelieu also made sure to protect himself in case the revolt failed. Publicly, he presented himself as a man of peace who favored reconciliation and cooperated with mediators. His letters and official behavior reinforced this image, and even at the start of the revolt, Richelieu argued that he warned Marie, and he did not push her into rebellion. Privately, however, he encouraged resistance and helped keep Marie’s cause alive. This created a kind of political alibi. No matter what happened, Richelieu could claim that he had been working for peace.
This duplicity became especially clear when the king sent Blainville as an envoy to Angers. Richelieu received him with every sign of goodwill and gave the impression that an agreement was close. Negotiations appeared to move forward, but they were repeatedly slowed down by carefully constructed difficulties and excuses. In reality, Richelieu was buying time.
   * Turning the Conflict to His Advantage
As the crisis developed, the royal army advanced first into Normandy and then toward the Loire. This made the crown appear increasingly aggressive, while the queen mother’s party, despite its disorganization, continued to hold its position. When the revolt began to weaken and Marie’s situation became more fragile, Richelieu did not panic. A total victory for either side would have been dangerous. Instead, he seems to have calculated that Luynes, facing growing hostility and instability, would eventually look for a negotiated settlement.
He was correct, and the result would follow with the Treaty of Angers.
Richelieu guided Marie toward accepting a settlement while allowing the clergy to take the visible lead in negotiations. He remained active behind the scenes, shaping the outcome without making himself too exposed. The final agreement was favorable to Marie de’ Medici. She received amnesty, her followers were pardoned, and the treaty presented her less as a rebel than as a wronged political figure whose grievances had to be addressed.

Personal Insights on Richelieu's 1620 revolt Strategy
Let me give my personal insights on this, because I think I’ve finally understood the situation more clearly after revisiting several sources on this episode. Why would he support a revolt while seeming indifferent to its success, and why conceal himself while still participating in it? In this sense, his strategy was not to win the conflict outright, but to ensure that its very existence reshaped the political balance in his favor, weakening Luynes while strengthening his own position.

Richelieu’s Political Program for France
In Richelieu’s Political Testament, he recalls a moment in which he spoke directly to Louis XIII about the condition of France. This must have been around the time Richelieu entered the king’s council. Hildesheimer notes this, although she does not fully determine the exact moment it refers to. Still, it seems reasonable to place it somewhere around this time.
In this conversation, Richelieu had presented Louis XIII with his analysis of the kingdom’s problems.
   1. First, he argues that the Huguenots were not merely a religious minority. In his view, they had become almost a state within the state. They possessed fortified towns, political assemblies, military structures, and special privileges. Because of this, Richelieu concluded that they were dangerously close to independence from royal authority.
   2. Second, he observes a similar problem among the nobles. Many nobles had accumulated too much power and behaved as if they ruled their own private kingdoms. Royal authority had become decentralized, and powerful governors and aristocrats acted less like subjects of the king than semi-independent rulers.
Richelieu therefore links the Huguenot problem with the noble problem: if one group could challenge the king successfully, others might imitate it. Disorder could become contagious, spreading through the kingdom until every powerful faction claimed the right to resist authority.
   3. Third, Richelieu observes a deeper moral and political corruption. People no longer measured their worth by loyalty, but by boldness. The more daring, aggressive, and rebellious someone was, the more important he imagined himself to be. Thus instead of valuing the favors, subjects constantly demanded more. And so the people who plotted the most appeared to prosper the most.
   4. Fourth, Richelieu turns from domestic disorder to foreign policy. He argues that France’s foreign alliances had been neglected because ministers preferred their own interests. Richelieu believed France needed an active foreign policy, especially to resist Habsburg power in Spain and Austria. In his view, France’s reputation abroad had declined, and the dignity of the monarchy had been so degraded.
From this, Richelieu moves to the need for reform. Even if cautious or intelligent men claimed that reform was too dangerous, he argues that action was necessary. He also implies that he was willing to accept the risks personally. If his policies failed, Louis XIII could always blame him, since princes often placed responsibility on their ministers when events went badly.
Finally, despite the dangers, Richelieu promises that the state can be restored. But he does not present himself as saying, “I alone will save France.” Instead, he attributes success to the king’s prudence, courage, strength, and to God’s blessing. Richelieu presents himself as the king’s instrument, carefully avoiding the appearance that he is overshadowing or controlling the monarch.
He then lays out his famous program.
   1. First, he wants to destroy the Huguenot political party. This does not necessarily mean destroying Protestant belief itself, but eliminating the Huguenots as an armed political body capable of challenging the monarchy.
   2. Second, he wants to lower the pride of the nobles. This means forcing the aristocracy to obey the king and ending their habits of rebellion.
   3. Third, he wants to bring all subjects back to their duty.
   4. Fourth, he wants to restore France’s reputation abroad. France must once again become respected and feared internationally through active diplomacy, stronger alliances, and a foreign policy worthy of a great monarchy.
Therefore, Richelieu’s program is both domestic and foreign.
Note: However, we must be careful when reading this passage. It should not automatically be treated as proof that Richelieu possessed a fully developed master plan from the beginning. What we can say with more certainty is that Richelieu presents a set of major aims and thus his later maneuvers can then be understood as attempts to pursue these aims.

The Anglo-French Marriage Alliance
Context: Since La Vieuville was gone, Richelieu was left to handle the question of the Anglo-French marriage, an alliance he strongly wished to secure. The marriage had originally been proposed by Henry IV before his death. And he actually intended to arrange it for his son, Louis, as a means of creating an alliance with England and establishing a counterweight to the Habsburg powers.
Richelieu now pursued this older objective and took an active role in the negotiations.
The alliance had two principal objectives for him:
1. Creating a counterweight to Spain
The first objective, which Henry IV had also pursued, was to create a counterweight to Spain and prevent the formation of an Anglo-Spanish alliance.
What is particularly interesting, however, is that Richelieu understood precisely why an alliance with England would benefit Spain. Spain was still locked in war with the Dutch Republic, whose military strength depended partly on English and Scottish soldiers. An Anglo-Spanish alliance might therefore allow Spain to pressure England into withdrawing those troops or preventing further recruitment, thereby weakening the Dutch war effort.
2. Weakening the French Huguenots
The second objective was closely associated with his own ambitions. He recognized that the marriage could weaken the French Huguenots by making England less willing to support them once it had entered into an alliance with France.
   * The Planning:
Richelieu nevertheless had to plan the marriage carefully. He had to prevent two major problems.
   1. First, he could not allow Henrietta Maria to convert to Protestantism, which would have appeared to France as both a religious and political betrayal.
   2. Second, he had to arrange the marriage in a way that did not appear scandalous. The marriage of a Catholic princess to a Protestant prince was deeply controversial and potentially unacceptable to many Catholics.
Richelieu therefore sought to resolve the problem by demanding guarantees for Henrietta Maria’s Catholic faith and, if possible, some relief for English Catholics. Burckhardt would thus mention that in England, Henrietta Maria would be permitted to maintain Catholic ladies-in-waiting, a bishop or confessor, theologians, and a private Catholic chapel. These conditions allowed the marriage to be presented not as a betrayal of Catholicism, but as a diplomatic opening through which Catholic influence might be preserved or even expanded in England.
Note: It is also important to emphasize Richelieu’s ability to make the alliance politically acceptable to several different factions. To the king and his ministers, the marriage could be presented as an anti-Spanish security measure. To those concerned about the Huguenots, it could be presented as a means of separating England from French Protestant rebellion. To Catholic skeptics, it could be defended as a way to protect a Catholic princess and perhaps improve the condition of English Catholics. To the papacy, it could be presented as a marriage surrounded by extensive Catholic safeguards.

First Instance of Indirect Warfare Against Spain
Context: We now turn to the Thirty Years’ War. Maximilian of Bavaria had seized the Protestant Palatinate, while Tilly and Wallenstein strengthened the Catholic-Habsburg position in Germany. At the same time, France and Spain competed over northern Italy and the Alpine passes, where Spain sought to connect Milan with Austria. Richelieu therefore saw Germany and Italy as two linked fronts in the struggle against Habsburg power.
Since France still faced internal problems, such as powerful nobles who could rebel, the Huguenots, and financial and military weaknesses, it was not yet prepared to begin an open war. However, Richelieu still believed that France had to do something. Thus he pursued an indirect war against Spain. And this would be the first instance of an indirect war policy, which Richelieu would repeatedly use in the future.
In this case, he employed a series of tactics aimed at weakening Spain by isolating it from the Holy Roman Empire, while simultaneously weakening the latter:
   1. Richelieu supported Mansfeld, a Protestant commander in the Thirty Years’ War who fought against the Catholic forces. Mansfeld was useful to Richelieu because he could keep the emperor occupied in Germany. France therefore promised him money and troops.
   2. Richelieu also sent diplomats to Denmark. He knew that the Danish king was worried because Tilly, the Catholic general serving Bavaria and the emperor, was becoming increasingly powerful in northern Germany. If Tilly advanced too far north, Denmark itself could be threatened. Richelieu saw an opportunity. By encouraging Denmark to intervene, France could help open another front against the imperial Catholic forces.
   3. The Anglo-French marriage alliance, which I  have discussed earlier, was also connected to this wider strategy. Its purpose within this policy was to keep England away from an alliance with Spain, thereby isolating Spain and weakening Habsburg influence.
   4. Richelieu also sought closer relations with Bavaria. Although Bavaria was firmly Catholic, it was also determined to preserve its independence from Emperor Ferdinand II. Richelieu did not hesitate to take advantage of this rivalry, since it helped prevent the emperor from consolidating his power within the Holy Roman Empire. He therefore explored the possibility of separating Bavaria from the emperor.
   5. Later one Richelieu would renew the old alliance France used to have with the Dutch Republic, after Spain had begun making gains in the Low Countries (Ambrogio Spinola, was besieging Breda), thus Richelieu responded by renewing. THE HUGENOT REBELION:

During his conversation with Louis, on the topic of his Political Program, Richelieu would mention that as long as the Huguenots in France are a State within the State, the King cannot be master within his realm, or achieve great things outside it. The Edict of Nantes had granted them far more than freedom of worship; they commanded fortified cities like La Rochelle, fielded their own armies, and maintained independent political assemblies, functioning in practice as a separate power embedded within France. Richelieu found this arrangement unacceptable. A minority holding its own military and governing structures could resist the crown, court foreign powers, permanently obstruct his grand strategies and the construction of a unified state. Each fortress they occupied was land beyond royal reach. Each assembly they held was a competing centre of authority. Each foreign contact they cultivated was a conflict waiting to be lit from the inside. Thus it was “A State within a State.” Richelieu’s aim therefore was not to destroy the Huguenots, it was to destroy the independence that made them ungovernable, and make them dependent.

Why Did No Foreign Power Strike France During the Huguenot Rebellion (Besides England)?
It's worth stepping back and asking why no other major power moved against France while it was preoccupied with the Huguenot rebellion. The answer, when examined carefully, is actually quite straightforward, every country in Europe simply calculated that weakening France was against their own interests. France was, after all, one of the very few powers capable of standing up to Spanish dominance on the continent. Allowing it to collapse would simply hand Spain an even greater advantage, and that was a prospect most European states found deeply unappealing. This explains why, when Buckingham attempted to assemble an anti-French coalition, he found almost no takers. The Venetians, for instance, not only refused to join but openly celebrated when the siege succeeded. Countries like Savoy and Lorraine did participate to a degree, but when Richelieu began turning the tide, he made clear enough of a threat toward them that they quietly backed down. Spain's involvement was interesting though, despite having some concealed role in Buckingham's coalition, financing the Huguenots and dragging its feet on aid to France, it never moved openly against Richelieu, largely because he had manipulated Spanish pride well enough to keep them publicly aligned with France. And the Dutch had been bribed by Richelieu with the Treaty of Compiègne (1624), before the siege began. Was all this just luck? Not really. It all lies in the contrast between Buckingham and Richelieu as strategic thinkers. Buckingham simply failed to grasp how Europe as a whole was reading the situation. Richelieu, on the other hand, understood it completely, and was very cautious at first, he knew about the risk of the conflict going international, and he actually held back at the start, pursuing negotiation even when the Huguenots were at their most vulnerable. But once the siege began, Richelieu spread the message across European courts, that any intervention against France would ultimately serve Spain. Since fear of Spanish was the defining anxiety of the era, the argument landed. It kept potential enemies on the sidelines, isolated England, and left Richelieu free.

Subduing the Last Remnants of the Huguenot Rebellion
After the first phase of the War of the Mantuan Succession, Richelieu redirected his efforts toward suppressing the last traces of the Huguenot rebellions. By this point most Protestant resistance had already been broken, but Henri de Rohan continued to oppose the crown. He started guerilla warfare in the Cévennes mountains. Since Rohan’s forces were elusive and difficult to capture directly, Richelieu planned a way to force his surrender; he sought to force his submission through psychological warfare.
Instead of chasing the rebel leader through the mountains, Richelieu targeted the Protestant cities that supported him.
To accomplish this, Richelieu devised a coordinated military plan. The Huguenot strongholds stretched across southern France in a chain of fortified cities that supported and communicated with one another. Rather than attempting to defeat them all simultaneously, Richelieu aimed to destroy their unity. Working together with the king, Louis XIII, and Prince de Condé, Richelieu planned to cut the communications linking the principal Protestant centers, especially Nîmes, Montauban, and Castres. Once these cities were isolated from one another, none could come to the aid of the others. Each town could then be attacked and subdued individually.
As the campaign progressed, city after city fell to the royal armies. In some cases the repression was severe. Certain commanders, particularly Condé, carried out brutal reprisals, including executions of sections of the population. The steady destruction of these Protestant strongholds gradually eroded the foundations of Rohan’s resistance.
Eventually the mounting devastation left Rohan with little choice but to surrender.

The campaign goes as he forecasted: Lorraine is defeated and forced to submit through the Treaty of Vic in 1632. Gaston has to escape again, this time to Brussels, where he resumes his anti-Richelieu propaganda campaign, now with Spanish backing.

Gaston would start yet another revolt, this time alongside Montmorency, but the uprising was so poorly organized that it collapsed almost immediately. Richelieu hardly needed to intervene, so the details are scarcely worth dwelling on.
The Execution of Montmorency
What matters more is what Richelieu did before and after the revolt.
Montmorency himself was a remarkably courageous noble, driven by a desire to restore unity within the royal family, and he entered the rebellion with this idea. Yet the effort was doomed from the start; its disorganization made failure almost inevitable, something Richelieu clearly foresaw.
Richelieu even warned Montmorency in advance, Burckhardt argues this was done to justify the severe measures he would later take once the rebellion had failed, since when Montmorency was captured, there was widespread sympathy for him, even from the king, given his status as a leading noble.
Richelieu, however, carefully positioned himself as moderate while subtly directing events toward execution. He did this by reframing the issue. Rather than presenting it as the punishment of an individual, he cast it as a matter of state survival. Richelieu understood Louis XIII perfectly and appealed to his deepest concerns: fear of weakened royal authority, fear of ambitious nobles exploiting disorder, and fear of instability, especially in times of illness. He reduced the situation to a stark necessity, either Montmorency must die, or royal authority would lose all meaning.
During the trial, Richelieu would write memos designed to damage Montmorency's reputation.

WAR AND DEATH:

After consolidating power, things went smoothly for Richelieu for a time. Gustavus's death conveniently removes the worry of Sweden growing too powerful. He then receives intelligence that Wallenstein might be willing to turn against the Emperor if France offered its support. Meanwhile, France begins occupying Lorraine after the Parlement of Paris provides legal cover for the move, and Richelieu sets about constructing a defensive buffer zone in the east. French influence expands further as princes in Alsace accept French protection, drawing them deeper into his orbit within the Holy Roman Empire. But then the situation turned upside down immediately. The Habsburgs have Wallenstein killed, and their forces go on to win the Battle of Nördlingen, a stronghold the Protestants had been counting on holding. The victory hands the Habsburgs control over southern Germany, and the Protestant alliance structure begins to crack. The Heilbronn League and similar coalitions start falling apart, with some member states openly considering a separate peace with the Habsburgs. Now, if the Dutch were to make peace with Spain, Madrid could redirect its armies squarely against France. Richelieu now faces a stark choice between two deeply uncomfortable options: continue with indirect support, which is clearly no longer working, or intervene directly in the war, a move that is effective but enormously risky. This is the crossroads at which the Cardinal finds himself.
Spain slowly starts tightening its grip on the situation.
In Brussels, where Gaston and Marie are exiled, the Habsburgs pressure them to swear loyalty, a demand that puts Gaston in an awkward position. Ever unpredictable and  patriotic, he escapes away and returns to France, where he reconciles with Louis. Richelieu, however, is under no illusions about Gaston's reliability. The man had secretly married Marguerite of Lorraine and had written to Pope Urban VIII making clear that any attempt to annul the marriage would be treated as coerced, hardly the behavior of someone to be trusted. Puylaurens, who had come back with Gaston and helped broker the reconciliation, was also suspect, with strong reason to believe he was still quietly maintaining contact with Brussels.
Richelieu and the king moved. They lay a trap, inviting Gaston and Puylaurens to a royal ballet rehearsal, at which point Puylaurens was arrested. Richelieu then moves swiftly to neutralize Gaston himself: seizing control of his household, imprisoning his associates, and replacing them with loyal men. Gaston, characteristically, seemed unbothered, he was back on the dance floor that same night. But whatever he felt about it, he had been effectively defanged.
Blanchard, Éminence: Cardinal Richelieu and the Rise of France, chap. 7, notes 62-69.

When the war starts, rather than fighting the whole Habsburg system at once, he sets his sights on Spain, specifically on the Spanish Netherlands, since the region had been thrown into political instability following the death of Infanta Isabella Clara Eugenia.
France manages to secure a victory at Avein.

Spain strikes back, capturing the Lérins Islands near Cannes, and French forces struggle across the board, failing in particular to sever the critical supply line linking the Spanish Netherlands to Spanish Italy, which had been one of Richelieu's objectives. Encirclement starts to look like a real possibility.
But Richelieu manages to achieve some success and counter this. As the siege of Corbie went well. The town is blockaded and starved into weakness, after which Richelieu orders trenches dug toward the walls, mines laid, and an artillery bombardment launched just as winter is setting in. The combination of cold, exhaustion, and relentless pressure breaks the defenders' will, and they surrender. The timely arrival of Dutch forces at that moment only adds to the pressure on Spain, whose commanders think twice about pressing further.

Richelieu's spy network duly brings this to his attention.
He uses indirect manipulation to deal with this problem.
He secretly enlists a Dominican priest, Father Carré, to nurture La Fayette's already evident religious inclinations toward convent life. Richelieu likely calculated that she was susceptible to this, after all, she was devout by nature and only 16 years old. The Case of Anne of Austria
Anne, who was being carefully spied on by Richelieu, had her letters intercepted by her servants who reported on her. Remember, after the day of the dupes Richelieu had her inner circle infiltrated. Anne had begun secretly corresponding with France's enemies, most notably the Marquis of Mirabel. When Richelieu informed the king, Louis XIII granted him full authority to handle the matter as he saw fit.
What's particularly interesting is why Richelieu didn't simply move to punish Anne the moment he had the letter. Blanchard mentions it vaguely, Anne could plausibly dismiss it as innocent family correspondence, after all, the Cardinal-Infant was her own brother. Without a fuller picture of her communications, Richelieu risked building a case that any competent defense could foil. A failed accusation against the Queen of France wouldn't just be an embarrassment.
His first move was to target La Porte.
One of his spies had identified him not as an ordinary servant, but as the queen's principal courier and trusted go-between for her secret correspondence. Richelieu had La Porte seized, imprisoned, and interrogated, while his home was searched. Investigators uncovered secret codes linked to the queen and a letter addressed to the Duchess of Chevreuse. Despite all of this pointing firmly at La Porte, and though more damning material would later surface, for now he had concealed it effectively, there wasn't enough to formally charge him.
Richelieu therefore shifted strategy: he needed a confession, either from La Porte or from Anne herself.
La Porte held firm under interrogation, refusing to break even when threatened with torture. Attention then turned to Anne.
Anne fully aware that any admission could implicate her in dealings with Spain, she denied everything. Richelieu responded with harsher treatment, and after two days without food, she relented. When Anne finally agreed to meet with Richelieu, she began cautiously, acknowledging she had written to the Cardinal-Infant in the Spanish Netherlands but insisting it was merely an exchange of pleasantries. Richelieu called her bluff, he had the intercepted letter and the evidence recovered from La Porte's rooms, and made clear he already knew there was more. He then offered to speak to the king on her behalf, so that she wouldn't get punished, if she provided him with everything. Anne asked the others to leave. Alone with the cardinal, she finally confessed in full. According to Richelieu's own account, she exclaimed, “How good you must be, Monsieur le Cardinal!” as she laid out the full extent of what she did. Then came a remarkable gesture: she extended her hand toward him,  a striking breach of protocol for a queen who almost never permitted physical contact. Richelieu, with characteristic precision, declined, stepping back rather than accepting. In that single restrained movement, he simultaneously projected deference and asserted dominance.
With the confession secured, Richelieu made a move that might seem surprising:
He advised Louis XIII to reconcile with Anne. But this was entirely strategic. Because a public rupture, divorce or exile, would have undermined royal legitimacy and caused a diplomatic crisis given Anne's Spanish ties. Controlled forgiveness served the interests of the monarchy far better than public punishment.

Yet forgiveness, in Richelieu's hands, meant something very specific, it meant total surveillance, now conducted openly. Anne remained queen, but effectively became a monitored political prisoner. Her letters were screened by an assigned overseer before dispatch, and her isolation deepened. Richelieu now did this publicly, he did not need a network of spies to keep her monitored, as this case served as an excuse for it.
Richelieu did not always destroy his enemies outright, but neutralized them, contained them, and kept them permanently within his sight.

Caussin was a Jesuit confessor loyal to Louis XIII, yet he harbored deep suspicions that Richelieu was manipulating the king. As an outsider looking in, he described the cardinal's manipulation in almost supernatural terms.
"it seemed as if [the cardinal] had emitted towards him some kind of active and penetrating venom, some enchanted fumes that altered his judgment, and made him suddenly like all he previously hated, approve all he condemned, and embrace with trouble of the mind and unnatural passion, everything that the cardinal wanted.”
Caussin urged Louis to arrange a direct confrontation, promising that given the opportunity he could dismantle Richelieu's arguments and expose the manipulation. Louis agreed, but with a telling condition. The meeting had to appear accidental, because he was afraid of provoking his own minister. That fear speaks. A king so wary of upsetting the man who served him was not truly in command at all; he was, in every meaningful sense, under Richelieu's thumb.

Richelieu's Reversal Trap Against Caussin
Richelieu, however, had spies watching Caussin and learned of the plan in advance. Rather than panic, he prepared himself quietly and waited. When Caussin arrived, Richelieu greeted him with warmth, and the two started a philosophical debate about the vanity of worldly affairs, exactly the kind of intellectual arena in which Caussin intended to expose and humiliate him. Caussin, still believing he held the advantage, waited for the king to arrive so he could spring his trap, but what he didn't know was that Richelieu was also waiting.
When Louis finally arrived, Richelieu made his move. He told Caussin to hide himself in a secret passage, claiming the king was suspicious of them both and might grow angry if he found them together.
Note: Louis was indeed somewhat wary of Richelieu during this time, which was precisely why he had agreed to Caussin's plan in the first place. Richelieu had taken a genuine truth and bent it to his own purposes.
Caussin obeyed, and in doing so sealed his own fate. But why? The reason is that he could not protest without revealing that the meeting was premeditated, that the king was expecting him, and that the entire encounter had been orchestrated against Richelieu. His own secrecy had become a weapon turned against him.
Richelieu had achieved total control of the situation.
Alone now with the king, Louis inquired about the confessor. Richelieu replied that he had not seen him. A lie obviously, but then he does 2 things simultaneously:

   2. Attacking Caussin: Then he would attack  Caussin's credibility entirely, suggesting the man's arguments were so weak he had not even dared to show up.
Through this, Richelieu manipulates the king, planting the impression that Caussin may be too weak as Richelieu suggested.

While Richelieu was managing domestic affairs at home, the war against Spain and the Habsburgs continued to grind forward on multiple fronts with mixed results. In Germany, French armies had success at the victory of Rheinfelden, and they pressed on to besiege Breisach, a fortress whose capture would hand Richelieu control of the Rhine, a strategic goal he had wanted. But also there were setbacks, Spain managed to reinforce positions, and Spanish forces struck at Savoy, a French ally/
With this complicated situation in mind, Richelieu turned to an attack on Fuenterrabía, a fortified Spanish town on the border between France and Spain. Whoever controlled Fuenterrabía controlled the Atlantic Gateway, the most direct passage between the two kingdoms, making it both a defensive anchor and a potential launching point for deeper operations into Spain. Richelieu thought ahead to what would follow it.  He reasoned that the moment Fuenterrabía fell, Spain would move immediately to retake it, just as the Spanish had done when the French captured Corbie. He therefore wrote that preparations for the aftermath needed to begin before the siege was even concluded.

When negotiating the marriage between young Condé and his niece Claire-Clémence, Richelieu offered a huge dowry to make it attractive, but quietly excluded her from his will, keeping his fortune out of Condé's hands. There was a quarrel over who had more authority, a prince or a cardinal, a quarrel in which Richelieu and Condé participated. To symbolize Condé' submission; Richelieu would set a trap against him. Condé's journey home would naturally pass through Lyon, where Richelieu's brother, Cardinal Alphonse de Richelieu, lived. Richelieu arranged for Alphonse to prepare an elaborate welcome, refurbishing his palace and organizing a grand reception, so that Condé's expected visit would serve as a public, symbolic act of submission, forcing him to formally acknowledge Alphonse's precedence over him.
