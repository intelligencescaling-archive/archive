// Battles against the Polish Uprising //
- Battle of Orekhovo:
In the Battle of Orekhovo, Suvorov, with only 450 men and two cannons, left part of his detachment in Brest and headed towards the Pulavskis. These Confederates were grouped in a clearing within a forest, making them vulnerable. Suvorov observed this disposition and saw it as a strategic weakness, so he decided to leave his detachment in Brest, despite opposition from others. Suvorov and his troops crossed three gorges in the forest to confuse the enemy about their positions.

Upon emerging from the gorges, they attacked the Confederates. To further demoralize the enemy, Suvorov ordered the village behind them to be set on fire with a grenade, causing chaos and panic in the enemy ranks. Suvorov repelled the Confederates' attacks with grapeshot. Seeing that the enemy was disheartened, he ordered a bayonet charge against the cavalry.
Why did Suvorov order a bayonet charge against cavalry?
Attacking cavalry with infantry was an unusual and surprising tactic, which Suvorov knew could cause confusion and panic among the enemy ranks, as they would not be prepared for such a bold maneuver. Suvorov had great confidence in the discipline and courage of his troops, knowing they could maintain formation and execute an effective bayonet charge, even against mounted forces. This tactic also aimed to avoid prolonging a confrontation that could wear out his limited forces. Attacking with infantry and using the bayonet charge allowed him to maximize his resources, an incredible "Efficiency Strategy."
Furthermore, Suvorov understood the psychological impact of combat. A bayonet charge, executed with determination and energy, could devastate the morale of the enemy cavalry, which might not be ready to face such aggressive infantry.
Conclusion: The Confederates fled in disarray, pursued by Suvorov's infantry, which maintained rapid fire to affect the enemy's morale. During the battle, a Russian soldier attacked Casimir Pulavski. His brother Francis came to his aid and saved him, but lost his life in the process, resulting in the death of a Confederate leader. The battle concluded with a decisive victory for Suvorov, solidifying his reputation as a brilliant strategist and military leader.

(Observation, Misdirection, Strategic Efficiency, Psychological Warfare: Demoralization)
Although defeating the Confederates, Suvorov treated prisoners with humanity, releasing them on their word of honor not to fight again. He understood that treating repentant rebels well would serve his interests better than shedding blood. (Relationship Management, Social Intuition)

After the previous battle, Suvorov received orders to move towards Krakow to face the main forces of the Polish Confederates, led by Dumouriez and Valevski. The left flank of the Confederates was protected by the stronghold of Lantskorona, while the center and right flank were defended by a steep, vegetated hill, giving them an almost invulnerable advantage.
Emotional Understanding: Suvorov likely understood that the enemy expected him to fight like other Russian generals, calmly, slowly, and with a "armchair" strategy. The Polish infantry did not anticipate the Cossacks reaching the top of the hill and attacking so swiftly. This caused panic among the Polish soldiers, who began to flee.

The Polish infantry crumbled and fled in disorder, unable to withstand the surprise attack of the Cossacks, thus losing the battle.
In his memoirs, Dumouriez heavily criticized Suvorov's action of attacking a strong position with cavalry, describing such an operation as contrary to all tactical rules and attributing Suvorov's success to mere good luck. Mediocrity criticizing genius! - Battle of Kinburn
Before the battle, it's important to understand Suvorov's reasoning. First, let's consider the context:
Everything happened in Crimea, where the military general Potemkin had already begun clashes with the Turks, who had initiated the first attacks. The Turks attacked a Russian frigate, angering Potemkin, who sent ships in retaliation. However, due to a storm, these ships were diverted and lost, demoralizing Potemkin as the Turks were about to disembark, posing a major problem. The main issue was that Potemkin didn't know where the Turks might land. Under this stress, he suggested to Empress Catherine that the best option would be to retreat from Crimea, but the Empress rejected this idea.
Despite the situation, Suvorov remained calm. Though he didn't have as many resources as Potemkin, he decided to stay composed and analyze the situation carefully.
Suvorov understood that the Turks wouldn't just land anywhere, but would seek a strategic point that would provide a base for future operations and directly threaten Russian positions in Crimea. At this moment, Suvorov put himself in the Turks' shoes. He analyzed the possible options and concluded that Kherson - Kinburn was a likely target due to its strategic importance. Furthermore, another reason Suvorov deduced Kinburn would be the landing site is that it offered an advantageous position for advancing. Suvorov accelerated the construction of defenses at Kinburn, including artillery batteries and fortifications, which would be crucial in repelling any landing attempt. He strategically positioned his troops on the promontory to cover potential attack routes, ensuring they could respond quickly to any enemy movement.

This fact was confirmed, and Suvorov's reasoning proved correct when the Russian fleet later approached that strategic point. However, the Turkish fleet remained inactive for a while, sailing near the Kinburn fortress without attacking. Suvorov was not surprised by this, deducing they would eventually return to port due to the approaching storm season.
This second deduction also proved accurate, as on October 1st, the Turkish fleet began to bombard and disembark. The Turkish attack began on that day.

(Deductive Reasoning, Inductive Reasoning, Associative Thinking)
The battle begins when the Turks initiate their landing, simultaneously bombarding the Russian fortifications with their naval artillery.
Upon realizing this, Suvorov doesn't panic or become alarmed. Instead, he remains calm and takes no immediate action. It seems as though he doesn't care. In fact, with a smile, he informs his army that it's a holiday and suggests everyone attend mass. This attitude leads many to believe that Suvorov has lost his mind, accusing him of being crazy, possibly due to the accumulated stress from Russia's recent failures. However, what no one knows is that this apparent indifference is actually a brilliant strategy. This act is the first step in Suvorov's master plan.
Why did Suvorov take no action?
1. The first reason Suvorov took no action was because he wanted all enemy troops to fully disembark so he could attack them with greater force and better concentrate his forces. Remember that concentration of forces is one of the greatest strategies a general can employ.

2. The second reason can be found in this part of the text: "Additionally, the tip of the promontory was within easy range of the Turkish naval cannons, but as the Turks approached the fortress, they would have to relinquish this advantage." The explanation is that the tip of the promontory is within easy reach of Turkish naval cannons, but as they approach the fortress, the Turks will lose this advantage.
Why do they lose their advantage?
As the Turks approach the fortress, natural obstacles such as hills, promontories, or vegetation may block direct line of sight from the ships to the advanced troop positions. The fortress structures and other built defenses can also obstruct direct fire from the ships. To effectively use their cannons, the ships need to maintain a certain distance. If the Turks get too close to the fortress, the ships will have to change positions or cease firing to avoid harming their own troops. Additionally, the firing angles become less effective as targets move beyond certain points, especially if the terrain elevates the fortress above the line of direct fire. As Turkish troops advance, there is an increased risk that naval cannon fire may accidentally hit their own forces instead of Russian defenses. This risk increases if Turkish troops are too close to the fortress, which could lead commanders to cease fire to avoid friendly casualties. Moving the ships closer to shore to maintain range can also expose the fleet to counterattacks.

How can Suvorov be so convinced that the Turks won't realize they're being manipulated by him and won't stay on the coast waiting for him to come down, as a tactical response to avoid losing advantages in battle?
Suvorov infers this due to his understanding of the Pasha's psychology. He knows that the Pasha will order the ships to move away from the coast to prevent his soldiers from considering retreat and thus fight with greater determination. Suvorov is aware of this strategy of the Pasha and uses it to his advantage, which is why he is so sure.

   3. Another reason Suvorov takes no action is because he is deceiving the enemy. In reality, he is preparing an ambush. He wants the Turks to feel confident and believe they have a total advantage over him. However, the Turks don't realize they're falling into his trap. This is also reflected in Suvorov's deception cover, who, besides taking no action, attends mass during holidays as a way to conceal his true intentions. But this is just a facade. He wants the Turks to think he's not acting because mass and the holiday are more important.

- Battle of Rymnik:
After a battle called the Battle of Focșani, where Suvorov had a minor role assisting the Austrians against the Turks, the Turks were defeated and the victors celebrated. However, in August, the Turks recovered from their defeat at Focșani and planned a major offensive. They waited for the Russians and Austrians to separate, intending to first defeat the Austrians and then attack the Russian forces.
Turkish Offensive:
Using a diversionary maneuver, the Turks managed to deceive Potemkin, who commanded the Russian army, into concentrating his main forces in Ismail. Meanwhile, a large Turkish army under the Grand Vizier gathered near the village of Rymnik, ready to attack. Austrian spies observed this and immediately informed the Austrian commander, Prince Coburg, who then requested assistance from Suvorov.
Upon hearing this information, Suvorov analyzed the situation and found it peculiar, questioning why the Turks did not attack immediately. Nevertheless, he decided to move towards the Austrians to offer his help.
Upon arrival, Prince Coburg was relieved and explained the situation to Suvorov. However, Suvorov arrived and asserted without hesitation that they must attack immediately. Though this seemed unbelievable, Suvorov understood something the prince did not.
Suvorov's Reasoning - Before reaching the Austrians, Suvorov noticed something unusual: the Turks were not attacking. According to Suvorov, this inaction indicated that the Turks had not yet fully concentrated their forces. To confirm his theory, Suvorov rode to the Rymna River, climbed a tall tree, and observed the Turkish positions. He saw they were well protected by ravines, forests, and the river, indicating they were still positioning and concentrating their forces, thus validating his reasoning.
For this reason, Suvorov insisted on an immediate attack, before the Turks could fully gather their strength. He argued that only a swift and surprising attack could offer any chance of success.

(Inductive Reasoning, Critical Thinking)
Suvorov's Attack:
Suvorov meticulously plans his offensive. His strategy involves initially attacking a flank of the Turkish forces while the Austrians advance slowly towards the Turkish center to distract them. Suvorov aims to surprise this flank, planning to execute everything during the night to ensure the Turks do not realize the attack until it's too late.

Suvorov executes his plan successfully, distracting the Turks with the Austrians' advance and completely destroying the Turkish flank, forcing them to retreat.
Planning and Versatility:
Original Plan: Suvorov's initial plan was, once the flank was eliminated, to proceed to the center to assist in the attack.
However, Suvorov notices an issue.
Versatility: He observes that the main Turkish positions are defended by strong enemy batteries located in the village of Bogcha, making a direct frontal attack difficult. Suvorov identifies Bogcha as key to weakening the Turkish defenses, believing that taking this village would facilitate a more effective attack on the main Turkish positions. Instead of attacking the center directly, he decides to capture Bogcha first to eliminate the threat of the enemy batteries positioned there. Suvorov heads to this location and employs a "Concentric Attack" tactic, essentially attacking a target from multiple angles. After securing Bogcha, Suvorov and Coburg were able to launch a coordinated attack towards the center of the Turkish positions.

Attack Towards the Center:
With this "Executed Protection Strategy," the only remaining task was to attack the center. However, Suvorov noticed another problem.
He observed that the final layer of Turkish defense consisted of a shallow trench and earth embankments that were not fully finished and were of little height.
Suvorov made an unexpected and bold decision: to attack the Turkish trenches with cavalry. This type of attack was uncommon, as trenches were typically defended by infantry. It was something not foreseen in any military theory of the time.

Following his instructions, reinforced Austrian troops and Russian forces formed a concave front. The first lines of infantry opened at intervals, allowing cavalry units to position themselves between them, while the rest of the cavalry deployed on the flanks. Under intense Turkish fire, the attackers approached within 600-800 yards of the enemy fortifications. At that moment, the cavalry burst from the intervals along the entire line and charged at full gallop against the enemy. In a matter of seconds, the riders crossed the space under fire, leaped over the trench and parapet, and penetrated the ranks of the Janissaries. Surprised by such an unexpected attack, the Turkish defenders were disoriented, allowing the attacking infantry to intervene almost unhindered. A fierce close combat ensued. However, the Russians and Austrians easily dominated the battle. It was a total massacre; the Turks attempted to flee. The Turkish military commander, the Grand Vizier, tried to stop them with the Quran in hand, but the panic was so great that it was futile. Suvorov achieved victory that day.

- Assault on Ismail:
Suvorov arrived at the siege of the fortress of Ismail with an army of only 30,000 men, most of whom were untrained Cossacks in infantry tactics, and their resources were very limited. Despite the daunting situation, Suvorov immediately began preparations for the assault, demonstrating his determination and ingenuity.
Siege Training:
Suvorov meticulously trained his men for the assault by ordering the construction of replica ramps similar to those at Ismail. Troops practiced assaulting these every night, following a strict regimen that included:
   * Approaching and filling the moat with fascines (bundles of sticks used in fortifications).
   * Crossing the moat.
   * Installing and securing assault ladders.
   * Scaling the ramps and destroying the palisades (defensive fences).

The Strategy:
Suvorov's strategy was to conduct violent assaults, but it was not just a typical attack plan. His approach was designed to penetrate the weakest sections of Ismail's defenses while demonstrating strategic resilience.
Here's why this strategy was brilliant:
Suvorov's Reasoning and Observations:
Since his arrival at Ismail, Suvorov tirelessly devoted himself to reconnoitering the terrain and studying the fortifications. Initially, the Turks shot at him, but eventually, they deemed him harmless and ceased wasting ammunition on his patrols. Comparing his observations with reports from his scouts, Suvorov identified that the weakest section of the fortifications was the one facing the Danube. This was due to three reasons: 1) the Turks did not expect an attack from that direction, 2) the defenses on that side were not formidable, and 3) it was only protected by the navy, which Suvorov remembered was weakened after the Battle of Tendra led by Admiral Fyodor Ushakov.

Strategic Resilience:

This attack also showed remarkable strategic resilience. To understand this, it is crucial to grasp that the nature of the assault and the plan itself involved surrounding Ismail and attacking from all directions. However, this was not Suvorov's true objective. As explained in "Reasoning and Observations," the main attack would take place on the Danube. Yet, Suvorov did not disclose this to anyone, not even his own generals. There are two reasons why Suvorov kept this secret:
He did not want the Turks to defend the Danube with more troops.
He did not want his soldiers to feel useless and merely a distraction, which would divert the Turks' attention towards them instead of the true objective.
This move is ingenious because by keeping his strategy hidden, it becomes a strategy of resilience. Why?
Adversity against the strategy: Although Suvorov had previously prepared for the Turks to think it was a normal siege and not an assault, ordering the construction of two batteries, this tactic failed. Ultimatum to Ismail
Two days before the council of war meeting, Suvorov sent Ismail an official demand for surrender, adding a personal note:
"To the Seraskier, Elders, and all the community. I have arrived here with my troops. Twenty-four hours to consider at your leisure; after my first shot, no more leisure; my assault — your death. Please reflect on this."
Aidos Mehmet Pasha responded with an evasive request for a ten-day armistice; one of his subordinates arrogantly declared to the envoy that the Danube would cease to flow before Ismail surrendered. Suvorov expected nothing less and did not respond to the armistice request, setting December 11th as the assault date. (Psychological Warfare: Abusing Insecurities)
The Assault:
The night before the attack, Suvorov toured the front, encouraging his troops and reminding them of their past glories. At 3 a.m. on December 11th, the first rocket signal was launched and troops took their positions. At 5:30 a.m., with the third signal, the assault began. Ismail was surrounded and attacked from all fronts, with the Danube being the most crucial. The outer walls fell by 8 a.m., shifting the combat into the city. The streets of Ismail turned into battlefields, with every house defended like a fortress. Russian artillery opened fire along the streets to facilitate infantry advancement. The Turkish commander's mistakes, including inadequate use of cavalry and ineffective defense planning, facilitated Russian progress. By 11 a.m., the battle's outcome was decided. Russian troops, having breached defenses at multiple points, converged towards the city center, encircling the Turks. Turks, including men and women, fought with fierce determination, defending each position with scimitars and daggers. As the day progressed, Turkish resistance began to crumble. By nightfall, all resistance was finally broken. (Unpredictability, Tactics)
