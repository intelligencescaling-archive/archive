The Man Who Sold The Eiffel Tower

Count Victor Lustig is an obvious alias. Lustig had gone by at least 45 recorded aliases throughout his career, but for the document’s sake we’ll refer to him as Victor Lustig. (Concealment)

Despite his proclaimed status later, Lustig was born into poverty, therefore; he nor his family was never given the title of Count ever. (Fabrication)

Victor was born into poverty, and had an abusive father. Records indicate he was beaten unmercifully with a bullwhip, with frequent thrashings by his abusive father. He was a very free-willed child, but mentally strong as well. At the age of 7, he was walking 3 miles every day to and from his school. Although Victor always appeared strong to his classmates, he was sick a great deal. He managed to keep this securely hidden from everyone around him. Migraines continued to plague him, his hands mysteriously swelled up in the summer months, and, on a trip to Prague, he fell and broke his collarbone. Nonetheless, he hated to miss even a day of school, where he had a perfect attendance record. (Stress Tolerance, Concealment)

Victor was a very innovative child. For instance, he produced elaborate plans for a flying bicycle to his little brother and best friends. When Victor was 12, the school took a weeks’ break due to an Easter holiday, so Victor realized that anything he did during this time wouldn’t affect his perfect attendance record. Thus, he decided to go on an escapade to Paris. For reference, he is currently in Hostinné, a town in the Czech Republic. At the age of 12, Victor had managed to travel over 700 miles (a 10 hour distance) to Paris. It’s impossible that he could have gotten there on foot, and taking into consideration his family was not financially well-off, the only way he could have realistically gotten there is by hitching rides and convincing strangers to drive him. Accounts show that he convinced the head of a house of prostitution to let him rest during his stay in Paris. Victor had planned his escapade so perfectly that by the time he reached Paris and explored the city, just about a week would have passed. So he made himself easily accessible to the police so that they would return him to his father and that he wouldn’t have to hitch rides back, and thus make it back just in time for school’s re-opening, meaning he wouldn’t break his perfect attendance record. (Creativity, Divergent Thinking, Persuasion, Prediction, Strategy Success Rate)

In boarding school, Victor was considered by his teachers a genius. (Academics)

Whilst hunting for scraps outside a fancy restaurant, a 12 year old Victor observed a couple just arriving. They ordered some food, but before the food came Victor noticed the atmosphere becoming more and more intense as the women made advances towards her lover. The man took out a large stack of bills and put it on the table, leaving with the women just as the waiter had arrived with their food. Seeing the food untouched, Victor resolved that there were people among them who didn’t deserve their wealth, and that it was his purpose to address this. (Perception, Goal-Setting)

At the age of 14, Victor convinced a village girl of his own age to accompany him back to his empty family home, where he had sex with her. (Persuasion)

At the age of 16, Victor took a trip to Vienna in pursuit of finding out what he wanted his career to be. In order to do so, he wanted to quietly observe his surroundings. He walked down to State Savings Bank, a building that appealed to him on both the visual and symbolic levels. He noted his observations of the heavy, barred windows and smooth stone walls. In fact, he spent hours standing in place there, noting his observations through sunbreaks and thunderstorms, noting down everything – the weather, the passing carriages, the ladies’ fashions, a sudden cacophony of striking clocks, the smell of the wet cobblestones – all grouped around his central fascination with the bank itself. Through the above observations, his mind allowed him to broadly sympathise with those he saw as fellow class strugglers against the system. (Observation, Empathy)

Accounts show Victor often confided in his friend, Karl, and shared with him his more intricate thought processes, such as: why didn’t the world treat everyone fairly, why were people such idiots, or why did they waste their lives pandering to the rich and powerful instead of seizing the moment and making something of themselves. (Divergent Thinking)

By the age of 17, Victor was fluent in Czech, German, English, French, and Italian. He was gifted in billiards and chess. (Crystallized Intelligence, VCI)

Lustig had always been a courteous, affable man. He was known to be scrupulously polite, even to his captors. On the night Britain declared war on Germany in August 1914, Lustig was unbotheringly admiring the mist in the night sky. (Charisma, Composure)

Many people viewed Victor in different ways, with two major divides: people who were close to him and people who weren’t. Lustig’s daughter had stated she could not tell the difference between the man whom she said ‘I loved every day of my life, and who loved me tenderly in return’ with the man who for the most part saw others as mere props or statistics rather than individuals. A US Secret Serviceman regarded him as a “genuinely charming fellow, but there was a kind of film between him and the rest of the world. He didn’t have feelings in the way other people do.” Very nearly every man, woman and child he met over the course of thirty years was a potential patsy for one of his schemes. He always seemed to be receptive, or sympathetic. He was popular with women, especially American women, who responded warmly to his polished manner. (Acting Skills, Charisma, Emotional Management)

Victor slowly progressed from shoplifter to pickpocket, to petty thief to card sharp. Already, at 18, Victor was described in the Viennese police files, which noted that his hands were “as swift as the west wind.” One victim wrote of him half-admiringly: "He could make a deck of cards do everything but talk.” (Crystallized Intelligence)

The first time the police encountered Lustig was on January 7th, 1908. He had turned 18 just three days earlier, and the complaint against him said that he had celebrated his birthday by installing himself in a suite of the Nemzeti Hotel and, sporting expensive new tailoring and a waxed moustache, posing as an apparently well-bred young insurance company executive sent on a continental tour by his associates in London. For only a token fee, he announced, he would be glad to take a piece of any potential client’s jewellery, leave behind a receipt, and then have the item professionally appraised by an expert before returning it in the morning. He had walked off in this manner with an expensive necklace and matching earrings, after introducing himself to the owner of the valuables under the alias ‘Earl Mountjoy’ and mentioning that he hoped she might find time later in the spring to join him on his yacht as he cruised the French Riviera. That was the last the lady had seen either of Lustig or her valuables. When the police came for him, Lustig remained calm. He told them his fiancée was his alibi. She didn’t speak Hungarian, though. The police found the fiancée at the suburban address Lustig gave them, and brought her downtown for questioning. Speaking in a colourful mixture of German and Italian, she told them that herself and Lustig had been many miles away exploring the natural beauty of the Baradla caves and the surrounding countryside on Victor’s birthday, making it quite impossible for Lustig to have presented himself that same day in the role of Earl Mountjoy in the hotel. He had never been out of her sight. She corroborated all his statements. Lustig was promptly released from police custody, getting away with the valuables. Lustig had foreseen the confrontation with the police and notified his fiancée on the cover story, ensuring she had it down to a solid. (Fabrication, Strategy Orchestration, Concealment, Strategy Fortification, Composure, Prediction)

Victor had courted multiple women throughout his “bachelor” years. While on honeymoon with his wife, he deceived her by seeing another woman under the name of Baron Eric von Kessler, while keeping this a secret from both. (Concealment)

To avoid getting caught by the authorities, Victor moved back and forth in a rough 300-mile semi-circle, rarely staying in any place for long. And if he did end up getting caught, he would trick the cops into believing he never committed the crime.  (Preclusion, Misdirection)

Lustig was back into custody in February 1909, because the police came calling for him there on the 21st of the month. They wanted to ask him about a complaint they had received from the manager of the Grand Hotel. The manager had hired a carpenter to help remodel several of the hotel’s rooms. But the workman he hired had done some questionable things. The workman had fashioned an invisible sliding door in one bedroom wall, hidden behind a desk. A guest had innocently laid out his valuables on that same desk before sleeping, and in the morning they had disappeared. Lustig told the police that they could question him all they wanted, but that they should also speak to his fiancèe, whom he particularly stressed was an ‘honest young lady.’ She would confirm that he had never offered his services anywhere as a carpenter, and what was more could vouch for his movements on both the day and the night in question. He denied having ever set foot in the Grand Hotel, and the manager there was unable to positively identify him. Following that, an exquisitely dressed young woman had presented herself for an interview. She confirmed all Lustig’s statements, and he was released without charge. The police report concluded that, ‘There is no stain on his character.’ Of course, the perpetrator was none other than Victor, who had run a variation of one of his earlier scams. Victor’s alibi of a year earlier had been slim and dark-haired, but the lady he introduced here was noted in the police report as ‘statuesque and blonde.’ Therefore, Victor must have courted a new woman, never keeping just one person purely in the know about his schemes to avoid capture. (Identity Deception, Creativity, Logical Manipulation, Concealment)

At the age of 20, Lustig courted a young African American woman who worked in theater. He went under the alias ‘Baron von Kessler,’ and after a few weeks of dating promised he would marry her. He just needed her to steal a few clothes and wigs from her place, saying he has a professional need for disguises. After getting the disguises, Lustig promptly ghosted her with the items he conned. (Concealment, Seduction)

In the same year, the police took him in for questioning: once for impersonation and another for the offence of ‘selling a bushel of finest Bintje potatoes knowing them to be poor imitations from Poland,’ raised by a witness. On both occasions, they didn’t have enough evidence he was involved so he was released. (Concealment)

Around the same time, a famous story was circulated that Lustig had seduced and danced with a young lady who was to be married to another man. (Seduction)

The FBI’s file on Victor was the following: ‘This is a quiet, responsive man. He expresses no feelings of hostility toward society … Overall, he shows a fair degree of persistence and continuity.’ (Concealment, Misdirection)

By the age of 21, Lustig had already honed several professional skills that would serve him as an adult. He had a plausible manner, particularly with women. He was a fluent storyteller, not hesitant to embellish to make a point or gain an advantage. He preferred to work with his mind instead of his hands, although he wasn’t above presenting himself in taxing physical roles like that of a human torch if it served his needs. He generally seemed to have plenty of money to lavish on travel and hotels, but no one really knew how much money he had or how he had acquired it. He bought a Parisian suit, and periodically grew a moustache to look older. (Charisma, Fabrication, Willingness To Act, Concealment).

Lustig had read books including Pierre Janet’s Symptoms of Hysteria, Wilhelm Wundt’s Hypnotism and Suggestion and Sigmund Freud’s Psychopathology of Everyday Life. (Knowledge)

While he was 21, Victor found himself celebrating the Easter holiday of 1911 with a well-heeled young woman in the Grand Hotel located in Budapest. He first took the time to shower her with gifts, including an ‘antique Ottoman marble inkwell’ before he made his play. He was temporarily low on funds, he told her. There had been some irritating technical delay in one bank communicating with another. He didn’t mind for himself, of course, but there was the matter of the refuge for underprivileged children that it had been his honour to sponsor, and as he proceeded to describe the place and its poor, malnourished inmates with their hungry eyes and simple, trusting smiles the woman had started to cry. Lustig told her that he was certain it would be all right, and that the necessary wire transfer would surely arrive at any hour. His companion stopped crying for a moment, but seemed unconvinced, and started sobbing loudly again. In between her tears she told him that she would like to help. Victor went to some pains to tell her that she should not do so, but the more he protested the more determined she became. He accepted the loan only with the greatest show of reluctance, along with his promise to return to the hotel in an hour or two. That was the last she ever saw of him. (Flattery, Fabrication, Emotional Manipulation, Emotional Understanding, Acting Skills)

Since Victor traveled a lot, anytime he showed up in a new country he put his criminal status as “None.” Every time he was apprehended by the police or forced to show up in court for any charges he may have been pressed for, this would therefore give him an easier sentence if he were to be convicted (about 14 days for a minor offence). During his time period (1900s) the courts took long periods of time to communicate to each other internationally, which Victor took full advantage of. (Fabrication, Logical Thinking)

Lustig decided, around this time, to enter the gold-and-white lobby of London’s Carlton Hotel and announced himself as Count Franz Honiok of Hesse, visiting Britain on some ‘small currency-exchange matter’ involving the Bank of England. In the days that followed, he had said that nine months earlier he had been one of those on board the RMS Titanic when she sank in the icy waters of the North Atlantic, and had survived the ordeal only after sliding headlong down the ship’s deck into the sea, where, surrounded by the drowned and drowning, he had remained for four hours before the occupants of a passing lifeboat pulled him to safety. He was very modest about it all. Lustig’s life story included an account of youthful adventures fleeing from tomahawk-wielding Lakota Indians in Wyoming, and helping suppress the Asir Rebellion on the Arabian Peninsula. Lustig kept a card index of fellow guests at the hotel, whom he studied for any possible discrepancies between their public personas and private conduct. The celebrated American light opera composer might have an unhealthy interest in auditioning local choirboys for the chorus of his latest touring production, for instance, or the wealthy City financier with an attractive young lady at a corner table in the restaurant had a wife and six children waiting for him at home in Esher. Once Lustig had successfully applied the data to his own advantage, he tore up the card. He was always extremely generous when it came to tipping chambermaids and waiters. They were among his most trusted informants. Several of the staff formed up in a receiving line for him, bowing and curtseying, when he finally checked out of the hotel. (Concealment, Fabrication, Info Gathering, Aura)

Victor had a knack for spotting and communicating with some of the most influential people in history before they were influential. He befriended a dishwasher in a hotel named Nguyen Sinh Cung. The two men were the same age, and they seem to have struck up a friendship in their short time together, because Victor gave him not only a pound note but also a sliver of wood held in a small glass phial that he pulled with some ceremony from a chain around his neck. It was taken from the wreckage of the Titanic, he claimed, and hoped it would bring him luck. That man later became Ho Chi Minh, the former president of North Vietnam. He later around the same time encountered a  fellow drifter in a Vienna cafe. This individual was described as a ‘deathly pale, skinny adolescent with a rough voice’. Just eight months older than Lustig, with whom he shared a broadly similar family background and frustrated artistic ambitions, he apparently dominated their conversation, speaking heatedly about society and politics ‘not avoiding vulgarisms, in a very impetuous way.’ Lustig let him talk and express his own ideals. The young man gave his name as Adolf Hitler, the notorious dictator of Germany. Victor was a man who was able to see the inner psyche of someone with just one interaction. (Influence Building, Fabrication, Emotional Perception)

Lustig, adopting the alias Count Otiok, persuaded a young woman whom he met one Friday evening at the Hotel de Crillon, that he happened to have just received a large inheritance and wished to distribute this to various local children’s charities. But he was a foreigner from Berlin and there were administrative problems with transferring the funds. The young woman assured him that she had no such inhibitions. In fact she would be glad to help him. And she did, giving Lustig ample cash to satisfy his charitable efforts until the banks opened for business the following Monday morning. Over her protests, he insisted on leaving the woman an exquisite gold watch as security against the loan. The funds then changed hands, and Lustig, adopting the job of a distinguished professor of molecular diffusion, told his friend that he would return immediately after delivering a brief Saturday-lunchtime lecture at the nearby college. She waited for many hours before calling at the society’s offices. They had never heard of a Count Otiok, and after some investigation the watch was found to be made of a then little-known material called stainless steel, with a thin veneer of gold paint. The woman told the police that, the central deceit aside, Lustig had treated her with ‘the utmost courtesy.’ (Concealment, Emotional Manipulation, Fabrication, Charisma)

During Lustig’s time, he lived through two World Wars. You might be wondering if he served any military experience. The simple answer is: Anytime someone came knocking on his door, he would produce a certificate supposedly signed by the adjutant-general of the central army recruitment office in Prague. ‘Unfit for combatant and auxiliary duties on grounds of severe nasal occlusion,’ it read. ‘Unable to bear arms.’ This allowed him to carry out his schemes in peace. (Forgery)

Lustig started to carry a diary with him to note all of his observations and views of people, while also using it to gather valuable information. (Observation, Info Gathering)

An unmarried Vienna schoolteacher named Sylvia Nenni immediately took a liking to Victor when they met in the spring of 1914, charmed by his ‘unspoiled nature’ and ‘quiet demeanour’, as well as by his obvious affinity for her. Often, after they had had tea or gone dancing together, she remembered, ‘He would make a great fuss of me as he described his hopes and dreams for the future, and how I had brought the first gleam of real light to his life.’ Lustig had grown up with no money, not much love, and little in the way of guidance, she believed. As a result, he had the air of a ‘lost child whom you wanted to clasp and foster.’ All of these feelings, however, were an act by Lustig, who had the ability to shape people’s perception of himself. (Charisma, Flattery, Acting Skills, Emotional Engagement)

Victor was described to be able to turn on an easygoing, cosmopolitan charm when it suited him. Often slovenly as a younger man, in recent years he had smartened up, leaving an image of a ‘vigorous and elegant gentleman’ in the mind of the manager of the Carlton Hotel and also those of his staff. Like many dishonest people, he had the gift of inspiring confidence. Sylvia soon tearfully agreed to marry him after Victor had led her to believe he was suffering from a terminal illness. She had immediately given him the funds with which to consult a specialist in Paris, praying that there might yet be hope for their future together. That was the end of their relationship, as Lustig swiftly took the funds and fled. (Charisma, Aura, Social Engineering, Emotional Manipulation)

Oftentimes, Victor liked to practice his deception on others to make sure he still had it. Although he already had a valid excuse to not participate in the war effort, he was always looking for more innovation in his craft. During the British's war with Germany, Victor wore ragged clothes and went to a London donation center. He presented himself as Otiok and reached into the pocket of his shabby jacket and produced a diamond necklace. He had extracted the gems with his own hands from a mine in which he had an interest in German South West Africa, he told the astonished official. It had been a hard struggle to successfully export them when he subsequently emigrated to Europe. Crocodiles were wrestled, terrible thirst endured, battles fought with murderous business rivals. Lustig had finally arrived in Paris, where the jewels were set into a necklace. Victor had kept the piece locked safely in his apartment while he went about town trying to establish himself as a painter. After several weeks he had been lucky enough to win a small commission to design the murals on a government building. He decided to relocate across the Channel and dedicate himself to the British cause. Regrettably, his health was indifferent and thus he would not himself be able to bear arms. But he hoped this small token of his regard for his adopted country might nonetheless be acceptable. The official at the front desk thanked him for his loyalty and gladly accepted the gem, pardoning him from any war efforts. (Creativity, Fabrication)

Having mastered his con skills, Victor looked about for a field in which to apply them. He noticed wealthy international people had taken to ship travel on fleets of glittering new vessels. The jaded and rich millionaires on board the ships, Victor reasoned, would make perfect targets of his deception. (Observation, Logical Reasoning)

Victor’s primary endeavour for doing so was the poker table. Sometimes he worked alone, perfecting his skills as a ‘mechanic’ who could palm, switch or steal cards from the table. Sometimes he teamed with an older passenger named Julius Arnstein in a so-called ‘squeeze play,’ goading an innocent third party to keep raising the stakes until the cash was heaped up on the table, at which point one of the two would triumphantly sweep up the entire pot. Throughout the game, Lustig and Arnstein had worked out a system of signals and codes beforehand to ensure they had everything under control. He would obviously need to memorize and process every hand signal from Arnstein (which couldn’t be up for longer than a few seconds to avoid suspicion) to cheat their way to victory. (Methods, Coercion, Creativity, WMI, PSI)

At the age of 24 and as a foreshadowing of his Eiffel Tower heist, Victor successfully sold Tower Bridge (a famous bridge in London) to a wealthy Australian tourist for $2,931 (about $76,210 today), telling the tourist that he was the chief treasurer of the London County Council, the competent local government body for the city, and that they needed the funds for emergency civil defence measures. He disappeared like a ghost once the funds were deposited. (Fabrication)

In 1915, a new ship called the Brittianic was being put to the test in numerous ‘sea trials,’ (basically just making sure the ship was ready to start taking large numbers of passengers) so they invited wealthy rich men to board their test run. The dangerousness of being on board a ship during its trial was precarious, but this actually further incentivized the men to go, to thrill seek in a way. Lustig saw and took advantage of  their gullibility or greed; but above all appealed to their sense of the precariousness of life. As there was a possibility they could be killed on the ship, they were willing to be more reckless and wasteful with their expenses. Lustig would never push his victim to the card table. He always let them suggest the game. That way they would feel in control. They would feel the need to gamble, and he would simply give him what they desire. Victor serenely wrote in his diary that he had gotten $17,000 from gambling on the same day the London press reported the news that 60,000 British soldiers had been killed or wounded. (Emotional Understanding, Indirect Manipulation, Composure)

One morning, Lustig observed a group of German soldiers getting out of a truck. They had all been wounded in the fighting and given a week’s medical leave to stay in a nearby lodge on the banks of the Elbe. Lustig wrote in his journal that the men had a ‘distant stare’ to their eyes, sunken cheeks, seemed ‘visibly weakened’ both by injury and malnutrition, and ‘hobbled off, some in boots still caked in battlefield mud, others [on] bandaged feet leaning on a comrade’s arm’. He added: ‘It is the more pitiful because they are only boys drafted from nice homes, knowing nothing about the conflict or the great issues at stake in London or Berlin or Paris. They just don’t see what it is all about.’ (Observation, Profiling)

Lustig next posed as a musical producer who sought investment in his latest production. The production in question was either an acknowledged hit, which required only a small further injection of capital – or other times it existed solely in Victor’s imagination. The target would be invited to dinner and had a drink, and sooner or later the conversation would turn to the theatre and Victor would mention what very little capital he needed left to ensure that the chorus line of young ladies would continue to entertain the public in the times ahead, and how the girls themselves would be personally grateful to their patron, and at that point the target would acknowledge that he had always been keen on the stage, a detail Lustig had known from the start. He presented himself as ‘André Dupre’, a displaced Parisian musical manager, at a series of meetings in the newly opened Ritz Hotel. A respectable specialist named Cyril McLintock had stated that ‘Monsieur Dupre offered me a quarter-share in his new West End enterprise for only £500 ($18,431). It was literally a steal, as no such revue existed.’ According to Dr. McLintock, ‘It seems easier to pretend to be what you are not than to conceal what you are. The person with whom I enjoyed those splendid dinners could accomplish both. He was a consummate manipulator.’ (Disguise, Fabrication, Emotional Understanding, Info Gathering, Concealment, Aura)

Around the age of 25, Lustig decided to travel to the ever-bubbling population of New York. He displayed a hardworking ethic during his first months in New York. He took correspondence courses to improve his English during the day, and devoted every free moment to concocting new ways to extract money from Americans. One of Victor’s minor swindles was sex workers. Upon leaving the premises, he would show a $50 bill which he would apparently tuck into their upper pocket, telling them that if they looked before the next day it would turn into tissue paper. The girls were naturally unable to stop themselves, and would look the moment Lustig left. They found tissue paper and, according to Lustig, would only have themselves to blame for it. What Lustig really did was use a sleigh of hand trick to hide the $50 under his sleeve and switch it out with tissue paper right when he put it in the pocket. Sex workers were uneducated, thus; they were easy targets of Lustig’s scam. Whether they checked the money instantly, it would have “turned into tissue paper,” or if they checked the money the next day, Lustig would’ve been long gone. (Academics, Creativity, Fabrication, Exploiting Weaknesses, Tactics)

As Lustig got older, his scams became more intricate. There was his role, for instance, in the phony off-track betting caper. The idea was to lure a wealthy person on to the premises and, once having won his confidence, invite him to wager on the outcome of a nearby horse race. Of course, the entire operation was simply a theatrical set, everyone there besides the wealthy person was an actor, and the ‘broadcasts’ from the track were actually being made up by an announcer in the back room. Lustig introduced himself as ‘Count von Kessler,’ a fellow high-stakes gambler, and his role was to lend an air of European class to the proceedings. Since con men are really actors, he pulled it off with success. A diary entry from 1916 (He was 26) shows that he successfully conned $300 in that scam, $8,890 today. (Coercion, Strategy Fortification, Fabrication)

Victor soon found himself having a talent for forgery. He was able to pass off documents from at least fifty historical figures, including George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, Marie Curie and the flying Wright brothers. Some of these works changed hands for ‘hundreds,’ which would be at the very least $3,000 today. (Crystallized Intelligence)

Victor briefly set up his own small church in Manhattan. He hung a notice on the door announcing the date and time of a special service that would ‘swell all hearts and souls with joy’ and, what was more, guarantee those in attendance an ‘eternal place of rest in God’s acre.’ There was a massive turnout on the day. Lustig recited a speech by Adolf Bertram, the Roman Catholic archbishop of Breslau, in front of his huge audience, translating it from the original German, adding one or two words of motivation, and pretending that he had originated the ideas. It all went over so well that he collected over $90 ($2,400 today) in the offering plate. (Emotional Understanding, Leadership)

All accounts of Victor agree that he already possessed two priceless qualities that helped shape his adult career. He was fiercely ambitious, often rising at two in the morning to walk 3 miles to his first fishing job. And that he was completely composed in a crisis. (Mental Fortitude, Composure)

Around this time, a mysterious series of hijackings took place in and around Wall Street. The major New York banks all employed messengers to transfer large bags of cash from one location to another. In several cases, the person carrying the cash never arrived. Sometimes he would be waylaid and relieved of his goods, while on other occasions both he and his cargo inexplicably vanished. The police could never identify a suspect. The few eyewitnesses spoke of the assaults as being carried out by an individual variously disguised as a rabbi, a priest or even a nun. Over an eighteen-month period from the spring of 1917 to the autumn of 1918 more than $3 million in cash vanished, the equivalent of some $70 million today. There is no direct evidence that Lustig himself was personally tied up in this crime wave. He had an alibi for most of the dates, and no known history of personal violence. On the other hand, he moved in the same circles as both Arnold Rothstein and Nicky Arnstein, who it’s widely believed were the masterminds behind the robberies. And in November 1918, just as the spree of Wall Street thefts came to an end, he moved into an eight-room home on Manhattan’s Upper East Side – a notable step up the social ladder for someone who’d been employed in a fish market just three years earlier. The striking thing about Lustig at this time is that he really appeared to be several different people. There was the hard-working immigrant who, his daughter would insist, was then ‘as straight as a die – he didn’t know any criminals and was never in trouble with the law’. And by contrast there was the unlicensed immigrant who forged documents. Lustig could successfully organize his affairs, and kept the details of his numerous scams in his head. (Concealment, Disguise)

One of Lustig’s swindles following his arrival in America was the ‘pocketbook’ scam. Victor would find himself with a suitably prosperous-looking stranger in a bar, on a train, or even while waiting to cross a busy New York street. Looking down, he would suddenly notice a well-stuffed wallet, which he picked up and showed to the other party. Together they would carefully examine it for any identifying name and address, and, once having found these, Lustig would propose that they walk the short distance to the rightful owner’s house and return his property. The two good Samaritans would politely decline the grateful man’s subsequent offer of a cash reward. But then the wallet’s owner – Lustig’s accomplice – would suggest an alternative formula. He would bet all the money in his wallet on the outcome of a horse race and, were he to win, as he was confident he would, share his good fortune equally with his two benefactors. At that point the victim was asked if he might wish to invest some of his own cash in the wager, thus increasing the expected jackpot, and in most cases they would. From there, the victim, Lustig, and his accomplice would go to a rigged horse race where everyone else were actors, and purposefully ‘lose.’ Lustig and the accomplice would then just take the money from the actors once the victim left the betting grounds. On this note, Lustig later wrote: ‘The successful [con] hinges on desire – what can the [perpetrator] offer the victim that will make him abandon rational thought for the promise of a fantasy? It’s very easy. All you really have to do is to probe and be patient. Sooner or later, the sap will tell you their innermost dreams, [and] then you pull the trigger. Victims don’t ask a lot of questions. They answer a lot of questions.’ (Methods, Logical Manipulation, Lateral Thinking, Emotional Understanding)

A Manhattan doctor’s secretary named Ruth Spivack later wrote that she had been dating Lustig at this time, and that he had been a perfect gentleman. Lustig moved to a modest, three-bedroomed family home located at 1614 W. Berteau Avenue in north Chicago. A widow named Edith Keep, who lived across the street, left behind a memoir in which she wrote that the American press had described Lustig with ‘almost divine powers in his criminal performances … He seem[ed] to do things that could not be explained by the ordinary laws of nature, so brilliant were the tricks and so multifold the jigs he performed.’ At first sight, Edith admitted, she met shuffling up Berteau Avenue one spring morning in 1935 and seemed somewhat disappointed to set this figure against this superhuman ideal. Now aged 45, Lustig had a stubby figure with unhealthy skin, a puffy face, and patches of receding grey hair he revealed when courteously lifting his hat. According to Edith, he was still ‘friendly and approachable, with brilliantly clear eyes that seemed to snap an X-Ray picture of each person to whom he spoke’. Victor possessed such courtly charm and insinuating manners that people were instantly beguiled by him. (Aura, Charisma)
Lustig had plenty with which to occupy that mind. His family life was in some disarray. He was now effectively separated from his wife Susan. His ex-wife Roberta and her new husband were making trouble for him financially. Peter Rubano’s elite team was on his case, and a few regional forces were interested in his activities. Some of his old associates were now in jail, he reflected, and others were already dead. On top of that, Lustig was also certain there would be another world war. What most troubled him wasn’t the prospect of further bloodshed, but the ‘blunders on our side’ that allowed Hitler to grow stronger ‘with each passing day of sloth’ from the West. ‘The British are sure all they have to do is give a tea party for him and he will fall back like a wolf who has had his fill,’ Lustig wrote, adding: ‘I do not believe it.’ (Stress Tolerance, Prediction, Analysis)
Lustig moved freely around the American Midwest, continuing to pull off isolated frauds as he went. Although he obviously did so chiefly for the money, sometimes he seemed to take more pleasure from the clean professional finish of a well-executed scam. In late April, a Chicago banker named Albert S. Snary complained that a ‘very smooth European character’ had stopped him in the street and asked him to sign papers that he understood were a petition to fire the notoriously anti-business mayor of the city, Edward Kelly. He was really signing a loan application made out in favour of a Robert Duval. Snary realised his mistake only after the well-spoken stranger had successfully collected his money from the bank later that same day. His second mistake probably lay in reporting the crime to the police instead of making good the loss from his own pocket. The bank’s directors soon learnt of the matter and wrote to formally dismiss their manager for his ‘gross negligence’ of judgement. They shifted the blame to Snary, who was fired. (Fabrication, Misdirection)
In 1935, Victor Lustig became America’s most wanted fugitive. (Mass/Global Influence)
Thanks to listening in on a few select phone calls and a tip-off from one of Victor’s upset girlfriends, the Secret Service went to Billie Mae Scheible’s red-brick apartment building on 74th Street with suspicion Lustig would be there. On arrival, Johnson recalled: “We [got out] of the car and stood silent … As I paused there, immersed in gloom, a vision floated slowly before my eyes. It was a Chevy car and in the front seat Victor Lustig now sat talking animatedly to the driver. I blinked my eyes and looked again. It was no vision. It was reality. As Johnson and his colleagues watched, Lustig’s car drew up and parked only some 50 yards from where they stood in their forlorn conference. There was a split-second of frozen silence like that of a football crowd reacting to a sudden goal, and then the [agents] were all moving together in a crouching run down the street, their weapons drawn, towards the parked car. When Johnson reached the right front side of the Chevrolet, he sprang upright and pulled open the door. ‘Secret Service!’ he said, seizing Lustig by the collar. ‘Hands in the air.’ Lustig did as he was told, calmly appraising the faces of the men now swarming all around him, until he noticed Peter Rubano. ‘Oh, I remember you,’ he said, as Rubano slapped on the cuffs. (Composure)
