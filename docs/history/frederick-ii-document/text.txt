CHAPTER I
A CONTESTED ORPHAN: THE YOUNG FREDERICK BETWEEN THE POPE AND THE SWABIANS (1194-1207)

Frederick II Hohenstaufen was born on St. Stephen's Day, 1194, in Jesi, central Italy. His father was Henry VI, son of Frederick I Barbarossa and Holy Roman Emperor, and his mother was Constance of Hauteville, daughter of Roger II and Queen of Sicily. Frederick was, in short, the heir to two royal houses and two very important figures, but his childhood was marked by instability and political conflict. When he was just two years old, his father Henry died suddenly at the age of 31, a death that sparked a heated conflict in Germany between those loyal to the House of Hohenstaufen (led by Frederick's uncle, Philip Duke of Swabia), and the supporters of Otto of Brunswick (a candidate close to the papacy). Although some German princes had already elected the child Frederick as the new King of the Romans, his mother, Constance, decided to safeguard her son by keeping him away from the political conflicts in Germany. Just a year later, however, Constance also died, and shortly before doing so she named Frederick King of Sicily and entrusted him to the new Pope Innocent III, who became his guardian and regent of the Kingdom of Sicily.
Innocent, engaged in an ambitious continental-scale political project, did not directly concern himself with the child's education, who indeed remained in Palermo, but entrusted him to the care of Gualtiero of Pagliara, chancellor of the Kingdom of Sicily, and four archbishops of Southern Italy, albeit with the order to be informed weekly about the child's cultural and personal growth. Said growth would have been a reason for wonder and appreciation not only for Innocent, but for many people around all of Europe. The first teacher of Frederick was, at least starting from 1201, Francesco Guglielmo, who probably taught him the basics of grammatics (and thus Latin) and literature.

In 1199 the Sicilian island was invaded by Marcovaldo of Annweiler, lieutenant of Philip of Swabia (Frederick's aforementioned uncle), in order to interrupt the papal regency over the kingdom of Sicily and to remove the guardianship of the child. Two years later Marcovaldo entered in Palermo and found the hiding place of the young king, but when he tried to take him out Frederick he jumped on his men and tried as best he could to stop them, and once he was blocked by force, in protest, he tore his clothes and scratched his face1. A reaction of unique dignity and determination for a child of only 7 years old.

Papal control over the child returned only in 1206, the year in which Gualtiero di Pagliara defeated Marcovaldo's successor and also regained control of Palermo. During the years he remained under Swabian tutelage, however, the child's tutors remained the same, and in addition to the aforementioned Francesco Guglielmo, Gentile di Manoppello and even a Muslim imam alternated. The child's education was therefore excellent, and Palermo's multicultural environment further fostered a multifaceted education for him2. Frederick studied Latin literature, natural sciences and also Arabic authors, like the geographer Ibn’ Ildris.

During his adolescence, the king's education focused as much on studies as on physical training. At twelve, he was already able to handle almost any weapon, preferring the sword and armor. He loved horseback riding and practiced until nightfall. Before bed, he studied military texts and was considered to have the same knowledge as an adult, considered a precocious intellectual and a boy with excellent verbal and oratory skills3. Frederick was also fond of the game of chess.

Given the presence of German soldiers and nobles in Palermo during some of his childhood years, it's possible that he learned German there, and he certainly did so during his trip to Germany between 1212 and 1214. It's also unclear whether his knowledge of French began during childhood (French was widely spoken at the Norman court in Palermo), but he certainly learned it as an adult, given that he read French texts. We have no solid evidence regarding his knowledge of Arabic, since the sources attesting to it are from after his death, although they are also of Arabic origin. It's nevertheless highly probable that Frederick had indeed learned the language, still spoken in some areas of Sicily and useful for diplomatic relationships with muslim diplomats. CHAPTER II
PUER APULIAE: FREDERICK’S RISE IN ITALY AND IN GERMANY (1208-1220)

Upon reaching 14, Frederick would have achieved the independence afforded by his majority, and thus Innocent III would have had to loosen the reins of his guardianship over the king. The Pope, however, intended to keep the young sovereign closely tied to himself and to the power of the Church, and for this very reason, in the summer of 1208 (just as his guardianship was about to expire), he arranged for him a marriage with the Princess of Aragon, Constance of Barcelona, ​​fifteen years his senior. The goal was, first and foremost, to unite two feudal kingdoms of the Church, and, secondly, Innocent hoped that, with said strengthening of Sicilian-Aragonese relations, from a Mediterranean perspective, Frederick's potential claims to the imperial throne would be undermined. One of the Pope's primary political objectives was precisely to avoid uniting the crowns of Germany and Southern Italy.
In August 1209, Frederick and Constance were married, and the king assumed effective government over the kingdom. His wife had brought 500 Spanish knights as a dowry, and the fifteen-year-old Frederick immediately set about organizing their military deployment. His goal was to immediately restore effective control of the kingdom and strengthen his position. For these reasons Gualtiero of Pagliara lost his influence at court, the royal influence election of the archbishop of Palermo was partially repristinated and the archbishop of Bari, Berardo of Castaglia, a close ally of Frederick, was nominated chancellor of the reign. These actions were made in purpose to diminish the papal influence in Sicily and although Innocent III tried to protest, he later accepted them1.

Frederick's intention was to use the five hundred knights to subjugate the royal continental territories (Calabria, Apulia, Basilicata, and Campania), but shortly after the wedding, a malaria epidemic struck the sovereign's camp, and he and his wife were forced to take refuge in the countryside to escape the disease that decimated his army. Thus, the plans were initially shelved, also because bigger problems were coming near.
Otto IV of Brunswick (remember? the papal candidate for the imperial throne) had won the decade-long contest against Philip of Swabia a year earlier after the latter's unrelated assassination. Following his coronation, however, he broke his promises to the papacy and organized an expedition to Italy to subjugate the entire region to the German Empire. After descending into Lombardy, he even attacked the South with the intention of also occupying the Kingdom of Sicily, invading Campania and Calabria. Frederick fortified the defenses of Messina, the Sicilian city overlooking Calabria, but his army would have been difficult to counter the imperial armies. Salvation for the young king came when Innocent III, having lost all possibility of negotiating with Otto IV, excommunicated the emperor and fomented an insurrection among the German princes against him (the very man who had facilitated his rise to power!)
Innocent did not stop there, but promoted Frederick as the new imperial candidate for the throne. Although he feared the possibility that the boy would unite the empire and Sicily in the future, his threat was just in potential and certainly less dangerous than Otto's immediate invasions. The King of France, Philippe Augustus, in turn supported Frederick's candidacy in order to weaken Otto, who was also the nephew and ally of the King of England, John Lackland. Within a year, Frederick was effectively launched into the complex geopolitical chessboard of Europe as a new pawn in the game of Franco-papal interests, and, at least for the moment, he accepted the position, albeit with his indomitable and proud spirit.

Innocent gave him money and the honour he needed, and then he continued his path to North Italy and from there to Germany.

Swabia, the homeland of his house, opened its doors to Frederick, and from there he reached Frankfurt, where he was re-elected King of the Romans and subsequently crowned. Naturally, papal propaganda and support had also played a significant role in ensuring such a rapid and triumphant rise, while from a military standpoint, Philippe Augustus would soon defeat the combined armies of Otto and the English at Bouvines, allying himself with Frederick's Empire.

It was the feast of St. James, patron saint of crusaders, and Frederick announced that he would undertake a crusade to the Holy Land. This announcement was not only linked to the traditional crusading commitment that the Hohenstaufen house had always guaranteed, but also to the desire to confirm the relations and support of Innocent III3. The power of Frederick was still unstable at that moment indeed, not only in Germany, but the full effective control over the South was not achieved either. Two days later he attended a ceremony for Charlemagne, recently sanctified under political pressure from his grandfather Frederick Barbarossa, in order to give public proof of the continuity of the Swabian ideological vision.

In November and December 1215, the Fourth Lateran Council was held, called out and presided over by Innocent III, which confirmed Frederick's title of King of the Romans and declared the beginning of the Fifth Crusade, which in theory was to see Frederick himself as its champion.
The coronation as Roman Emperor was yet delayed because Frederick was still delaying the separation of the Kingdom of Sicily from the Holy Roman Empire.
Fortunately for Frederick, Innocent III died in July 1216. While he, a formidable politician and at the height of his power, remained alive, Frederick had respected the agreements with the Church and had proven relatively meek. The appointment of the new pope Honorius III, however, opened potentially better avenues of opportunities, given that the new bishop of Rome lacked the political depth and firmness of his predecessor.
In April 1220, Frederick, ready to return to Italy, granted the German bishops the document Confoederatio cum principibus ecclesiasticis, which guaranteed them a wide range of privileges (from minting coins to building their own fortifications). These concessions, although they diminished the emperor's direct control over Germany, allowed Frederick to focus his energies on the Kingdom of Sicily, and potentially later on the crusade, in the following years.

In exchange, he promised Honorius III that he would renounce the title of King of Sicily, formally appointing his son Henry as sole ruler. This compromise was obviously based on mere theoretical formalities, since Frederick would naturally continue to govern southern Italy as well. However, the renewal of promises regarding the crusade and submission to Rome, in addition to the assurance that the two thrones would remain separate were sufficient, after four years of mediation, to convince Honorius III4.

In November 1220, Frederick was named Holy Roman Emperor and crowned by the Pope in St. Peter's Basilica. On that occasion, he reaffirmed his intention to launch a crusade for the following year, and also affirmed that he would respect the Church's orders and commit himself to fighting heresies (the great crusade against the Albigenses, launched by Innocent III, was not far behind in the memories).

CHAPTER III
THE IDEAL KING: THE RULE OVER SICILY AND THE FIRST CLASH WITH THE POPE (1220-1227)

After his coronation in Rome, Frederick returned to the Kingdom of Sicily after an eight-year absence, in a much stronger position than the one from which he had departed, still seventeen. Within eight years, he had achieved independence for his kingdom, the crown of King of the Romans, witnessed the fall of Otto, the alliance with France and the papacy, the death of his former guardian, and had even been named emperor.
On December 20, upon arriving in Campania, Frederick promulgated the Assize of Capua, a series of programmatic laws that demonstrated the centralizing and determined intentions of his rule. All castles built after 1189 without royal approval were to be demolished, and all decrees passed during the interregnum of his childhood and his mother's reign were to be reexamined and amended. The aim was to eliminate the privileges that the kingdom's nobility had wrested from royal power during those years of political instability. A few months later, Frederick also promulgated the Assize of Messina, which introduced Roman law, as revised by the University of Bologna, as the kingdom's legislative system. A second part of the document also respected the recommendations of the Fourth Lateran Council and banned blasphemy and gambling, and stipulated that Jews and prostitutes were required to wear distinctive clothing in public. The fact that this second part of the document will not be included in the definitive Constitution of Melfi (1231) suggests that it was intended mainly with the aim of placating papal criticism, which had attacked the promulgation of the Assize of Capua1.

For these reasons, he had always managed to delay his departure, one way or another, for many years.

In 1217, a crusade, the fifth, had indeed set out without Frederick, led by nobles from all over Europe. Due to the shortage of men, caused by the defection of Frederick himself and the King of Hungary, the crusaders had decided on an expedition against Egypt to cut the Islamic supply lines, and despite initial successes, in 1221 they were swept away by the sultan's forces, while they awaited in vain the arrival of Frederick and his army.
Honorius then threatened Frederick with excommunication, but once again, incredibly, he managed to negotiate diplomatically with the Pope and in a direct meeting in 1223 he promised that he would organize a massive expedition in 1225. As a sign of his good intentions, Frederick married Isabella of Jerusalem, daughter of the last Queen of Jerusalem, even though she was only thirteen years old. His wife, Constance, had died in 1222, and little Isabella also died very young, at just sixteen in 1228, soon after giving birth to the couple's son, Conrad. Frederick was then able to appoint himself regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem.

In 1223, however, Frederick's attention was focused on suppressing the revolts in Sicily. The Arab population, already in opposition to the royal government for years, had rebelled, occupied the city of Agrigento, and captured the local bishop. The emperor's response was immediate: one by one, he attacked and destroyed the Islamic strongholds on the island (Jeto, Corleone, Entella, etc.), then marched on Agrigento, besieged it for three months, and, once captured, massacred the leaders of the revolt. Imperial fleets also surrounded the island of Malta to prevent the local Islamic community from aiding the rebels. Islamic resistance, however, was extreme, and the repression of the first outbreak sparked new ones on the island, which were fought for a year and a half, until the spring of 1225. Frederick directly coordinated the military operations against the Saracens, and by the end of the campaign, the Muslim forces were greatly weakened. At that point, Frederick negotiated with them, and instead of exterminating them, as some had wanted, he decided to deport them from the island, promising them the administration of the city of Lucera, in Apulia, where they could live peacefully and continue their religious traditions as long as they remained loyal to the king2. The deportations of Muslims represented the definitive end of the Arab presence on the island. The Islamic community of Lucera would eventually always remain faithful to Frederick and provided significant contingents of archers for the emperor's armies.

In response, alarmed by the requests and already wary of Frederick's centralizing policies, they revived the Lombard League, the coalition of free cities that had defeated Barbarossa in 1176. Frederick attempted to negotiate with them, but both sides essentially distrusted each other and were on a war footing. When Verona, a League city, blocked the German armies led by Prince Henry (Frederick's eldest son) from entering Italy, and the League itself refused to attend a summons from the emperor, war seemed imminent. Frederick, however, wishing to avoid a long and destabilizing conflict in the North, therefore asked Honorius III to mediate between the two sides. The Pope, after months of negotiations, finally managed to reach a compromise: the emperor was to be more lenient with the communes, and they worked to secure 400 knights for the crusade. Honorius died around that same time, in 1227, and Gregory IX, nephew of Innocent III, was elected in his place, a pope who would prove surely more hostile to Frederick than his predecessor.

Excommunication not only prevented one from receiving the sacraments, but also released all subjects from the obligation of obedience to the sovereign, effectively undermining the foundations of royal power. CHAPTER IV
GOD’S CHOSEN EMPEROR: THE SIXTH CRUSADE AND THE UNIVERSAL POWER (1228-1239)

Frederick hoped that success in the Holy Land would lead to the lifting of his excommunication and thus a reduction in the political crisis caused by his absence1. His intentions for the crusade, however, were not limited to the liberation of Jerusalem; he also had strategic objectives in the Middle East. Frederick, in fact, landed in Cyprus before Palestine. The island was under the control of the regent John of Ibelin, given the minority of King Henry I, and the emperor's intention was to subject it to his feudal control, as his father Henry VI had also planned to do. Negotiations with John of Ibelin, whose support could also be important for the crusade (given that he owned significant assets in Syria and Palestine), led to six weeks of mediation. At the end of these, Frederick reached a compromise: the kingdom would recognize the emperor's feudal rule, would hand over two hundred knights to the crusading army, and John would guarantee his support for the crusade; in exchange, Frederick promised to reduce the taxation imposed on the island2. Having achieved this success, in October 1228 the crusader army landed at Acre, de facto capital of the Kingdom of Jerusalem.
(Planning: Formulation/Aim/Process-Strategy: Logistics/Building-Social Engineering: Communication/Persuasion)
Frederick's crusading commitment had not softened papal opposition at all: Gregory IX ordered the Patriarch of Jerusalem to promulgate the excommunication in Palestine as well, and ordered the monastic-military orders (Templars, Hospitallers, etc.) not to lend aid to Frederick. Only the Teutonic Knights, the monastic-military corps of exclusively German ethnicity, decided to disobey papal orders and remain at the side of the crusading army.
In any case, the emperor arrived in Palestine with an optimistic outlook. He considered an armed conflict against the Muslims a needless weakening of his forces and an excessive expenditure of energy that would have prolonged his stay overseas. For this very reason, diplomatic negotiations with the Sultan of Egypt, al-Malik al-Kamil, Saladin's nephew, had begun even before the fleet's departure. Frederick and al-Kamil already enjoyed excellent relations, facilitated by the proximity of their Mediterranean dominions (southern Sicily and Tunisia were much closer than the former to the northern provinces of the German Empire) and by the like-minded personalities of the sovereigns, both undoubtedly different in mentality from many of their contemporaries. Al-Kamil had in fact attempted to pursue diplomatic channels to prevent the aforementioned Fifth Crusade, without success. Before the fleet left Brindisi, however, Frederick had received, after several weeks of negotiations, the promise that the sultan would hand over to the emperor the holy places of Christianity, including Jerusalem (with the exception of the Temple Mount), in exchange for support against his rebellious brother in Palestine and Syria. Frederick's influence over al-Malik was also due to the relationships he had built in the territory: not only among Christian forces, but also with Islamic ones, such as the Assassin sect.

Months later, by the time of his arrival in Acre, the situation had changed and become more complex. Al-Malik had already defeated his rebellious brother and was therefore less inclined to grant all the promises he had initially made. Frederick, however, needed rapid success, as the situation in the Kingdom of Sicily was deteriorating: the papal armies had invaded his territories, and the Pope was inciting the nobility to rebel and depose the king.
Negotiations with al-Malik lasted five months, but finally, in February 1229, an agreement was reached, in what was perhaps the greatest diplomatic success of the entire late Middle Ages. Al-Malik pledged to give to Frederick Jerusalem (with the exception of the Temple Mount, where Christians were still permitted access), the cities of Bethlehem, Nazareth, Jaffa, the barony of Tyre in Lebanon, the city of Sidon, and a territory that allowed direct access to Jerusalem from Acre. The sultan also allowed the Christians to fortify all these granted lands, while he promised not to build new castles on his own. A ten-year truce was also proclaimed between the two factions. With the exception of the first, no crusade had ever achieved similar results, not even the third led by the Lionheart3. For Frederick, such a success was the proof that God was on his side once again. Despite the positive outcome of the diplomatic action, both the Pope and the Patriarch of Jerusalem criticized Frederick's peaceful methods and the results obtained, considering them incomplete.

On March 17, Frederick entered Jerusalem triumphantly, and a few days later, on a Sunday, he was crowned King of Jerusalem in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. This did not happen during Mass, as the king was still excommunicated and thus unable to receive the sacraments. Indeed, once he learned of the news, the Pope ordered the Patriarch to place the entire city of Jerusalem under interdict. That same day, however, Frederick sent official letters to the Pope, the King of England, the King of France, the great nobles of his kingdom, and his son Henry (whom, we recall, he had left behind as King of Germany), in which he announced the success of his expedition and the reconquest of the Holy Land, indirectly presenting himself as new champion of Christianity.

THE ANTICHRIST ON EARTH: THE ETERNAL WAR IN ITALY AND THE END OF THE DREAM (1239-1250)

On July 1, 1239, an encyclical by Pope Gregory IX (perhaps written by the energetic Cardinal Raniero Capocci, archenemy of the imperial party) spread terrifying news throughout Europe: the agent of the Antichrist had awakened and was ready to destroy the world. However, he was neither the devil nor an unknown man, but the Roman Emperor, King of Sicily and Jerusalem, Frederick II Hohenstaufen. It must be said that most of Europe's rulers did not respond to the pope's call for war against the emperor, except for the King of Bohemia and the Duke of Bavaria in Germany. The church's apocalyptic propaganda, however, was very successful in Italy.
In response, the emperor began to spread his own propaganda that portrayed himself with Christological traits: in his public letters he compared Jesi (his hometown) to Bethlehem, described himself as a "new David" and associated his empire with the concept of a messianic return.
