Napoleon Bonaparte feat list

This post presents a list of feats attributed to the former Emperor of France Napoleon I.

He crossed the Alps via the Great St. Bernard Pass with the Army of the Reserve—40,000 men—to launch a surprise attack on Austrian forces in Italy during the Second Coalition. To do this, he first maintained an active social life in Paris, attending the opera and making public inspections of troops to mask his intention of marching against Austria. Then, he actively encouraged people to spread rumors that he was not fully in charge of the main campaign and that his army would take a less direct, less audacious route, causing Austrian intelligence to underestimate the speed and direction of the French movement. He then crossed the Alps through the Great St. Bernard Pass while transporting artillery by hauling cannons in hollowed-out tree trunks to manage the narrow, slippery paths. He then pushed quickly through the terrain, maneuvering to position his army behind the Austrian supply and communication lines, cutting them off. And surprising the Austrian forces as planned.

In the Battle of Austerlitz he intentionally placed fewer troops on his right flank near the village of Telnitz and Sokolnitz, making it appear weak to the Russo-Austrian army so they would leave the high ground of the Pratzen Heights to attack it. Once the army moved off the heights, as anticipated he launched a massive assault on their exposed center, taking the Pratzen Heights and splitting the Russo-Austrian army in two, securing a decisive victory.

After his Egyptian Campaign that left his army diseased and isolated, he engineered a propaganda campaign to frame it as a success by founding newspapers in Cairo, such as the *Courrier de l’Égypte*, and *La Décade égyptienne* having them specifically designed to glorify his military triumphs and highlight the intellectual, nature of the expedition, downplaying the actual military failure. Then sent those excerpts to Paris publishing the newspapers in the mainstream press, ensuring the French public both civilians and scholars read a better version of events, suppressing the impact of the defeat.

He would eat with his soldiers, talk with them at camp, dress in the traditional attire of his imperial guard, and sometimes get involved in the battles themselves, such as loading cannons at the Lodi against the Austrians. Allowing his troops to loot the towns and villages of conquered territories as a means of compensation for their poor pay during the Italian campaign. And handing out rapid, on-the-spot promotions that made even the lowest-ranked men believe they could rise to the rank of marshal. This created extreme loyalty in the soldiers.

During the Ulm Campaign, he commanded the *Grande Armée*, which was organised into seven corps totalling over 200,000 men, keeping track of their positions and strengths. For example when one of his subordinate commanders could not locate their own division amidst the fast-paced manoeuvres, he informed the officer of their unit's exact location, where they would be for the next three nights, the status and resume of the unit's strength, and the subordinate’s military record.

At age sixteen, he completed a two-year course at the École Militaire in just one year, becoming a second lieutenant in the La Fère artillery regiment. Before this, he had already spent five years at Brienne-le-Château. This means that by the age of sixteen, he had accumulated six years of military education. And with these six years alone, he was experienced enough to conquer Austria and Prussia.

During the 13 Vendémiaire incident, he ordered Joachim Murat to secure forty cannons from the Sablons plain and hide them in close-range positions around the Tuileries Palace and the Church of Saint Roch. He then had those cannons loaded with grapeshot, recognising that grapeshot would be capable of swiftly breaking and dispersing a formation. This way, when the royalist insurrectionists attacked, he could stop them quickly due to the close-range positions of the cannons and the shotgun effect of the grapeshot.

He divided his army into independent units called Corps, each with its own infantry, cavalry, and artillery. This allowed them to march separately and move faster than enemy armies. He would prevent his enemies from replicating this newfound advantage by using his Corps and placing them between the two enemy forces to prevent them from uniting. He also had his Corps use speed-focused logistics by getting them to procure food locally, allowing for even faster movement.
