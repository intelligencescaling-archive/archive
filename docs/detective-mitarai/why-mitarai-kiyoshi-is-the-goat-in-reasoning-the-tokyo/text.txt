Why is he THE GOAT in reasoning?

I want to talk about **Mitarai Kiyoshi** in *The Tokyo Zodiac Murders*, specifically how he thinks, because I don't see it discussed much. I wrote out Mitarai's reasoning as numbered steps to see whether it actually held together or just felt smart. It ran past a thousand steps. What I took from it wasn't a pile of clever tricks so much as a way of thinking, and I think that's what separates him from most fictional detectives.

Light spoilers only. I won't explain how any of the puzzles get solved or say anything about the ending.

Start with what most detectives get. A clue the narrator skipped, a witness who turns up in chapter 30, a confession, or a hunch the book rewards. In all of those the ending is a delivery. Mitarai gets none of it. Everything he uses was in the police file for forty-odd years, and you have the same file. So the only thing left to judge is the **quality** of his reasoning.

The first habit is that he doesn't treat a fact as a fact until he knows where it came from. Most of us take the setup of a mystery as given and reason forward from it. He reasons about the setup first. Who told us this, and did anyone ever confirm it? A surprising amount of what looks impossible depends on the answer.

The second is that he judges theories by what they would require, not by whether they fit. Almost any theory can be made to fit the evidence, so fit is cheap. He asks what the world would have to contain for the theory to be true, in people, time, tools and leftover traces, and checks whether it actually does. That includes quantity. If a theory predicts a certain amount of something and the world offers a different amount, that mismatch is information, even when the thing itself exists.

Third, he treats details as decisions. Instead of asking what a detail means, he asks who it helps. A detail that helps nobody probably wasn't designed, and one that's suspiciously convenient probably was. Most detectives sort deliberate from accidental by gut feel. He has a way to do it on purpose.

Fourth, he sorts variables into fixed and free. Work out what was already forced by other constraints, and whatever's left is where someone had a real choice, and choices carry intent. Then he tests his guess against data that already existed. Nothing new gets collected. He just lines up records nobody had put next to each other. He treats the file as under-analyzed rather than exhausted, and I think that's the most useful habit in the whole thing.

Fifth, he reads testimony in two layers, what the witness saw and what the witness concluded from it, and he trusts the first much more than the second. People can be completely honest and completely wrong at the same time, and he builds on that.

The one that really separates him is what he does when things stall. At one point every piece of evidence agrees with every other piece. Most detectives would say they need more clues. He says the agreement is the problem. Investigation works by finding two facts that contradict, so a case with no internal contradictions can't be cracked from the inside, and honest analysis will reliably walk you to the wrong answer. That reframes forty years of failure from "nobody's figured it out yet" to "this approach was never going to work." I can't think of another detective who reasons about the limits of detecting itself.

It's also fair play in a way that's rare. You're holding exactly what he's holding, and the book even challenges you to solve it before he does. So try it. Imagine you have that file and nobody's helping you. No sidekick asking the right question, no character who happens to mention the key detail, no confirmation when you're on the right track. Where would you start? Probably with the zodiac, the numbers and the map, because the case practically begs you to, and forty years of professionals and obsessed amateurs did exactly that. None of his individual steps is hard once someone shows you. The hard part is that nothing tells you which step to take. I don't think I'd have gotten past the first layer, and that gap between having every fact and knowing what to do with them is what I mean by quality of reasoning.

The obvious objection is that Mitarai is never under pressure. Nobody's about to die, there's no clock, and the case has been cold for forty years, so he gets to think in a way Conan or a cop with a killer loose never does. That's true, and it doesn't change my view. Time pressure is a condition, not a kind of reasoning. Detectives with a deadline mostly get their answers fast because the author lets them. And a cold case has its own problems: the scene is gone, nothing is fresh, and the paperwork was assembled in a way that misleads. His edge is how he thinks and how sound each step is, and that would hold whether he had a week or a year.

He isn't flawless either, and the chain doesn't pretend he is. At one stage it followed a premise the culprit had arranged to be believed and had to back out later. A method that keeps its wrong turns on the record is easier to trust than one that's perfect on the first pass.

If you want to see any of this in action, it's all in the book. Watch how he handles the locked room, the physical evidence found at the suspect's house, the burial depths, and the testimony of the man who thinks he's implicated. I'd rather not spoil the details, and I think they land better if you go in cold.
