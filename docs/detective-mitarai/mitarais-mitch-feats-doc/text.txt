Before He Walks In
Two men are dead in a house that leans, at the northern tip of Hokkaido, both of them in rooms locked from the inside with no way in and no way out. Four officers have been living in the building for five days and have nothing. On the night of 30 December the chief investigator gives up and wires Tokyo. Tokyo sends an astrologer.
Here is everything Mitarai is carrying when the door opens. No badge, no rank, no power of arrest, no power to search, no forensic access. The murder weapon evaporated five days ago and there was never a fingerprint or a length of rope to find. Every person in the house has already been questioned to exhaustion by men who question people for a living. Three of the four officers despise him before they have seen his face. His entire staff is one illustrator who, by his own later assessment, cannot act. Eiko puts him in room 10, where the first man was stabbed, a room with no bath and no toilet.
He is also five days late, which is worth saying plainly, because it is the one limit in the book he cannot argue with. Both victims were dead before the telegram was sent. Nothing he does can be counted as prevention. Whatever he is going to produce has to be produced out of a scene that has already been walked over, photographed, dusted and given up on.
Everything below happens in the ninety six hours after that.
The Board
The house is the thing to understand first, because every deduction in this document is really a deduction about the house.
Kozaburo Hamamoto, sixty eight, president of Hama Diesel, retired to the top of Hokkaido and built the Ice Floe Mansion. The floors slope five degrees. The furniture has had its legs cut down to stand level on them. Guests fall over and the owner finds it funny. Attached to the west end is a glass tower that leans harder than the house does, reached from the main building by a drawbridge staircase that only comes down when the owner lowers it. Around the foot of the tower is a fan shaped flower bed nobody can make sense of. Every room in the west stack has a ventilation hatch above the bed, and the hatches line up vertically. The stairs run in two separate flights, east and west, with a ten centimetre gap left at every corner between the last step and the wall. Room 3 is papered floor to ceiling in tengu masks and its window is kept open thirty centimetres in a Hokkaido winter for ventilation nobody needs. Icicles more than a metre long hang off the eaves.
Every one of those features is load bearing for a murder. None of them looks like anything except a rich eccentric with too much money.
Christmas Eve, 1983. Thirteen people in the building, family, staff and invited guests. On the first night Kazuya Ueda, a chauffeur, is found in room 10 with a knife in him, the door locked, one wrist roped to the bed frame, both arms raised over his head, hips twisted so the legs sit at right angles to the body, and a smear of blood by his left hip. Before that, at five past one, the face of the Golem doll appeared at the third floor window of Kumi Aikura and then vanished. There is no ledge under that window. The doll itself is found in the snow the next morning, taken apart at the joints and laid out in a line, its head buried far away to the west. Two stakes are standing in the snow near the wall and are gone by the time anyone thinks to ask about them. There are no footprints anywhere.
The second night, Eikichi Kikuoka dies in room 14 in the basement with a knife in his back, a white cord on the handle, the door bolted three ways and barricaded from inside with his own sofa and table. At the estimated moment of death the owner of the house is at the top of the tower drinking brandy with the chief investigator, with a record playing, and has arranged for his daughter, his grand nephew and a constable to be at the billiard table all night. Nobody hears anything except the chef, who reports a hissing like a snake sliding, mixed with a woman sobbing, and is not taken seriously.
After five days the police have two locked rooms, no weapon beyond the knives, no prints, no rope, no footprints, no motive for anyone, and a suspect list they have already worked through twice. Hamamoto was struck off it early, because on paper he and Kikuoka were mild business acquaintances who had met fewer than ten times in their lives. Ozaki has settled on the chauffeur as a burglar and Kikuoka as a jealousy killing and cannot make either stand up. Ushikoshi has stopped pretending and sent the telegram.
That is the board. A murder method built into the architecture of a building, run by a man who spent forty years on it, against a detective who arrives with nothing and gets four days.

Sasaki is never discovered, no officer breaks ranks, Hamamoto never does the one thing that would have beaten it, and the blizzard arrives on the right night. He is never shown failing to adapt because he is never made to, so adaptability leaves this book unproven rather than low.
