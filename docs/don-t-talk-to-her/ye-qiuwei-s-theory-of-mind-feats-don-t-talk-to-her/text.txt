Chap 74 – 第七十四章《正向强迫与逆向强迫》

Ye Qiuwei explains obsessive-compulsive disorder. According to the classification she uses, fear is the root cause of the condition, while compulsive behaviors are the mind's attempts to protect itself and relieve that fear. She divides the condition into two types based on how people cope with fear. The first is called positive obsessive-compulsive disorder, in which sufferers try to ease their fear through repetitive behaviors, such as washing their hands to avoid germs. For example, a child may be taught excessively that germs are everywhere and can cause illness, while also being required to follow strict hygiene rules. As the child grows up, they may develop a deep fear of dirty objects and form the habit of washing their hands as a ritual to reassure themselves. Each time they wash their hands, they feel relieved only briefly; then the fear returns, compelling them to perform the ritual again. Even after growing up and understanding that washing their hands excessively is unnecessary, they may still find it difficult to control the behavior, because subconscious fear cannot easily be changed through reason alone. Ye Qiuwei then moves on to the second type: negative obsessive-compulsive disorder. People with this condition do not merely try to reassure themselves; they also want to eliminate the source of their fear completely, hoping that once it no longer exists, their fear will disappear as well. In most cases, this remains at the level of compulsive thoughts, but in extreme cases, those thoughts can turn into dangerous actions.

Ye Qiuwei asks Zhang Yixin whether he has ever experienced thoughts that he considers frightening or unacceptable, such as having sexual relations with someone of the same sex or killing his own child. Zhang Yixin has had fleeting thoughts about having sexual relations with someone of the same sex, despite having no sexual desire toward them; in fact, even thinking about it makes him feel disgusted. He has also repeatedly imagined letting go of his son while holding him and allowing the child to fall to the ground, or pushing him out of a window while the child is playing nearby. However, Zhang Yixin loves his son deeply and has never intended to act on these thoughts. He has always believed that such thoughts come from the evil side of his inner self, so when Ye Qiuwei asks him directly, he immediately denies having them. She realizes that he is not being honest, but also understands that ordinary people find it very difficult to admit to thoughts that make them feel ashamed or afraid. Zhang Yixin eventually says that he once came into contact with someone who had similar thoughts: Zhao Dongmei.

Ye Qiuwei explains that being separated from her child for nearly a year left Zhao Dongmei with a deep-seated fear. When the child was rescued and returned, she not only loved him even more, but also constantly feared that she might lose him again. The child was about two and a half years old at the time, an active and curious age. He might often follow other children to play by the river or watch older children swimming. She may have taught him to stay away from dangerous places, but those warnings could not stop the child from continuing to go to the riverbank. The fact that her son kept going near a place where an accident could happen caused Zhao Dongmei's fear of losing him to grow increasingly intense, until she could no longer bear it. Her mind sought to eliminate this fear through an extreme means. Since teaching her child had proved ineffective and she could not control every danger in the outside world, her subconscious gradually formed the thought that the only way to stop fearing the loss of her child was to remove the child herself from the situation that triggered that fear. Once her son was dead, all she would have left to endure was the pain of losing him, rather than continuing to live with the anxiety that she might lose him again. Ye Qiuwei considers this an example of negative obsessive-compulsive disorder: prolonged fear drives the mind to try to eliminate the very person or thing associated with that fear, leading to an extreme action. Zhao Dongmei did not necessarily kill her child because she hated him; the act arose from a fear that had gone beyond her ability to control.

Compulsive thoughts do not necessarily reflect a person's genuine desires. According to the model she proposes, a person who fears a particular act or fears the consequences it might bring about may experience intrusive thoughts related to precisely the thing they fear. For example, someone who is afraid of having sexual relations with a person of the same sex may develop compulsive thoughts about engaging in that act; someone who loves their child deeply and fears that the child might be hurt may experience thoughts of harming them; and someone who is afraid of falling from a height may suddenly think about jumping. Both positive and negative obsessive-compulsive disorder are attempts to relieve fear; they differ only in the way a person responds to it. However, most people who experience intrusive thoughts do not turn them into actions, and having a frightening thought does not, in itself, mean that the person genuinely wants to act on it.

Usually, people with this condition try to soothe their fear through repetitive thoughts or behaviors. Chap 82 – 第八十二章《解离症与X的抵触》

Zhang Yixin realizes that, in his nightmares, the shadow claiming to be X always takes the form of a man, even though the only person he has been interacting with during this period is Ye Qiuwei. (If the image of X had been suggested by Ye Qiuwei, the figure in his dreams should have borne some of her characteristics.) The fact that his subconscious independently portrays X as a man proves that both Zhang Yixin’s conscious and subconscious minds have known X’s identity for a long time, providing compelling support for the theory that he himself is X.

However, if he really is X, why has he been completely unaware of his own identity all this time? Zhang Yixin thinks of dissociative disorders, a group of conditions involving a separation or disruption in self-awareness and the recognition of one's own identity. He recalls the psychological knowledge he encountered during his university years. Under normal circumstances, the various psychological functions work together as a unified whole, enabling people to maintain a continuous and clear sense of self. However, when a person is subjected to overwhelming psychological pressure or trauma, certain psychological functions may become detached from that whole, causing their self-awareness and self-memory to become fragmented. Milder manifestations may include dissociative amnesia, in which a person forgets a period of time or part of their experiences while retaining a relatively stable sense of self. More severe cases may cause a person to forget their entire identity, no longer recognize who they are, and establish a new life under a different identity. An even more extreme condition involves multiple psychological selves coexisting within one person. Each has its own awareness and behavior, and they may even compete for control of the body. This is the form of identity-related disorder that Zhang Yixin refers to as multiple personality disorder.

Thus, after experiencing a series of abnormal memories, Zhang Yixin arrives at a hypothesis: he may not simply be suffering from memory loss or being influenced by Ye Qiuwei’s suggestions; there may also be a profound split in his perception of his own identity. One part of him still believes that he is Zhang Yixin, a husband and father leading an ordinary life, while the memories he has just recovered suggest that he may actually be X—the person who investigated and killed numerous people through psychological manipulation.

However, if X exists within the same body, why has neither his family nor the people closest to him ever noticed? How could X have operated in secret for so many years without Zhang Yixin knowing anything about it? And why has he only become aware of X now? The moment these questions arise, Ye Qiuwei’s image suddenly appears in his mind. He realizes that his psychological reactions are connected to the suggestions Ye Qiuwei has been employing since their very first meeting. Every detail of her appearance, gestures, words, and even her gaze could have been part of a plan designed to lead Zhang Yixin into contact with X.

He reasons that, throughout the eight consecutive days of their meetings, Ye Qiuwei was the one taking the initiative to arrange them, rather than him seeking her out of his own accord. However, the intuition belonging to X tells him that Zhang Yixin also had his own purpose in meeting Ye Qiuwei, and was not simply someone being led by her. As he tries to uncover that purpose, he notices that X reacts with intense resistance, even attempting to prevent him from accessing his own memories. The stream of memories that had been surging forth begins to slow down. Many fragments grow blurred and then disappear, causing his thinking to become sluggish as well. He realizes that X is trying to withdraw from his consciousness. Unwilling to lose the opportunity to uncover his past, Zhang Yixin steadies himself and follows the memories back to his meeting with Liu Xiangdong.

Chapter 84 – 第八十四章《诱饵》

Zhang Yixin continues to analyze the mechanism behind this fear. The human body must constantly defend itself against invisible dangers, such as bacteria, viruses, and tiny organisms that may enter the body. The skin serves as a protective barrier, while every opening that the body needs has its own defense mechanism. The nose has nasal hairs and mucus to trap foreign particles; the ears have eardrums, earwax, and winding ear canals; the eyes are protected by eyebrows, eyelashes, and eyelids; and the mouth usually remains closed when a person is neither eating, drinking, nor speaking. The excretory and reproductive tracts also have certain protective mechanisms. Harmful organisms generally enter the body through the digestive tract or by penetrating the skin. As a result, humans have developed instinctive responses to eliminate or prevent these organisms from entering. When someone consumes food contaminated with bacteria or parasites, the body may induce vomiting to expel the foreign substances. Through evolution, vomiting became a defensive mechanism, making people prone to nausea when they see or smell excrement, which may contain bacteria and parasite eggs. Similarly, when tiny organisms crawl across the skin, the arrector pili muscles contract, causing the hairs to stand up and the pores to close tightly. This phenomenon is what we call goosebumps. This reaction also serves as a defense against tiny organisms that may enter the body. In modern times, some people who see images containing numerous small holes may associate them with insects or tiny organisms, which can trigger feelings of disgust and fear, as well as goosebumps. He connects this phenomenon to trypophobia—the fear of clusters of small holes.

Based on this mechanism, Zhang Yixin explains Liu Xiangdong’s case. As an introverted and sensitive person who already suffers from obsessive-compulsive disorder, Liu Xiangdong is particularly susceptible to the story about someone whose teeth had been hollowed out from the inside. The image makes him imagine that his own teeth might also be deteriorating without his knowledge. He then pictures his front teeth suddenly crumbling inside his mouth, causing his fear to intensify. Since his front teeth have become closely associated with this fear in his mind, Liu Xiangdong develops a compulsive thought that removing them would allow him to escape the danger. The fear triggered by the story itself may not be particularly severe. However, when combined with the obsessive-compulsive disorder he has suffered from for years, it sets off an increasingly extreme chain of thoughts and behaviors. Eventually, Liu Xiangdong is unable to resist the urge to pull out his own front teeth. Zhang Yixin concludes that Liu Zhipu is directly responsible for triggering this latest attack. The story Liu Zhipu told his father became the means through which the mysterious investigator manipulated Liu Xiangdong’s psychological state.

Zhang Yixin realizes that the newspaper article could exert two powerful layers of psychological suggestion on Liu Xiangdong. On the one hand, it revives memories of the turbulent years when many people committed atrocities against innocent individuals under the pretext of revolution. On the other hand, it evokes a sense of despair by suggesting that those who had committed crimes in the past had never been punished and could continue to harm others. For someone like Liu Xiangdong, who had personally lived through that period, the article would have a far greater psychological impact than it would on an ordinary reader. It not only brings back the fear and humiliation he experienced in the past, but also makes him feel that there is no way to escape these threats. This is precisely the fuse that causes his fear to erupt into obsessive-compulsive thoughts.

Feelings of grievance, jealousy, and hatred have accumulated into a powerful psychological force, lying dormant deep within her instinct and occasionally launching fierce attacks against her ego.

He realizes that Ye Qiuwei may not simply be recounting her psychological experiences; she may also be influencing his own mind. Ye Qiuwei points out that Zhang Yixin has developed a powerful sense of empathy while listening to her story because he, too, has experienced a similar sense of psychological separation, although his is not as complete as hers. Her words leave Zhang Yixin feeling dizzy, and the strange ringing sound begins to echo in his ears once again. Although he recognizes that Ye Qiuwei is using psychological suggestion to attack him, he finds it difficult to resist her influence in his current psychological state.

Chapter 106 – 第一百零六章《粉碎性人格解离与X的诞生》

Ye Qiuwei explains that Zhang Yixin is a man torn between two opposing psychological tendencies. On the one hand, his childhood deprivation of affection and unusual upbringing led him to develop abnormal feelings and desires toward his younger sister. On the other hand, social and moral norms, together with his sense of social responsibility, remain deeply rooted in his mind, forming a relatively intact ego.

When abnormal desires and moral awareness coexist, the human psyche has only two possible outcomes: one side gradually weakens and disappears, or the two become separated from each other. Under ordinary circumstances, desires that conflict with social norms are gradually restrained by external disapproval and criticism, as well as by internal self-control. Many people may have experienced similar desires toward family members, yet continue to live normal lives because those desires are not reciprocated and are eventually suppressed.

Zhang Yixin, however, finds himself in a different situation. Mingxi also loves him. She is lonely, deprived of affection, and desires intimacy with him. Because Mingxi reciprocates his abnormal feelings, the desires that should have been restrained within Zhang Yixin are not only prevented from disappearing but are instead reinforced and given an outlet. When neither the desires nor the moral awareness can be eliminated, the only remaining option for Zhang Yixin’s psyche is to separate them into two distinct parts.

Zhang Yixin has developed a form of psychological inertia toward dissociation. Having previously experienced a separation between his instinct and ego, his psyche is particularly prone to undergoing another dissociative episode when exposed to an extreme psychological stimulus, such as Mingxi’s death. Witnessing the entire process of his younger sister being tortured to death creates a shock that far exceeds his psychological capacity to endure. In an attempt to protect itself, his psyche tries to completely separate the part of his instinct associated with abnormal desires from his moral ego. At the same time, however, it must preserve the integrity of his personality, which compels it to bring that instinctive part back into the psychological system. These two opposing processes repeatedly collide, causing the psychological foundation to become increasingly unstable until it eventually collapses completely. Ye Qiuwei calls this phenomenon fragmentary dissociation of personality.

After the old psychological structure collapses, fragments of memory and thought begin to reorganize themselves, forming a new structure. Although the existing memories and patterns of thought are still retained, the way they are connected changes considerably, resulting in the emergence of a new person who is vastly different from Zhang Yixin. Ye Qiuwei believes that, during this process, the psychological elements do not come together to form a single, unified personality. Instead, they divide themselves into multiple groups, each developing its own distinct structure and corresponding to a different person existing within Zhang Yixin. She cannot determine exactly how many personalities there are, but the two most significant ones are Zhang Yixin, who inherits most of the original memories, and X.

Following the tragedy of Mingxi’s death, Zhang Yixin enters a prolonged state of unconsciousness. When he awakens seven days later, he experiences an intense internal struggle as fragments of his psyche take turns controlling him:

Personality 1 (Surface Consciousness): An ordinary man who loves his girlfriend and temporarily forgets that his younger sister exists.

Personality 2 (Desire and Guilt): Sees his girlfriend as an image of Mingxi, and experiences an eruption of desire toward his younger sister.

Personality 3 (Mingxi’s Psyche): Assumes the identity of his vulnerable younger sister, longing to be protected, but immediately becomes terrified upon realizing that she is inside a male body.

Personality 4 (X – Hatred): Filled with rage and hatred, accompanied by the strange, persistent shrieking sound echoing inside his head.

According to Ye Qiuwei, the other personalities gradually give up and recede into the background. Only “X,” sustained by profound grief and overwhelming hatred, continues to accumulate psychological strength, relentlessly seeking to break through the barriers restraining him and seize control of the mind.

There was also a second layer running throughout these stories. Ye Qiuwei did not simply provide Zhang Yixin with examples to reflect upon; she gradually trained him to examine those examples for himself. She rarely offered her conclusions upfront. Instead, she would leave a case unfinished, reveal an intriguing or questionable detail, and then wait for him to formulate his own hypothesis. When he guessed that Liu Xiangdong’s habit of touching his front teeth was an expression of compulsive thinking, she merely confirmed his deduction before continuing her explanation. When reading Chen Xi’s notebook, he independently concluded that Ding Junwen had never intended to help her. As early as their second meeting, she had pointed out that after his suppressed desires resurfaced, he had become much more sensitive to other people’s subtle gestures. He then confirmed this for himself through Tang Jiechao’s unusual behavior on the way to the hospital. The theoretical framework of instinct and ego that the two had discussed during their early meetings also became the language he would later use to understand his own dissociation. Each meeting was therefore a training session in its own right, during which he learned how to read people, trace seemingly disconnected behaviors back to an underlying fear, and place himself in another person’s psychological position until he could understand why they acted the way they did.

Ultimately, Ye Qiuwei did not eliminate X. Instead, she led Zhang Yixin to recognize X as a part of himself that had become dissociated from the rest of his psyche. To understand this, two things must be distinguished: X’s extraordinary abilities and the memories of the events that gave rise to those abilities. Zhang Yixin may be able to use X’s abilities, but that does not mean he remembers the events that created them. He can act in X’s state without being fully aware of the memories and emotions that have been separated from him. Therefore, identifying himself as X or living according to X’s role does not prove that he truly understands himself.

Ye Qiuwei gradually undermined this identification. She led Zhang Yixin back to Mingxi, to his childhood and family traumas, and then to the kidnapping in 2002. As these memories resurfaced, he began to see another person beneath the identity of X: a child who had once been frightened, who had loved his younger sister, who had hoped to escape his miserable circumstances, and who had ultimately been driven to despair by Mingxi’s death.

This creates a very important distinction. X may be a psychological state formed as a result of the tragedy, but Zhang Yixin is not merely that state.

He is also the person who experienced the tragedy, with his own memories, emotions, and relationships. Once these aspects are brought back to the surface, X can no longer be regarded as a complete identity that replaces Zhang Yixin. The mechanism can be understood as follows: initially, the traumatic memories were separated from ordinary consciousness, and the state of X emerged and operated on the basis of that dissociation. Zhang Yixin was only aware of the parts of himself that he could access, so he was inclined to regard X as a separate person or an independent ego. Ye Qiuwei, however, guided him in the opposite direction: from X’s manifestations back to the events that had created X, from abilities back to emotions, and from identity back to memory. As the fragments of memory were reconnected, the boundary between Zhang Yixin and X began to lose its absolute nature. She did not necessarily need to make X disappear. What she needed was to ensure that Zhang Yixin would no longer be controlled by X simply because he did not understand himself.

Once he recognizes the origins of X, he can understand that these abilities did not appear out of nowhere, nor do they prove that he was inherently an entirely different person. They are the result of a psychological process with specific causes. By bringing back his memories of Mingxi and his family, Ye Qiuwei helps Zhang Yixin realize how he was created. She does not allow him to regard X merely as a separate ability within himself. Instead, she leads him to recognize that these abilities may have attracted the attention of an organization, which then exploited and made use of them. What he once regarded as fate or an innate ability may, in fact, have been the result of a deliberate plan.

Finally, after Zhang Yixin has come to understand everything, Ye Qiuwei deliberately stages her own death, leading him to believe that she has committed suicide. Her departure not only brings their meetings to an end but also becomes a powerful psychological blow to him. After everything they have been through, Ye Qiuwei has become someone extraordinarily important to Zhang Yixin. Consequently, believing that she has taken her own life makes him feel an even deeper sense of attachment and regret, leaving him unable to let go of her image easily. At the same time, her death is arranged in circumstances that give Zhang Yixin further motivation to confront Yuan Xinqiang.
