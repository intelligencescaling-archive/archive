Another Chinese character that misapplied the Monty Hall Problem (Cai Zhiyuan's flawed reasoning in chapter 11)

First was the author of 18LoH misapplying it to a situation where the probability doesn't even change.

Now the author of GI commits the same mistake. I read the analysis of the first game by the side characters (up to chapter 11 where their reasoning finishes) and the author actually showed he understood how the Monty Hall Problem works at first because he explained the classic problem correctly, ***but*** when the characters analyzed how Lin Sizhi tried to apply it to the game he created, their analysis of the probabilities made the same logical mistake that the author of 18LoH did, although not as bad.

Cai Zhiyuan is apparently one of the highest ranking characters in the entire verse and even he makes this reasoning mistake.

Basically, a criminal had a gun where 3 chambers were left to be shot, with only one of them assumed (by the criminal) to hold the bullet. The gun actually had 6 chambers overall, but the first three are irrelevant to the monty hall problem scenario, so I'll focus on the last 3 chambers. Anyways, the criminal could either accept the specific chamber he was up to (shoot the innocent person) or reject the chamber and move to the next one (shoot himself).

The twist is that just as the criminal was about to shoot himself, the prompt suddenly revealed the chamber AFTER this one (2nd of the remaining 3 chambers) is empty and has no bullet, which allows the criminal to shoot himself with no consequences once he reaches the next chamber.

According to the analyses given by the players who were supposed to be smart (like Cai Zhiyuan), due to the monty hall problem, this changes the probability of the remaining third chamber having the bullet to 2/3 with the first chamber being only 1/3. This is nonsense and mathematically flawed.

The probability of 2 chambers can only be lumped if the prompt itself gave the prerequisite that they were lumped together. For example if the prompt said "An empty chamber among the 2 final chambers shall be revealed," then after the 2nd is revealed to be empty, this makes the probability of the 3rd one having the bullet 2/3. Similarly, if the prompt had said "An empty chamber among the 2 initial chambers shall be revealed", then after chamber 2 (empty one) is revealed, the probability that chamber 1 has the bullet becomes 2/3.

This is how the monty hall scenario works. The 2 doors must first be lumped together before the empty one among them is targetted in order to skew the probabilities.

However, if the prompt treats them all independently from each other by not stating that it is targetting an empty chamber from a particular set of two among the three, such as the revelation having been possible for the first, second, or third chamber, then in that case the probability after the empty chamber is revealed remains equal across both remaining chambers. This is the default assumption without any statement about how the revealed chamber is chosen which the prompt did not even mention for the criminal. A random choice which is the most logical from the criminal's perspective given the available information (or a pre-chosen choice which was the case in hindsight) would not skew the probabilities of the 2 remaining chambers.

This leaves the probability of the bullet being in chamber 1 OR 3 at 50%, from the criminal's perspective. The monty hall problem simply does not apply. But according to Cai Zhiyuan and Cao Haichuan's analysis of Lin Sizhi's game, it is mistaken to believe the probability is 1/2 for both shots due to the monty hall problem.

You see, the author misunderstood the main requirement of the monty hall problem. The main requirement is not in the "switching" by the character subject to the problem, which both the 18LoH author and this author assumed, and which is what led to them forcing the scene where the character choosing makes a specific choice and switches away from it. Rather, the switching may not even be required depending on how the problem is formatted, since the main requirement for the probabilities to skew is in the "lumping" by the host who removes an empty choice from a lumped set that the subject is informed of. The authors ignored the lumping and only did the switching which ended up destroying the required mathematical consequences for the reasoning to hold.

Cai Zhiyuan misapplied the counterintuitive math and made a deductive reasoning mistake. The math may be counterintuitive, but it's not really complex, which is disappointing. Antifeat for him and the author.

This is what I mean when I say complex fixed situations with tightly coupled causality are generally very difficult to formulate. This is what makes situations like Liar Game scenarios scale high in strict methodology scaling. Results are always easy to formulate diegetically. The extradiegetically evaluable methodology that leads to the result is the hard part.
