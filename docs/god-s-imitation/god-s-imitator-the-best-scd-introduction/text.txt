God's Imitator: The Best SCD-Introduction

So far, I have read **Akagi**, **Usogui**, **Liar Game**, **18LOH**, **Heavenly Genius**, **Death Note**, **Kakegurui**, **Alice in Borderlands**, **Classroom of the Elite**, **Reverend Insanity**, **No Game No Life, Kaiji**, and **Tomodachi Game**.

I believe each work has merits, and can show what aspects of SCD are loveable. For example, **Akagi** fully showcases what it means to be extremely intelligent in a philosophical sense, someone that transcends the dimension of thinking but unable to transcend their own humanity. **Usugui**, on the otherhand, is one of the best in-terms of purely outsmarting each other while also dealing with themes of evil and good.

In short, many of these works introduce SCD in some way and are core literature for SCD.

After scouring for a lot of SCD works, I fully believe that the best SCD introduction work is **God's Imitation**. Before reading it, I can't really recommend any of the works, perhaps maybe Usogui or **Liar Game**, but I haven't finished **Liar Game** to make an honest recommendation, so I usually just suggest **Usogui**.

But let me point out what makes **Usogui** inferior to **God's Imitation** when it comes to introducing SCD:

First off, it takes a while to get started. **Usogui** fans/readers should be able to confirm this. While I particularly enjoyed the entirety of **Usogui**, I can't say the same for others, and this one is a huge hurdle for many. Second off, a lot of the games are inherently unfair, while it makes a good story and hype ending, this usually makes it lose its logic and rigour, thus relying on many "coincidences" to win. Third, a lot of games have physical strength as a factor, which I don't think SCD should be.

I'm not saying **Usogui** is a bad work, in fact, if it's purely about writing, **Usogui** is far superior to **God's Imitation**.

Now with the preface thrown out, let me explain why I believe **God's Imitation** should be your next read, and why it should've been your first read:

# Categorizing Intelligence

>*"This is because, in the setting, characters are not defined by the two dimensions of “smart” and “stupid.” Each character actually has a five-pointed star, representing problem-solving, strategizing, politics, social skills, and good/evil*."
*- Author's Note, "God's Imitation"*

This is one of the fundamental reasons why I think it's the best SCD introduction work. Many of the examples I provided above don't care to *categorize intelligence* like **God's Imitation** did. There's nothing wrong about that, but it fundamentally misunderstands what Intelligence really is. Characters that aren't good with gambling, but good at socializing, get dumbed down to "stupid" while those who are good with gambling, but lack socializing skills are dumbed down to "intelligent". Let's take **Akagi** as an example. The main character is clearly a god amongst men or a demon on earth in terms of thinking, but by the end of the manga, we were shown that **Akagi** is basically worse than a normal human in terms of socializing. He gets better in **Ten,** but if we were to make **Akagi** face against **Ichinose** in a game about gathering votes and trust among participants (who are strangers), can you confidently say **Akagi** will still win? As a community, we have long realized these kinds of issues, so we categorize intelligence into different aspects, but not many works try to incorporate this line of thinking.

Another example would be **18 Layers of Hell: Lying is Forbidden Here**. If you have read this work, then you should understand where I'm going with this. Instances in **18LOH** are about only two aspects: deduction and scheming. It incorporates other aspects of intelligence, but it's ultimately focused on deduction and scheming. Still, that's a good start. In the novel itself, it's an explicit narrative that the main character may be the best in terms of deduction, but he's inferior to other characters in terms of scheming or resource utilization. Actually, this is more obvious in how the author repeatedly mention concepts like  Four Military Arts. Still, it's not yet rigorous or logical enough; it doesn't consider aspects like political skill and social skills.

On the other hand, **God's Imitation** does this the best among the mentioned works. Due to the setting of the novel, characters who may not be proficient in problem-solving or strategizing may be amazing in terms of political ability and socialization, and in different games, they can produce different results. Just because they don't excel in one aspect doesn't mean they're entirely useless. Of course, characters who are almost maxxed out in all aspects of intelligence exist, but they're rare.

# Rigorous and Logical, Realistic and Humanity

>*Judgement-type games are inherently unfair... because it targets human nature.*
*- God’s Imitation*

To be successful, an SCD work must have at least two of the aspects above. Logic is about deduction and strategizing. Rigor is about ensuring rules don’t have an obvious blindspot or loophole. Realism is about making sure that some strategies and deductions can be deduced or realized. Humanity is about factoring in unpredictability of decisions. An SCD work cannot be successful if it only utilizes one of those four, and the success of an SCD work hinges on how well they utilize those four.

To prevent certain spoilers, let me use an example in **Classroom of the Elite** that everybody should know… probably. During the first year volumes, the first Deserted Island Special Test was what stood out the most in the series, and that’s not without reason. It’s because it successfully utilizes Rigor, Logic, Realism, and Humanity. In the exam, the rules were clearly stated and there were no obvious loopholes about it, making it Rigorous to a certain extent. The fact that most characters act according to the rules proves this. The strategies, while not all of them were successful, can be said to be a “valid strategy”, making it Logical. Thinking of them isn’t difficult either as long as you have a clear grasp of the rules, making it Realistic. The difference of strategy is what makes it Human. With the combination of all four, it becomes an successful SCD work.

Most of the works mentioned above are successful in using these four, hence their acclaim. Liar Game, especially so. But there are also successful SCD works that only utilize two of them, such as Death Note (Logic, Humanity). Although the lack of Rigor and Realism makes it inferior to other SCD works, it’s still a good work that’s worth reading.

But God’s Imitation knows that these four are and utilizes them more explicitly than other works. All the games in the novel are Rigorous, leaving no obvious loophole and ensures fairness to all participants. All the strategies are Logical and if they’re not, they’re immediately countered. It has a touch of Realism as most of them are inferred or referenced from real algorithms or game theory. Strategies differ from each other depending on the person, making it Human.

# It’s Larger Than A Character

>*The community is more like a sandbox simulating social operation, and the relationship between the protagonist and these people is more like neighbors.*

A lot of SCD works, successful or not, focuses on only a couple of characters, and the main character usually occupies most of the screentime. For example, **Usogui**, **Akagi, Death Note, Kakegurui,** and **No Game No Life**. While this isn’t inherently bad, but it can get boring because the narrative will always ensure the main character will win, so we often only read the process not the result. Of course, any competent author or writer understand this is a problem, so they use different writing strategies. In **Usogui**, we could see Kaji get smarter and deal with various opponents, offering a different experience. In **Akagi**, the twist by the end completely reshaped the entire narrative. In **Death Note**, the author made the main character lose out to L in the beginning, bringing the possibility of failure. In **Kaiji**, the main character can genuinely lose.

In my opinion, this issue becomes more visible in works like **Kakegurui**, **No Game No Life**, and **Classroom of the Elite**, where the protagonist's success can feel too predetermined.

So what makes **God’s Imitation** have a slight edge to other works?

In music, there’s a term called Ensemble. In webnovels, this is a genre that refers to a story with no singular main character, often the story will include multiple main characters. I have only read few SCD works that completely fall under this genre, such as **Alice in the Borderland**, **Liar Game**, and **As The God’s Will**. Even then, they’re not entirely Ensemble-type works. As far as I read in **Liar Game**, the main character is always present in the story, so if I were to be strict, it should be **Alice in the Borderland** and **As The God’s Will** that fall under this genre,

But a fully Ensembe-type story isn’t automatically a good story, and a singular focus isn’t automatically a bad story. But in my opinion, the mix of these two are what makes a great and memorable SCD work. A SCD work that does this well is **Reverend Insanity** and **Lord of the Mysteries** as due to their sprawling worldbuilding, even if the main character will win in the end, his story is just a single fish in the sea. But what if the worldbuilding isn’t that big, then how would you mix it? The answer is **God’s Imitation**.

**God’s Imitation** is similar to **Liar’s Game** in this regard. The main character acts as the supreme strategist, the guy who always wins, no matter the opponent. So how does **God’s Imitation** circumvent this? Don’t include the protagonist at all. Due to how the story works, some games don’t include the protagonist, and so there’s always tension if they’ll survive or not. This is similar to **Ten Day’s Ultimatum**, which is another excellent SCD work.

# Conclusion

As SCD grows in popularity, and more and more readers come to the community, having a singular “good” recommendation is essential. I believe **God’s Imitation** fits this quite well. It’s the perfect SCD introduction work. Of course, it’s not without flaws, so it’s not entirely perfect. But if you’re new to SCD or simply looking for another work that’s different from **Usogui** and similar to **Liar Game**, while not as insane or brainburning as **18LOH,** then read **God’s Imitation.**

**God’s Imitation** is about people getting isekai’ed to a New World where 12 people form a community (microscopic society). To stay in the New World, one must have Visa Time (a time currency) and when it runs out, one dies. Everyone in New World is subjected to deathly puzzles created by **God Imitators**, who creates the games. The main character, Lin Sinzhi, is one of the **God’s Imitators**.

Think of the New World as a blank world, and everyone’s actions determine the path of that world. To be a successful, a community needs people good at Politics and Socializing, to survive they need a good Puzzle Solver and Strategist, and their system is determined by whether they’re good or evil.
