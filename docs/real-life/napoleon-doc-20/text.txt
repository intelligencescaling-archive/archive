FRENCH REVOLUTION:
Le Souper de Beaucaire
https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Le_souper_de_Beaucaire
Le Souper de Beaucaire is a political pamphlet authored by Napoleon Bonaparte in 1793, during his early career as a military officer amid the French Revolution. This work showcases Napoleon's exceptional rhetorical and propaganda abilities.
To understand the context before delving into the analysis of this pamphlet, it's important to note that Southern France was entangled in a civil war between Jacobin revolutionaries and counter-revolutionary fédérés. Through this text, Napoleon aimed to garner support from the Jacobins and undermine the counter-revolutionaries, positioning himself as a staunch defender of the revolution through effective propaganda.
Summary of the Pamphlet: The pamphlet takes the form of a fictional conversation that unfolds over a dinner between Napoleon, a revolutionary soldier, and a group of counter-revolutionaries known as "the Band of Marseille." The goal of the discussion is to persuade the group to cease their opposition to the Revolution and align with the Jacobins.
Let's begin with something as straightforward as the format in which this pamphlet is presented.
* The format:
The format employed in this pamphlet, utilizing a fictional dialogue rather than straightforward propaganda, is highly astute. The dialogue format enables the presentation of multiple perspectives, granting Napoleon the opportunity to address and refute opposing arguments within the text. Napoleon skillfully capitalizes on this format. For instance, the counter-revolutionaries in the dialogue present arguments that advocate for the continuation of the conflict and suggest that there remains hope for the counter-revolutionaries. In response, Napoleon will methodically deconstruct these notions, providing a detailed refutation of their hopes.
Let's take an example from the first few paragraphs of the pamphlet:
In the text, there’s a section where the counterrevolutionaries claim there is still hope because they supposedly won a victory in Avignon against the Jacobins. While this has some truth, Napoleon counters by arguing that their so-called victory was actually a strategic retreat by the revolutionary army, effectively shattering their hope with solid military theory.

Napoleon will thoroughly repeat this tactic in his pamphlet.
By framing his work as a dialogue, he enhances his propaganda, making it more convincing by showcasing the logical superiority of his perspective. This approach effectively undermines the reader’s morale by presenting both A) the false hope and B) the harsh reality.
* Demoralization:
Although I have already explained how Napoleon employs demoralization in this text by offering false hope followed by harsh reality, let's delve deeper into how this actually works.
First, it's essential to understand that Napoleon at the moment is extensively educated in military theory. This knowledge allows him to meticulously identify and highlight errors, even when they seem non-existent. By doing so, he effectively manipulates the reader's insecurities. Additionally, he subtly undermines the reader's understanding by targeting the counter-revolutionaries' overconfidence and ignorance.
For instance, we can observe this exact demoralization process in another excerpt from the pamphlet:
A: False Hope
The men of Marseille will argue that their struggle isn't over. They claim to have an army stationed in Aix—a strategic location—boasting more troops, cannons, and seasoned generals, ready to retake Avignon within days.
B: Harsh Reality
Napoleon counters this by first critiquing their inflated sense of superiority, their belief in their own invincibility, and their assumption that their position in Aix is unassailable. He warns them that such self-assurance is deeply harmful, suggesting that individuals driven by these instincts are naturally prone to anger and poor judgment. Following this, Napoleon turns to dismantling their false hopes using military theory. He argues that despite their newly acquired resources and leadership, the Marseille army remains disorganized and lacks unity. He forecasts that it will take at least two months for them to be ready for action. Additionally, he highlights that Aix's fortifications are inadequate for a strong defense and that the shortage of long-term supplies would make a prolonged siege unsustainable.

* White Knight:
Now I want to describe the most remarkable feat of this pamphlet, highlighting Napoleon's approach to influencing the reader through a technique known as the "white knight manipulation" method.
White knight manipulation is a tactic where an individual presents themselves as a hero or rescuer to gain power or influence over others.
Here, the idea of the "White Knight" entails:
1) Depicting the leaders and supporters of Marseille (Counterrevolutionaries) as villains -> 2) Portraying the Jacobins (revolutionaries) as heroes.
Napoleon employs this tactic throughout the text, and it's only upon reading the entire pamphlet and reflecting on it that we recognize this deliberate manipulation.
Let's first illustrate the initial step of the white knight approach: 1) Depicting the leaders and supporters of Marseille (Counterrevolutionaries) as villains.
At the start, this becomes apparent when a Marseillais mentions the strategic position in Aix: Napoleon, having previously undermined him with his expertise, will proceed to criticize their leaders. He will not only crush any lingering hope, as he has already done, but will imply that this hope may have been an illusion from the start. Napoleon will argue that their leaders are likely manipulating them into engaging in a futile civil war that could destroy Marseille. To back up his argument, he will reference past instances where Marseille's leaders provided false assurances:
* He will remind them of the deceitful promises about crossing France and leading the Republic, which ultimately failed.
* He will recall that Avignon fell in just twenty-four hours, despite assurances of a prolonged defense.
* He will emphasize that they were abandoned in their fight, despite pledges of support from southern France.
* And he will underline that their supposed allies were surrendering.
Naturally, this will lead the men of Marseille to question their leaders' credibility. Doubts will start to arise. This is Napoleon's objective: to make them believe they are being manipulated. He will not stop there but will continue to reinforce this notion.

This sets the stage for the second step of the white knight approach: 2) Portraying the Jacobins (revolutionaries) as heroes.
Napoleon will skillfully reshape the narrative by addressing the tarnished reputation of the Jacobins among the counterrevolutionaries. He will argue that the leaders of Marseille have deliberately distorted the image of the Jacobins, who, in truth, are not as ruthless as they are portrayed.
This becomes evident when a Marseillais expresses fear that, by submitting to the revolution, individuals like Dubois de Crancé and Albitte from Allobroges will take control of Marseille. To the Marseillais, these men are undesirable, labeled as bloodthirsty and opportunistic products of the tumultuous times.
Napoleon will refute this portrayal, positioning Dubois de Crancé and Albitte as steadfast friends of the people, who have consistently adhered to moral principles. He will assert that their depiction as villains is a fabrication by corrupt leaders, a fate often suffered by the virtuous. To further bolster this heroic image, Napoleon will cite examples of other revolutionary figures like Condorcet, Brissot, and Barbaroux, who were similarly maligned despite their integrity. He will argue that true heroes are frequently misunderstood and vilified by those with nefarious intentions. Napoleon will illustrate their benevolence with a compelling example: despite having the authority to confiscate Marseille’s goods in Beaucaire during the conflict, Dubois de Crancé and Albitte chose restraint, leaving the goods intact and accessible. This act of leniency, he will claim, demonstrates their compassion and justice, allowing the citizens of Marseille to return home without fear of loss.

In concluding his argument, Napoleon will underline the futility of civil war and how it unjustly divides the French people. He will remind the Marseillais that the Allobroges are not foreign enemies but fellow compatriots, urging them to reconsider their perspective and see the Jacobins not as foes, but as heroic defenders of the Republic. This approach in the text effectively elevates the Jacobins to the status of heroes, casting their actions in a noble light and contrasting them sharply with the manipulative counterrevolutionary leaders.

The pamphlet brought Napoleon to the attention of influential Jacobin political leaders, including Augustin Robespierre (cousin of Maximilien Robespierre) and Christophe Saliceti. Siege of Toulon

After Napoleon earned a promotion due to his political pamphlet and Saliceti's persuasion of Carteaux to appoint him to a crucial artillery role, he became involved in the siege of Toulon.

Later, on October 9th, he was granted full command.

Toulon had been seized by Admiral Alexander Hood, who led a substantial force of British, Spanish, and Neapolitan troops.
* Napoleon's Strategy:
Now, how was Napoleon going to recapture this vital port?
First, we need to understand the layout of Toulon. Toulon, which was a port, consisted of two harbors—the outer harbor and the inner harbor.

During his reconnaissance, Napoleon would have observed this and identified a strategic location called L'Eguillette, a promontory to the west of the city. This position, situated between both harbors, was crucial. Napoleon realized that controlling this high ground was essential for dominating the harbors.

It's even reported that Napoleon informed the war minister, Jean-Baptiste Bouchotte, saying, "To become master of the harbor, one must become master of L'Eguillette."
While this strategy might initially seem like simply securing a strategic position for advantage, it was more intricate. Here, we see Napoleon's reasoning process and foresight in action. By capturing Eguillette, Napoleon anticipated that from there, his forces could bombard the British Royal Navy ships in the inner harbor with heated cannonballs. He foresaw that this would compel the British to withdraw. The reasoning behind this was that targeting the ships would sever essential maritime resupply routes, rendering the siege of Toulon ineffective. Napoleon aimed to force the British and Spanish forces to abandon Toulon without engaging in direct confrontation. Recognizing that a direct confrontation was unfeasible, he intended to exploit their vulnerability— the supply lines. By doing so, they would be left with no option but to retreat, thereby isolating the counter-revolutionaries (fédérés) in Toulon. Although these counter-revolutionaries would still pose a challenge, their numbers would not be sufficient to engage in a direct confrontation against Napoleon's forces.

13 Vendémiaire
The 13 Vendémiaire refers to a significant event during the French Revolution that took place on October 5, 1795. After the fall of Robespierre, the Convention faced opposition from the royalists. On 13 Vendémiaire, the royalists organized an armed demonstration in Paris, aiming to overthrow the Convention and reinstate the monarchy. Napoleon was coincidentally near when he heard about the planned uprising. Barras, the president of the National Assembly, appointed Napoleon as second-in-command of the Army of the Interior, ordering him to crush the revolt by any means necessary. On that day, Napoleon instructed Captain Joachim Murat to take 100 cavalrymen to the Sablons military camp to secure the cannons and bring them to central Paris. Napoleon then strategically placed the cannons at key locations: the entrance of rue Saint-Nicaise and facing Pont Royal on Quai Voltaire.
Napoleon planned to quell the revolt with brutal force.
He understood that gentle methods were effective for persuading good people, but dealing with the rabble required instilling fear. His insight was that treating the mob with kindness made them feel invulnerable, while hanging a few would make them submissive.

So when the rebels crossed the Seine bridges, Napoleon ordered an assault with the cannons using grapeshots, a type of ammunition that discharged multiple musket balls, which was a creative and innovative tactic at the time.

This caused significant casualties, forcing the rebels to retreat. The main conflict in Paris ended by 6 p.m., but resistance continued at the church of Saint-Roch, which served as the rebels' headquarters and a place for the wounded. Snipers kept firing from rooftops and barricades until Napoleon positioned his cannons within 60 yards of the church, compelling the rebels to surrender. Approximately 300 rebels were killed that day.
This was a total victory for the Convention and especially for Napoleon. He entered high-level national politics, promoted to général de division by Barras, his income increased to 48,000 francs per year and he became known as the savior of the republic.
This was very relieving for Napoleon, because despite his good reputation from Toulon, Napoleon had been almost forgotten after the fall of Robespierre. This event marked his return to prominence.

THE WAR OF THE FIRST COALITION:
Reorganizing the Army
Now we enter the renowned Coalition Wars, where Napoleon truly begins to showcase his exceptional capabilities.
Now, the first aspect I'll examine in this first Coalition war is Napoleon's actions before it, specifically his organization of the army.
It's important to understand that the War of the First Coalition was fought across various regions, with some areas prioritized more than others. Napoleon's campaign in Northern Italy was the least prioritized of all, given only 40,000 francs for the entire campaign. Additionally, his army faced numerous challenges:
* The soldiers endured freezing temperatures without proper overcoats.
* They had not received meat for three months, and bread was delivered inconsistently.
* There were so few horses that mules had to be used to pull artillery, as most draft horses had died from starvation.
* Many soldiers lacked proper footwear, with some wearing clogs, and their uniforms were often scavenged from the dead.
* Some soldiers were only identifiable by their army-issued cartridge pouches, and many muskets were missing bayonets.
* The troops had not been paid for months, leading to discussions of mutiny.
* A widespread fever claimed the lives of 600 men from one unit in just twenty days.
Although Napoleon faced significant obstacles that prevented him from leading any battles personally, he was resolute in his aim to reorganize and strengthen his army. He concentrated on three major initiatives:
1. Tackling resource shortages
2. Addressing internal social issues within the army
3. Boosting the morale of his troops

Tackling resource shortages:
This was a critical issue. Napoleon understood that mere promises of a better future wouldn't suffice. He knew that basic resources, like food, were essential to preventing violence and indiscipline among the troops. He couldn't push them any further without addressing these needs. Therefore, Napoleon took several measures to alleviate the situation:
* He instructed Citizen Faipoult, France’s minister in Genoa, to discreetly secure a loan of 3 million francs from Jewish financiers in the area.
* He recalled cavalry from their winter quarters.
* He directed his quartermaster, Chauvet, to overhaul the supply system and threatened corrupt contractors.
* He persistently demanded supplies from Paris and secured 5,000 pairs of shoes for his troops.
* He also insisted on more food supplies, coining the famous quote, "An army marches on its stomach."
Addressing internal social issues within the army:
In addition to resource shortages, Napoleon's army faced social instability, with corrupt commanders, anti-revolutionaries, rebellious soldiers, and indiscipline among the troops. To address these issues, Napoleon implemented several social reforms within the army.
* He demoted many commanders whom he deemed corrupt or ineffective, such as Meynier.
* To prevent mutiny, he controlled or, in extreme cases, disbanded battalions. For instance, he disbanded the 3rd Battalion of the 209th Demi-Brigade, dismissed their commanders, and redistributed the soldiers to other units, ensuring uniform adherence to the rules.
* He punished any signs of revolt or anti-revolutionary behavior. For example, he punished soldiers for singing anti-revolutionary songs and in one instance, even court-martialed two officers for shouting "Long live the king!"
Boosting the morale of his troops:
Finally, to motivate his soldiers, Napoleon recognized the profound fatigue and weariness pervading his ranks. Understanding that these men were largely unpaid veterans, exhausted from numerous wars, he realized that conventional methods might not restore their dwindling spirits. Therefore, he employed a controversial one: he permitted them to pillage the lands they conquered. Napoleon didn't just allow this practice; he emphasized it as a motivational tool. He delivered impassioned speeches that directly addressed their hardships and promised substantial rewards. It is said that he declared, "Soldiers, you are poorly clothed and poorly fed. The government owes you a lot. Great provinces and cities will be yours. There you will find glory and wealth." Napoleon's psychological insight was evident in this approach. He understood that by appealing to their immediate needs and desires for improvement, he could reignite their enthusiasm and commitment. By offering tangible incentives and acknowledging their struggles, he aimed to transform their exhaustion into a driving force for conquest.
Later on, Napoleon began to impose stricter control over pillaging, particularly after the events at Mondovi. He observed that, although it initially boosted his army's morale, it also fostered a degree of indiscipline and, at times, even perverse behavior among the troops.

His small stature and youth were seen as disadvantages, and his visible affection for his wife led some to suspect favoritism. Napoleon's Montenotte Campaign
* Napoleon's Divide and Conquer Strategy:
On April 5, 1796, Napoleon revealed his strategy for the war in Northern Italy to his troops, now known as the "strategy of the central position,". This strategy involved positioning his forces between two opposing armies and launching an attack on one before the other could unite. You see, Napoleon was facing an interesting challenge: he was outnumbered but…. Not by one individual army, but by two separate armies. The Piedmontese army consisted of 25,000 soldiers, while the Austrian army comprised 30,000 soldiers. Despite having a substantial force of 38,000 men, Napoleon knew that if these two enemy armies united, their combined strength would vastly outnumber his own, thus lose.
To counter this threat, Napoleon decided to strike before the Piedmontese and Austrian forces could converge. He foresaw that if either army felt threatened, they would retreat to their respective bases, the Austrians to Milan and the Piedmontese to Turin, the latter to protect their capital. By causing this separation, Napoleon aimed to weaken their unity and defeat each army individually (defeating in detail), thereby improving his chances of success.

(Strategy Forecasting, Psychological Warfare: Abusing Insecurities)
* The Planning:

Now let’s see Napoleon’s planning phase.
Once he had finally brought order to his disorganized army, Napoleon advanced his headquarters to Albenga. Why Albenga? Because it was a strategic forward position, closer to the frontlines and the Ligurian Alps. From there, he could more effectively coordinate troop movements through the mountain passes and monitor enemy activity.
It was from this location that Napoleon began to execute his operational plan.
* Operational Plan: Isolate and Divide the Enemy
To make his strategy work, he created an operational plan to Isolate and Divide the Enemy. He decided to launch an offensive between the towns of Carcare, Altare, and Montenotte. At the time, the Austrians were stationed to the east near Genoa, a city they had recently taken from the French, cutting off a critical supply line. Meanwhile, the Piedmontese forces were positioned to the west. The area between Carcare and Montenotte was the critical point where both enemy armies were attempting to unite, making it the ideal target to disrupt their coordination.

By striking right at this middle point, Napoleon aimed to split their forces in two.
This was key. If he could divide them, he could thus isolate each one and then focus on defeating them one at a time. And as his Strategy indicated: if either army felt seriously threatened, they would retreat to defend their bases; the Austrians would fall back east toward Milan, and the Piedmontese would go west to protect Turin, their capital.
Napoleons Coverage:
Still, there was a real danger: what if the Austrians and Piedmontese managed to join forces before he could cut them off? Napoleon was aware of this risk. So how did he plan to neutralize it?
To answer that, we need to understand two key facts Napoleon likely considered:
1. The Austrians had moved east, toward Genoa, a strategic point they had recently taken, severing French supply lines.

2. Napoleon had studied the Austrian commander Beaulieu’s past campaigns and recognized that he was extremely cautious by nature. If Beaulieu sensed a threat, he would likely overreact and move to protect his position (The captured town of Genoa).

With these points in mind, Napoleon set up a clever deception. On March 27, he formally asked the Genoese government for permission to move French troops through their territory, deliberately giving the impression that he planned to attack through the Bochetta Pass and retake Genoa. Napoleon correctly assumed that Genoa would pass this information to the Austrians, thus indirectly manipulating Beaulieu.

And this act of deception misled Beaulieu into believing an attack on Genoa was imminent. But in truth, Napoleon had no such plan. The request was a calculated misdirection, a smokescreen designed to distract the Austrians while he carried out his real operation elsewhere.
(Planning Process, Planning Formulation, Info Gathering Bluffing, Misdirection, Planning Coverage)
* The Battle of Montenotte
Even though Napoleon’s overall strategy and operational planning were solid, an unforeseen development disrupted his plan. He had expected to have more time to prepare, but the Austro-Piedmontese force, positioned around Carcare, Altare, and Montenotte that were trying to link up, launched a premature attack. They struck five days earlier than Napoleon had intended to begin his own offensive, and, critically, they advanced through the same mountain pass Napoleon had planned to use to carry out his maneuver.
This was a serious challenge. Napoleon had been caught off guard and could have suffered a major defeat. But this is where his ability to adapt made the difference.
Within 48 hours, Napoleon completely turned the situation around.
When he was hit by the enemy’s advance, he quickly withdrew his forces and regrouped. During this short pause, he made two important observations:
1. The terrain was mountainous and heavily forested.
2. The enemy had stretched their lines too far, which made them vulnerable to being hit at specific points.
With this in mind, Napoleon reorganized his army and decided to strike back immediately. He launched a frontal assault to pin the enemy, and at the same time, during the early hours of the morning, at 1 a.m., in heavy rain and darkness, he sent General Masséna around the right flank to envelop the enemy. Thus the mountainous and heavily forested terrain helped the French, since it hid their movements and created confusion among the Austro-Piedmontese troops, thus in this manner taking advantage of the terrain.

The result was a decisive French victory at the Battle of Montenotte. The enemy forces, shocked and overwhelmed, were forced to retreat in disorder.
After this minor setback, Napoleon chose to move forward with his original operation and strategy. The enemies still had not fully converged, and this setback actually shows us how versatile his planning really is, he didn’t need to throw out or completely redesign his entire plan or strategy just because of a minor setback.
(Adaptability, Tactical Response, Observation)
* Napoleon’s plan to disrupt the link between the Austrian and Piedmontese forces:
After winning the battle at Montenotte, and taking advantage of the momentum, Napoleon wasted no time and advanced quickly to the town of Carcare (one of the towns where he aimed to separate the Austrian and Piedmontese forces, reaching it meant he was now inside the zone where he planned to disrupt the wedge between them) and the next step was figuring out how he could break this wedge. With this in mind, he began to create another plan, this time aimed at disrupting their wedge.
He identified three principal goals/threat’s:
1. The Austro-Piedmontese forces under General Provera, which had retreated westward;
2. A second enemy contingent at Dego, a strategically located force capable of reinforcing Provera;
3. General Beaulieu, the Austrian commander, who was stationed farther east and not immediately involved in the fighting but still posed a potential threat if he moved to support the other forces.
To address these threats simultaneously, Napoleon launched a multi-pronged operation:
* Against Provera’s forces (1): He deployed Generals Augereau and Joubert. Augereau was instructed to engage the enemy from Millesimo and advance toward Montezemolo, while Joubert was tasked with cutting off the troops at Saint-Jean and later surround Ceva, a strategically vital fortress for the Piedmontese.

Capturing the Stronghold of Ceva would directly threaten Turin, the Piedmontese capital, and likely compel them to withdraw and defend their homeland, thus breaking their unity with the Austrians.
* At Dego (2): Napoleon ordered General Masséna to confront and pin down the Austro-Piedmontese forces led by General Argenteau, preventing them from linking up with Provera. To support this, he sent Dommartin as reinforcements and held Menard in reserve should additional support be required. Acknowledging the risks of a tough engagement, Napoleon also instructed Masséna to secure a fallback route through Spinola by stationing light troops there, ensuring an organized retreat in case of a setback (Contingency).

* Concerning Beaulieu (3): General Laharpe was dispatched toward Cairo to monitor Beaulieu’s movements and maintain French pressure in the eastern sector. This was intended to delay or divert any Austrian attempt to reinforce the forces at Dego or with Provera.

In the end, everything went according to Napoleon’s plan:
Augereau and Joubert succeeded in defeating Provera’s forces at the battle of Millesimo and surrounding Ceva, forcing the Piedmontese to retreat and breaking their connection to the Austrians. Masséna was victorious at Dego after a hard-fought battle, requiring all the reinforcements Napoleon had provided. This outcome validated Napoleon’s precautionary measures. Laharpe successfully prevented Beaulieu from intervening.
Although Beaulieu did send a detachment under General Wukassovich toward Dego, it was easily defeated.
As a result, the Austro-Piedmontese coalition was decisively split, and Napoleon secured the central position he had been aiming for since the outset of the campaign.

The Defeat of the Piedmontese:
* From Ceva to the Fall of Mondovì
After successfully dividing the Austrian and Piedmontese armies, Napoleon aimed to eliminate them one by one. He chose to deal with the Piedmontese first, since they posed the smaller threat. While he advanced westward to apply pressure on them, he left General La Harpe to keep watch over the Austrians in the east, ensuring they wouldn’t interfere (Coverage).

At that time, General Colli, the last major commander of the Piedmontese, was stationed in the fortified town of Ceva. However, once Napoleon surrounded the region, Colli became afraid that his forces would be encircled. Instead of risking a trap, he decided to retreat toward Mondovì to make a stand there.
In essence, he fled out of fear, and this move reflected just how low Piedmontese morale had fallen by that point.
When Napoleon reached Ceva, he encountered resistance from the town’s elderly governor, Tornaforte, who refused to surrender despite being clearly outmatched. However, Napoleon didn’t want to waste precious time on a drawn-out siege. While Ceva had strategic value, defeating Colli’s army and removing the piedmontese from the war was the real priority. So, instead of wasting time in a siege, Napoleon left a significant French force to contain the town. He also secured local support to further isolate and weaken it. This meant that even if Tornaforte held out, he would be completely surrounded, unable to break out, and ultimately irrelevant to the overall campaign.
Napoleon continued his pursuit of General Colli, moving swiftly to cut off key routes such as the Lesegno–Mondovì road.

By doing so, he effectively isolated Colli near San Michele, just at the gates of Mondovì. This left Colli with no choice but to retreat through Vicoforte, a poor, narrow, and exposed route that offered little protection. At one point, as Colli attempted to reach Vicoforte, he tried a deceptive maneuver. He ordered his troops to quietly withdraw under the cover of night, leaving campfires burning to create the illusion that his forces were still in position.
It was a tactic reminiscent of Hannibal’s escape at Ager Falernus.
However, Napoleon was not fooled. Trusting his intuition, he suspected a trick and sent out scouts to verify the enemy’s movements.

The scouts confirmed his suspicions.
Despite the late hour and the exhaustion of his troops, Napoleon immediately resumed the chase. He caught up with Colli at Vicoforte just as the Piedmontese general was trying to form a defensive line. Taken by surprise, Colli’s forces were attacked before they could properly organize. The Piedmontese panicked and fled in disorder, with Colli retreating further west in chaos.
With Colli gone, Mondovì lay open and undefended. Napoleon reached the town without facing resistance. However, the Piedmontese governor, Dellera, intentionally delayed its surrender to buy time for Colli’s escape. This angered Napoleon, as his campaign depended entirely on speed, every day counted, and the Austrians could send reinforcements at any moment. In a cold, calculated, and ruthless move, Napoleon ordered the city to be bombarded until surrender. Eventually, Mondovì surrendered. At 7 p.m., French forces made a ceremonial entry. Town officials presented the keys to the city and formally requested protection.

Though Mondovì was spared from being looted, it was required to support the French army. Thousands of rations of bread, meat, wine, and biscuits were demanded, and delivered, to feed the occupying troops.

* General Colli Resignation
After suffering repeated humiliations at the hands of an upstart, King Victor Amadeus in Turin finally decided it was time to seek peace.
* He ordered envoys to approach General Colli with instructions to begin an armistice with Napoleon and halt the fighting.
* While also dispatching diplomats to Genoa to initiate formal peace talks.
However, once Colli made the request, Napoleon began acting strangely. He didn’t stop his advance, instead, he pushed forward and delayed his response until the evening. Even then, he didn’t agree to the armistice. Instead, he demanded that two of the three key cities, Cuneo, Alessandria, or Tortona, be handed over to the French before he would consider a ceasefire. On top of that, he instructed his representative in Genoa, Faipoult, to reject the Piedmontese peace envoys entirely.

Why would he do this?
Everything could have proceeded smoothly had Napoleon simply accepted the peace and armistice. But his actions raise suspicion. So let’s analyze this using what we know in hindsight:
Known Facts (Premises):
1. Napoleon received a legitimate peace proposal via Colli, approved by King Victor Amadeus.
2. He didn’t accept right away and instead delayed his reply.
3. He continued his military advance, further cornering Colli.
4. When he finally responded, he demanded control of strategic fortresses, territory he later received and used against Austria after defeating Piedmont.
5. He instructed Faipoult to block any peace talks in Genoa.

This is interesting, because we can't really say that Napoleon was aiming for normal peaceful negotiation. If that were the case, he wouldn’t have stalled or demanded control over key strategic cities, nor would he have continued pressing Colli’s forces.
First let’s address the obvious, Napoleon clearly had a long-term strategic plan in mind for defeating the Austrians after knocking Piedmont out of the war, and it hinged on controlling those crucial forteresse.
But then why keep advancing? Why delay peace negotiations? Wasn’t he worried the Austrians might catch up or that King Victor Amadeus could reconsider the armistice?
At first glance, it seems irrational. But everything becomes clear if we assume Napoleon was thinking like this: Colli’s army was Piedmont’s last card to play. As long as it stood, negotiations would need to be mutual. But if Napoleon crushed it, he could dictate the terms, securing not just any territory, but vital strongholds like Cuneo, Alessandria, and Tortona, which were key for launching his next moves against Austria.
Seen this way, his delays and continued offensives weren’t irrational, they were calculated moves to eliminate all leverage from the enemy and secure strategic superiority. It wasn’t hesitation; it was brilliance.
Now that we understand Napoleon's weird behaviour, let's continue.
Colli relentlessly pursued,it was clear he was in the final phase of his resistance. He attempted to retreat to Carmagnola, just south of Turin, highlighting how dangerously close Napoleon had gotten to the Piedmontese capital. But the retreat turned into a disaster: rain, snow, and mud turned the march into a nightmare, costing Colli many of his remaining troops. Desperate, Colli made repeated efforts to secure an armistice with Napoleon. This was Napoleon’s plan. He decided to cross at Piacenzaut Napoleon refused each time, insisting on the cession of specific territories, demands Colli couldn’t fulfill without direct authorization from the king. Meanwhile, Napoleon kept pressing forward. Eventually, the pressure shattered Colli both physically and mentally. He had been completely outmaneuvered, worn down, and broken. Napoleon had become his worst nightmare. At last, Colli wrote to the king, announcing his resignation and admitting he could no longer continue. And just like that, Colli’s campaign came to an end.

* Peace Negotiations
After General Colli resigned and the Austrians abandoned any hope of rescuing the Piedmontese, King Victor Amadeus finally gave in and accepted Napoleon’s terms. He sent two commissioners, General de La Tour and Colonel La Costa. for peace negotiations.
When they arrived at Napoleon’s camp, the atmosphere was eerie: silent, calm, with the entire base asleep. They were quietly greeted by the French staff and brought before Napoleon. General de La Tour attempted to renegotiate the terms, arguing they were too severe. Napoleon grew irritated and snapped, “You should find me very moderate. I’ve already taken Cherasco, Fossano, and Alba.” It was a sharp reminder of the leverage he now held.
Still, La Tour persisted. At one point, Napoleon theatrically checked his watch and bluffed:

 “A general attack is scheduled for 2 a.m.… If I don’t receive Cuneo before the end of the day, the attack will begin.”
Then he added, “I may lose battles, but I shall never lose minutes through idle talk or sloth.”

This was pure bluffing, there was no planned assault, but it was a classic Napoleonic tactic: psychological pressure through manufactured urgency. He even walked around the room and left Berthier to continue the talks, reinforcing the illusion that time was slipping away. Ultimately, the Piedmontese gave in and accepted the terms.
At dawn, Napoleon watched the sunrise beside Colonel La Costa. Casually, yet with prophetic confidence, he told him that Beaulieu wouldn’t be able to stop him before he reached Mantua, and that his army would always strike at the enemy’s flank. La Costa listened, both amazed and crushed. Napoleon’s vision and intellect overwhelmed him, but it was a bitter admiration, he stood before the man who had just symbolized the collapse of his nation.

The Conquest of Lombardy:
* Strategic Means of Taking Lombardy:
Following the decisive victory over Piedmont, the campaign shifted to the Po Valley against the Austrians. In Tortona his new headquarters, a position previously mentioned in this document as one Napoleon had deliberately sought even prior to the Franco-Piedmont negotiations (demonstrating his strategic forecasting, as we can confirm here he selected it as a strategic place advantageous from which to launch offensives against the Austrians once the Piedmont were covered).
From Tortona, he formulated his strategic goals. His immediate and prime goal was the conquest of Lombardy, a region of immense economic value, abundant in both financial resources and essential supplies that his army urgently required to sustain the ongoing campaign. Yet Napoleon’s intentions extended beyond mere material gain. The occupation of Lombardy carried significant strategic implications: by seizing control of this territory, he would effectively interpose his forces between General Beaulieu and Austria itself, cutting the Austrian commander’s line of retreat and trapping him.
Part of his strategy also involved keeping nearby Italian states, Parma, Modena, and Bologna, neutral. He couldn’t afford more enemies while focusing on Lombardy.

Beyond that, Napoleon already had his sights set on Mantua. He saw it as both a strength and a liability for Austria: heavily fortified, yes, but a garrison there was effectively stuck, limiting Austria’s ability to maneuver, so maneuvering them there would be an advantage to him.
(Analytical Thinking, Strategy Building, Strategy Forecasting, Planning Coverage)
* The Cross of the Po River
At this point, General Beaulieu had already moved into Lombardy, while Napoleon remained on the opposite side of the Po River. To enter Lombardy, Napoleon needed to cross the river, but the obvious crossing points were heavily guarded. Realizing that Beaulieu expected him to attempt a crossing at Valenza, Napoleon devised a deception. He secretly planned to cross much farther east, at Piacenza, around 70 miles away.
To do so he divided his forces like this:
* Sérurier was ordered to stage a diversionary attack at Valenza, keeping Beaulieu convinced that the main French advance would occur where he thought.
* Augereau, Laharpe, and Dallemagne each received detailed orders to march toward Piacenza and nearby towns, preparing for the real crossing.
* Masséna, commanding around 8,500 men, was positioned near Alessandria to support Napoleon once the river had been crossed.
All these movements occurred simultaneously. Napoleon’s aim was to confuse Austrian intelligence, ensuring Beaulieu wouldn’t realize where the true crossing would happen until it was too late.

The plan worked perfectly. Once Napoleon’s forces successfully crossed into Lombardy, Beaulieu suddenly understood the danger, his lines of supply and communication were exposed, and Napoleon had effectively moved behind him and was about to flank him. In a panic, Beaulieu abandoned his position at Valenza and rushed to Casalpusterlengo to link up with General Liptay, the only Austrian commander close to Napoleon.
Liptay initially resisted the French advance, but Dallemagne defeated him at Fombio. Beaulieu hurried to intervene, managing a brief local success against Laharpe, but the situation collapsed quickly. With Liptay defeated, Napoleon’s army across the Po, and his own flank in danger, Beaulieu had no choice but to retreat from Lombardy altogether.
Napoleon’s speed and deception had been overwhelming.

* The Battle of Lodi:
As the Austrians withdrew, the French pursued them without pause. Napoleon left one column advancing through Lombardy toward Milan while he personally chased the main Austrian force. By this stage, Beaulieu had already crossed the Adda River, retreating toward Tyrol, but he left General Sebottendorf stationed in Lodi to cover his rear.
When Napoleon reached the river, he found Sebottendorf’s troops fortified on the opposite bank, ready for battle.
Napoleon could have ignored them and gone straight for Milan, securing the capital and Lombardy itself. But he saw beyond that. Milan was just a trophy; the real objective was to cut Beaulieu entirely from Austria. To do that, Napoleon had to control both sides of the Adda River, because whoever holds both banks dictates movement. The river ceases to be a defensive barrier and instead becomes a weapon of control. And Beaulieu’s only escape routes toward Austria lay along those crossings. If Napoleon could seize them, he could isolate Beaulieu, drive him south toward Mantua, and complete the first stage of his Italian campaign by besieging the fortress there. Keeping Beaulieu pinned near the Po would have risked prolonging the campaign or changing his goals entirely, so maneuvering him toward Mantua was essential.

Returning to the immediate tactical situation: Napoleon initially ordered General Beaumont’s cavalry to cross upstream at a ford near Mozzanica and attack the Austrians’ right flank. But Beaumont moved too slowly, and Napoleon’s patience wore thin. Every minute of delay was dangerous, Beaulieu might send reinforcements, the Austrians could strengthen Lodi’s defenses, and if Napoleon failed to secure the river quickly, his strategy to drive the enemy toward Mantua would fail.
So he abandoned the plan and decided on the boldest possible move: a direct assault across the bridge.
He called for volunteers to lead the attack, knowing full well it was suicidal. Austrian guns and muskets commanded every inch of the bridge. Still, about a thousand grenadiers stepped forward, inspired by Napoleon’s sheer force of will. He ordered the drums to thunder, banners to wave, and bands to play music, drowning fear in noise and defiance.
As the grenadiers charged into a hail of cannon and musket fire, Napoleon sent sharpshooters in boats to a small islet under the bridge, from where they could rake the Austrian flank and relieve the pressure on the attackers.

The assault at Lodi became legendary. Against impossible odds, the French forced their way across, engaging in fierce hand-to-hand combat once they reached the opposite bank. The Austrians broke and fled. With control of the Adda River secured, Napoleon gained leverage over Lombardy and it was wide open for him.

* Napoleon enters Milan:
When Napoleon entered Milan following Masséna’s capture of the city, he was met with public acclaim. He understood, however, that the cheers were mostly ceremonial rather than true signs of loyalty. It seems, he sized up the population, dividing it into two groups. While most Italians welcomed the Austrians’ departure, many remained cautious about French rule. The other group though small, which he identified were the educated elite, were drawn to revolutionary ideas and showed a genuine curiosity. Capitalizing on this, he cast himself as an enlightened liberator to the first: when he was invited to the opulent Palazzo Serbelloni, he entertained intellectuals, artists, and aristocrats, promoting meritocracy, free thought, and the notion of Italian nationalism.

The next day, he declared the Lombardic Republic, abolished Austrian institutions, reformed universities, held elections, founded a National Guard, and collaborated with advocates of Italian unification like Francesco Melzi d’Eril, granting them significant authority. At the same time, he and his officials levied a 20-million franc contribution, highlighting the tension between French “liberation” and fiscal demands. Beyond politics, Napoleon implemented sweeping social reforms across newly conquered territories: abolishing internal tariffs, dismantling feudal privileges, restructuring finances, ending restrictive guilds, promoting religious toleration, opening ghettos, and occasionally nationalizing Church property. These measures modernized society, won the support of progressive middle classes, and embodied Enlightenment principles of equality, free trade, and intellectual freedom.

But Napoleon also uses fear as a weapon in Lombardy. For example:
When a revolt broke out in Milan and Pavia, he ordered a ruthless suppression: anyone found with a weapon was to be shot, the rebel leaders executed. He personally crushed the uprising in Pavia and even punished the French commander who had surrendered too easily, making it clear he wasn’t to be taken lightly. He declared that if even one of his soldiers had been killed, he would have wiped out the entire town.

Along with that he cracked down on the Church and didn’t hesitate to eliminate priests who resisted him. However, despite his anti-clerical stance from his Corsican youth, he promised to safeguard priests who kept religion separate from politics. It seems he recognized the Church’s influence as an institution, one he understood he couldn’t completely challenge.

Napoleon also took hundreds of hostages from local families, creating a tie that was difficult to break and ensuring their cooperation through fear and obligation. It was a calculated move, he needed their loyalty, one way or another.
In Conclusion: when expanding his control, as seen here, Napoleon divides the population into supporters and oppositors. He wins over the supporters presenting himself as a liberator. And the opposition faces fear and punishment—executions, hostages, and repression—binding them to his rule.

After many victories, the Directory began to become alarmed by Napoleon’s growing fame, and they tried to limit his power by forcing him to share command with General Kellermann, Napoleon saw it as both an insult and a stupid idea. He rejected the proposal immediately, grounding his refusal in his reasoning that command must be singular. In war, he argued, decisiveness and unity of direction are everything. Two brilliant generals with competing ideas would paralyze an army, arguing over plans instead of executing them.
“It is better to have one bad general than two good ones,” he wrote.
Meaning that even flawed leadership, if undivided, preserves speed, clarity, and authority, qualities far more vital in war than shared brilliance. His reasoning revealed an instinctive understanding of power: authority diluted is authority lost. To back his words, Napoleon threatened resignation, perfectly timing it right after his triumphs at Lodi and Milan.
“Each to his own way of making war. Kellermann will do it better than me; but both of us doing it together will do it extremely badly.”
His victories made him untouchable; the Directory, desperate for continued success, couldn’t afford to lose their most effective general. The threat worked. The plan to split the command was quietly dropped.

* Napoleon faces a Dilema:
At this moment, Napoleon had just taken Milan and established control over Lombardy. His army was on the offensive, and Austrian forces were retreating. But he then faced a dilemma:
1. Should he press forward to crush the Austrians before they could regroup?
2. Or pause to let his troops recover, secure supply lines, and reduce risk?
On the surface, waiting seemed reasonable, especially because the Piedmont armistice was fragile, and could come to an end at any given moment.

Napoleon, however, thought differently. While the armistice appeared weak, backed only by a piece of paper, in reality it was held together by fear. Piedmont was a demoralized enemy (Now neutral), intimidated by the French. If Napoleon’s army slowed or suffered a setback, there was a risk Piedmont might perceive Austria as stronger and rejoin them, since its loyalty was to the coalition, not him. Thus, the peace was mostly psychological. Napoleon understood he had to keep Piedmont afraid enough to remain neutral.
He also grasped the strategic danger of waiting: Austria could regroup and strike offensively with superior forces, and even a single defeat (as mentioned) could trigger a chain reaction leading to Piedmont switching sides. With this in mind, he realized the first option, pursuing the Austrians, was the only one viable. Because the armistice depended on fear, he could afford to abandon extreme caution, as long as his reputation remained intact. Napoleon saw his reputation as a weapon pointed at Piedmont’s head: the stronger he appeared, the less likely they were to betray him.

Basically given the scenario:
* Momentum > caution.
* Psychological dominance > temporary army recovery.
* Reputation = real strategic leverage.

* Napoleon using Roman symbolism to motivate his troops:
After reorganizing his troops quickly, he went on the offensive, but not before he motivated his soldiers by appealing to both their pride and their sense of  destiny. Napoleon praised their past victories, while simultaneously shaming any hint of hesitation:“days lost for glory are lost for happiness.”
He framed their campaign as more than a military conquest, casting it as a mission of liberation and resurrection. Drawing on the legacy of Rome, he invoked the great figures of the past, Brutus and Scipio, and presented the army as the heirs of Roman virtue. Soldiers were promised that their victories would: “re-establish the Capitol; place there in honour the statues of famous heroes; awaken the Roman people, torpid through ages of slavery.”

By framing their actions with restoring the glory of Rome, Napoleon made them feel that they were not just conquering, but reviving an ancient moral and cultural order.

* Napoleon's Intelligence:
Napoleon was known to not just rely on second-hand reports, he sought raw intelligence straight from the source so he could judge its reliability himself. He didn’t trust other officers to interpret it for him.
His methods of gathering info were:
* He’d question deserters and captured soldiers.
* He sent out cavalry patrols to scout terrain and enemy movements.
* Sometimes he disguised his own troops as farmers to gather local intel, and even held the real farmers’ wives hostage to make sure they didn’t expose the disguise.
Napoleon also knew that spies and scouts could exaggerate or misinterpret what they saw, like mistaking a small unit for a whole corps, so he tried to account for human error and panic in their reports.
Finally, his standing orders for intelligence officers were basically:
* Scout every possible route, crossing, and choke point (like fords and passes).
* Get trustworthy local guides.
* Question priests and postmasters—they always knew the gossip and routes.
* Make friendly contact with locals fast.
* Send spies regularly and try to intercept public messages or mail.

Siege of Mantua (First Phase):
Napoleon quickly began the siege of Mantua, but it proved far from easy. The city was a fortress surrounded by a lake and marshes, and he lacked sufficient heavy siege artillery; some pieces remained in Milan, while others had been intercepted by Admiral Horatio Nelson. The siege promised to be a nightmare. To make matters worse, the Directory ordered him to lead an expedition deeper into Italy, now that the peninsula lay open before him. Napoleon refused, arguing that launching a campaign in summer would be suicidal, with disease likely wiping out his army. The Directory ignored him. Caught between the ongoing siege, the threat of Austrian reinforcements from the north, and the Directory’s unrealistic demands, Napoleon pressed on, maintaining the siege while preparing to take control of Italy at the same time, and he had to do it fast, before Austrian reinforcements arrived.

Napoleon obviously realized he couldn't launch a full military campaign in Italy, so he relied on manipulation to force their surrender. On June 7, he explicitly argued to the Directory that a deeper invasion was unnecessary, suggesting that Rome would comply if presented with clear incentives and threats.
* Southern Italy: Napoleon easily forced Naples out of the war through an armistice. When Naples faced the threat of invasion, their negotiator, Prince de Belmonte-Pignatelli, signed the treaty within two hours.

      2. But if he turns on Quasdanovich, he leaves Mantua completely exposed to Wurmser’s advance.
Napoleon chooses survival over position. He decides to strike Quasdanovich first.

This also highlights Napoleon’s strategic flexibility, his ability to adapt and abandon Mantua when the situation demanded it.
The Planning:
      1. First, he commands most of his generals to gather west of Lake Garda to strike Quasdanovich.
      2. To strengthen that attack, he needs more troops, so he orders Sérurier to abandon the siege of Mantua, destroy or bury the artillery, and join the main force. If Mantua’s doomed, so be it, Napoleon won’t waste 10,000 men defending a fortress that Wurmser could overrun anyway.
      3. Once those reinforcements link up, Napoleon plans to move north quickly to back up Masséna and Augereau, aiming to crush Quasdanovich’s army.
Aftermath:
Surprisingly, Masséna manages to defeat Quasdanovich at Lonato, and with reinforcements joining in, Quasdanovich realizes he’s outnumbered and begins to retreat. Augereau recaptures Brescia, restoring Napoleon’s vital supply lines.
Quasdanovich makes one last attempt to push Masséna out of Lonato, but by then Napoleon has reorganized his entire army south of Lake Garda, and the Austrians are crushed. Masséna attacks from multiple directions, Salò, Brescia, and Gavardo, smashing Quasdanovich’s forces. Around 3,000 Austrians are lost, and the survivors flee north into the mountains.
Half of Wurmser’s army is effectively destroyed. Now, with 30,000 men still under his command, Napoleon can turn his full strength against Wurmser’s remaining 25,000, holding the upper hand.

(Strategy Logistics, Strategy Castling, Strategy Flexibility, Strategy Forecasting, Field Envisioning. Planning Preparation)
      * The remarkable incident
During the previous fight, two Austrian battalions, lost and cut off from their main force, accidentally by bad luck stumbled upon Napoleon himself and his small staff. Napoleon was in serious danger, he had barely 1,000 men, while the Austrians numbered around 3,000. Capture or death was a very real possibility.
But Napoleon bluffed his way out. Acting with total confidence, he claimed to command 60,000 troops nearby and demanded their immediate surrender, warning:
“If your division hasn’t laid down its arms in eight minutes, I’ll spare no one.”
To make the deception believable, he even shouted fake orders to Berthier, telling him to move imaginary grenadiers and artillery into position.
The Austrians, completely fooled and panicking, surrendered on the spot, only realizing afterward that they’d been tricked by a tiny escort. Had they known the truth, they could have captured or killed Napoleon himself.

(Strategy Castling, Bluffing, Fabrication, Sophistry, Acting Skills Fast Thinking)
      * Second Battle of Castiglione:
So to all this, what actually happened to Wurmser? Where did he go?
Well, remember his strategy: relieve Mantua, disrupt Napoleon’s supply lines, and send a small force from the east during the chaos. For a while, Wurmser stuck to his plan. He got reports saying French troops were still near Mantua, but that intel was already outdated Napoleon had moved on, and by the time Wurmser figured it out, he rushed north along the Mincio River, captured Borghetto and Valeggio, and then started besieging Peschiera. He still thought he could connect with Quasdanovich, but when he learned that Quasdanovich had already been defeated and had left, it was too late, Napoleon’s forces were regrouping and ready for him.
Wurmser, brave but stubborn, refuses to retreat. He’s counting on Quasdanovich’s forces to return, though he has no real idea where they actually are. Still, he pushes forward toward Castiglione, arriving on August 3 and spreading out his troops. There he clashes with the French under Augereau (First Battle of Castiglione) until Napoleon arrives on August 4. Now face to face with him, Wurmser has to choose whether to hold his ground or pull back. He chooses to stand and fight. On August 5, the two armies face each other in their first real tactical showdown.
Napoleon opens with a frontal assault, and Wurmser answers in kind. At first, it looks like Wurmser’s winning, he’s pressing hard and nearly flanking Napoleon’s left.
But Napoleon’s real plan unfolds.
The day before the battle, when he first reached the field, he’d already ordered Sérurier’s division (now under Fiorella) to move from Marcaria to Guidizzolo (near the Austrian rear). Napoleon had positioned them in Marcaria long earlier precisely for the purpose of:
      * Guarding a key road,
But now its purpose changed
      * To act as a hidden strike force if the Austrians overextended themselves.
This was that moment.

He’d hold Wurmser in place, distract him with a seemingly desperate fight, and lure all Austrian attention to the front, while Fiorella swung in unseen from the south. When Fiorella’s division suddenly appeared in Wurmser’s rear, it was chaotic. Napoleon immediately went on the full offensive, dropping the act. Wurmser’s army broke under the double attack. He was forced into a hasty retreat, narrowly avoiding total destruction. Napoleon’s victory at Castiglione secured his control over northern Italy once again, leaving Mantua isolated and pushing Wurmser to retreat to the north of the Alps.

By August 6th, Napoleon had crushed Wurmser’s first offensive so thoroughly that he wrote to Paris bragging, “Another campaign done in five days.”
(Misdirection, Bluffing, Concealment, Tactics, Planning Coverage, Planning Contingency, Strategy Building)
Siege of Mantua (Second Phase):
After Castiglione, Napoleon returned to lay siege to Mantua. It's said that in his letters to the Directory, he wrapped himself in false humility. By then, every victory had tightened his grip on political power, and he knew it. So, when he claimed that if anyone doubted his loyalty he’d nobly resign, it wasn’t sincerity,, it was bait. He understood perfectly that the Directory feared losing him, the one man turning France’s fortunes around. His humility was a mask to keep the directory calm; behind it, he was quietly tightening the strings of command while they applauded his “virtue.”

(Acting Skills, Bluffing, Emotional Manipulation)
      * Napoleon's Strategic Net
Eventually, Napoleon learned that Wurmser had been reinforced and was planning another push toward Mantua. Rather than wait, he decided to act first. His strategy was to: attack and defeat Wurmser as fast and aggressively as possible, force him to retreat north, trap the Austrians in the mountains, and at the same time advance to join forces with Moreau in Germany. With this strategy, he moved his army across northern Italy, taking the initiative and seizing the offensive.
The problem was, he didn’t know which route Wurmser would use, so he turned that uncertainty into a strategic net. He split his army with calculated precision:
      * Vaubois with 11,000 men advanced along the west side of Lake Garda to seal that path.
      * Masséna with 13,000 and Augereau with 9,000 formed the central line between Rivoli and Verona, ready to pivot in any direction.
Napoleon arranged them like chess pieces within reach of his masse de manœuvre, his mobile strike core that could swing toward any emerging threat.

Meanwhile, Kilmaine patrolled the east with 1,200 infantry and most of the cavalry, covering the least likely approach, but covering it nonetheless (Coverage).
And even in the middle of this strategic net, Napoleon kept his machine running: brandy, flour, ammunition, and hard-tack flowed to the front without pause. Every detail was sharpened for the moment he’d unleash the blow.
In the end, his strategy works. When he learns that Wurmser is advancing through the Vallagarina Valley along the Adige River, he immediately gathers his troops and launches a rapid and aggressive attack on Wurmser’s vanguard. The Austrians are taken so completely by surprise that at Calliano they’re literally caught cooking breakfast.

(Strategy Building, Planning Coverage, Risk Assesments)
After the Austrians were defeated and scattered, Napoleon learned from interrogating prisoners that the Austrian army had split, with Wurmser heading east toward Bassano to relieve Mantua. This was unexpected, since Napoleon had planned for the Austrians to retreat north, allowing him to trap them and link up with Moreau. Even though Wurmser had lost much of his force, he was still advancing toward Mantua. Recognizing the situation, Napoleon quickly understood he had to change his strategy: intercept Wurmser before he reached Mantua. The city’s strategic value was too high to ignore.

Napoleon couldn’t just chase Wurmser down the Brenta: Davidovich’s division in the Tyrol could attack the French rear, which would be disastrous.

Solution: pin Davidovich in the Tyrol and push him further back to prevent interference, while a single French division (Vaubois) could hold him.
(Strategy Flexibility, Risk Assessments, Planning Coverage)
So Napoleon kept up the pursuit. Wurmser believed he could join forces with the rest of his army near Mantua, but as mentioned Napoleon had them already pinned down and in retreat. Regardless, Wurmser was bound to head for Mantua, he had no real choice if he wanted to relieve the fortress and keep it from collapsing.
In the end, Wurmser manages to reach Mantua, but that wasn’t really Napoleon’s fault. Critics often blame him, but in truth, Napoleon did everything possible to stop him. He ordered Masséna to intercept Wurmser near Arcole, yet Masséna took a longer route and missed the chance. Meanwhile, Napoleon had already commanded that all bridges near Mantua be destroyed before even battle with Wurmser started. Unfortunately, one of his officers, SAHUGUET, failed to demolish key bridges at Villimpenta and Molinella. SAHUGUET's blunder therefore allowed Wurmser to slip through, cross safely, and reach Mantua without resistance, while Napoleon’s pursuit arrived too late.
So although Napoleon couldn’t prevent Wurmser’s entry, it was clearly the fault of his subordinates who didn’t execute orders precisely, ironically proving Napoleon’s judgment was correct.
Even with Wurmser now inside Mantua, the broader picture was still a French victory. Most of Wurmser’s forces had been split, beaten, or scattered in the north. He reached Mantua with only a fraction of his army, essentially defeated and now trapped inside, surrounded by Napoleon’s forces. Still, it wasn’t a total win for Napoleon either. Strategically, Mantua was now slightly stronger from within, commanded by a capable general. His main strategy; to starve the garrison, remained intact, but it would now take longer and face greater resistance.
Siege of Mantua (Third Phase):
From this point, Napoleon’s campaign would enter its toughest phase. Up to now, he’d mostly faced mediocre commanders like Beaulieu, and a few somewhat capable ones like Colli and Wurmser who at least tried to counter him. But now, a far more dangerous rival appeared, one who would prove to be among the finest generals of the Napoleonic Wars: Jószef Alvinczy. The Austrian Emperor, still determined to free Mantua, appointed Feldzeugmeister Alvinczy to lead a new relief effort. A seasoned Hungarian officer, old-fashioned yet shrewd, and the Austrian Emperor’s personal teacher, Alvinczy would be the first to truly challenge Napoleon and push him into difficult situations.
Alvinczy met with officers Davidovich, Spork, and Weyrother. Together, they made the following plan.
The idea was a pincer movement:
      * Davidovich would come down from the Tyrol, pushing south.
      * Alvinczi would advance west from Friuli toward Bassano and challenge the French near Verona.
      * Meanwhile, Wurmser, still trapped in Mantua, was supposed to break out and hit Napoleon’s army from behind.

Meanwhile, Napoleon remained tied down at Mantua, though he still held scattered forces in the areas where Alvinczy was preparing his offensive. Unfortunately for him, Alvinczy’s army heavily outnumbered the French. Masséna, positioned at Bassano, was forced to abandon the town, while farther north, Vaubois was driven back toward Rivoli by Davidovich. The Austrian pincer movement was unfolding perfectly, closing in on Napoleon’s stretched lines. The French fought hard to hold their ground, but the Austrians maintained their momentum.
Frustration boiled in Napoleon. His northern flank in the Tyrol was collapsing under pressure, and in the east he was being battered by Alvinczy. Napoleon decided to join Massena, but then at Caldiero, he suffered his first defeat against Alvinczy, having been lured into a trap and overwhelmed. He confessed the grim reality himself: “We may be on the eve of losing Italy,” admitting that his army was worn down, depleted, and left to fend for itself.
      * Napoleon’s Indirect warfare Strategy against Alvinczy
Although Napoleon appeared to be on the brink of defeat, he was in fact far from beaten. Even in this moment of apparent crisis, he was calmly calculating his options with precision. He recognized that a direct engagement against Alvinczy, given the severe numerical disparity, would lead only to destruction. Any hope of success, therefore, lay not in frontal battle but in indirect action, which became the core of his strategy.
Therefore:
Rather than confronting the Austrian commander head-on, Napoleon sought to force him into a strategic dilemma. This required rapid movement and strict secrecy. His aim was not to attack Alvinczy’s front, but to suddenly emerge in the Austrian rear, threatening the enemy’s line of retreat and communications, simultaneously forcing them into bad terrain. By placing Alvinczy’s withdrawal routes, supplies, artillery, and baggage at risk, Napoleon aimed to halt the Austrian advance without seeking immediate battle.
The execution of this maneuver was constrained by extreme time pressure. Austrian forces were steadily breaking through on the French northern front, leaving Napoleon with little room for delay. Speed was therefore critical.
To secure the time needed for this high-risk operation, Napoleon ordered Vaubois to maintain his position in the north for as long as possible, fully aware that this meant exposing those forces to serious danger while the decisive maneuver was carried out elsewhere (Coverage).

The plan to execute this Strategy would follow:
      1. Leave Macquard at Verona to maintain the illusion that the French army hadn’t moved, keeping the Austrians distracted (Coverage).
      2. Secretly cross the Adige River at Ronco.
      3. Maneuver behind Alvinczy’s main force, the Friuli Corps.
      4. Cut off their line of retreat.
      5. Force them north into the mountains or trap them completely, destroying the enemy from a position of advantage.

Although the route through Ronco formed one of the strangest and most constricted battlefields of the campaign, dominated by marshes, high dikes, and narrow causeways, it still offered a dangerous but viable path. Movement was channelled into a few raised roads, turning the terrain into a severe bottleneck where large forces could neither deploy nor manoeuvre freely.
Napoleon had actually identified this corridor weeks before and had already foreseen its potential value, whether exploited by himself or by the enemy, possibly as early as September, as suggested in the text that follows.

Dangerous though it was, the corridor represented the only viable route for turning Alvinczy’s flank and threatening his rear. Alvinczy was aware of the area’s vulnerability and had posted forces to watch it, yet the French night movement and the construction of the pontoon bridge at Ronco was so fast that it escaped Austrian detection.
(Critical Thinking, Adaptability, Planning Coverage, Misdirection, Concealment, Indirect Manipulation, Strategy Logistics, Strategy Castling, Anticipation)
      * Alvinczy's Thin Line, Napoleon's Desperate Stand
But then when the French crossed the Adige river at Ronco and advanced toward Arcole, they encountered a prepared Austrian defence. The approaches to the village were swept by musketry and artillery fire from Croat battalions positioned behind barricaded, loopholed houses and guns emplaced along the dikes. The final stretch of the causeway became a lethal killing zone, halting repeated French assaults and turning the fight into a brutal struggle for control of a single narrow approach.

Alvinczy likewise seems to have understood the weakness of the Adige crossing, prompting him to secure Arcole with a small defensive force.
This moment represented one in which Napoleon came dangerously close to losing everything. Although his strategy was genius, the conditions on the ground were relentlessly hostile. The terrain was nearly impassable, troop morale was low, and Alvinczy was smart enough to have left a small but effective detachment at Arcole. This force proved enough to block the final bridge and prevent a direct crossing.Once Alvinczy realized that Napoleon’s presence in the area was genuine rather than a diversion, he immediately dispatched reinforcements, correctly identifying this sector as the decisive point upon which the campaign would turn.
These additional Austrian forces were, fortunately for the French, delayed by Masséna, largely through circumstance rather than design.
Even so, the situation at Arcole remained dire. Napoleon quickly concluded that a frontal assault was futile, the bridge could not be taken head-on. He therefore altered his approach: abandoning direct attacks on the bridge, he ordered a detachment to march away from the crossing, recross the river by ferry, and attempt to approach Arcole from the rear. The maneuver was slow and fraught with danger. Time was slipping away, Austrian reinforcements were closing in, and the margin for error was vanishing.

Meanwhile, Napoleon was forced to hold the enemy at Arcole with increasingly strained forces. He attempted to press the advance himself, even exposing his own person in an effort to inspire movement.
(Adaptability, Critical Thinking, Misdirection)
In the end, Napoleon’s plan unfolded as intended. The troops he had sent on the wide turning movement succeeded in surprising the defenders and capturing Arcole. Tactically, it was a success. Yet Napoleon did not regard it as a true victory. His concern was no longer tactical; he was thinking in operational and strategic terms, beyond the present. Although he had achieved his immediate objectives, crossing the river, emerging in Alvinczy’s rear, and securing Arcole, Napoleon was acutely aware of the delay this had required. That delay raised a far more dangerous possibility: Davidovich may already have defeated Vaubois at Rivoli, despite Napoleon’s earlier orders for him to hold out for as long as possible. This fear was justified, as the coverage Napoleon had left in the north was thin and inflexible. If Vaubois had indeed been beaten, Napoleon’s army would be strategically outflanked and cut off from Mantua. Faced with this risk, Napoleon chose speed over consolidation. He withdrew from Arcole as rapidly as possible, recognizing that the only viable course now was to reunite with Vaubois, confront Davidovich (this shows strategy flexibility). It was this overriding strategic consideration, rather than the situation at Arcole itself, that compelled Napoleon to abandon the position.
Years later, Marmont reflects:
Renouncing apparent success to obtain real success later.
This decision confused soldiers at the time, sparked later controversy, but showed operational maturity and detachment from battlefield ego. Napoleon sacrifices a village, a symbol, to preserve freedom of maneuver.
This is important to take in mind for what is about to happen next….
(Critical Thinking, Abstract Reasoning, Abductive Reasoning, Strategy Flexibility)
      * The Battle of Arcole
During the withdrawal, Napoleon suddenly received important news; that Vaubois had not been attacked and that Davidovich had not advanced at all.
This news meant that Napoleon still possessed a narrow but vital window of time.
Alvinczy’s inaction proved to be a serious error, one that Napoleon was quick to recognize and exploit. He then immediately halted the retreat and ordered his forces to reverse course and return to the offensive.
Alvinczy, meanwhile, moved into Arcole after Napoleon’s departure, only to find himself once again confronted by the French army.
The French crossed the Adige yet again at Ronco.

Austrian forces initially attacked them on the left bank near Bionde, but Masséna decisively repelled the assault through skillful and deceptive maneuvering. Augereau, however, was less successful in his attempt to advance toward Arcole, as the Austrians had reoccupied the ground and established strong defensive positions.
Napoleon’s Strategy at Arcole:
Napoleon now appeared to be locked in a stalemate. Although he was able to beat back repeated Austrian attacks, he could not force his way back into Arcole. The question, then, was what course of action remained open to him. From his earlier attempts, Napoleon already understood that Arcole could not be taken through frontal assaults alone; success required crossing by alternate routes and striking the Austrians by surprise. As before, this principle would guide his strategy. This time, however, the circumstances were far more difficult. Napoleon was no longer facing minor decoy forces, but Alvinczy himself. For this reason, his initial attempts to cross at Albaredo, made twice, failed. Aware that the Austrian commander was anticipating further maneuvers, Napoleon recognized that only greater subtlety would succeed. He therefore devised a new and more elaborate plan, one designed to break the deadlock and decisively shift the course of the battle.
The plan to execute this Strategy would follow:
Napoleon’s primary objective in his plan was to cross the Alpone River, thereby gaining access to the rear of Arcole.

Previous attempts had been frustrated, but this time he intended to succeed through careful preparation.
He first ordered the construction of a bridge to secure the crossing, but the plan extended far beyond that single measure.
Fortification of the Strategy:
      1. Napoleon assigned the 51st Line to guard the new bridge, ensuring its immediate security.
      2. At the same time, Augereau received detailed instructions: a battalion of the 40th Line and fifty cavalry were to be dispatched to Legnago, from where they would advance north along the left bank of the Adige in support of the new crossing. Although this movement from Legnago would take longer to develop, once in position it would reinforce the forces crossing the Alpone.

In this way, Napoleon planned 2 successive layers to his strategy, one to protect the crossing itself and another to strengthen it once established, further reinforcing the strategy's resilience.
Napoleon still needed to hold the Austrian forces in the north in check and block any renewed advance from Bionde. To do this, he instructed:
      1. Masséna to contain Austrian attacks on one flank while continuing to apply pressure at Arcole…↓
      2. As he ordered Robert to advance along the causeway toward the Arcole bridge. This forced the Austrians to keep their attention fixed on the bridge and to commit their infantry and artillery there. By pinning the enemy in place, Napoleon created the distraction necessary to carry out his decisive river crossing maneuver.

The Battle:
The attack from multiple directions was launched, and by its very nature it limited the Austrians’ ability to concentrate their fire effectively. For the most part, the plan unfolded as intended. Augereau’s division succeeded in crossing the Alpone, although their bridge came under heavy fire and only part of the force was able to pass. This setback did not stop the advance. Augereau continued toward Arcole, eventually engaging the enemy and driving them back, steadily compressing the Austrian position within the village. Shortly afterward, the French column marching from Legnago arrived to support Augereau as planned; they threatened the Austrian flank at Arcole while Augereau fixed them in place from the front.
Under this growing pressure, Austrian resistance began to falter.
At the same time, on Masséna’s sector, Austrian assaults once again failed. Masséna repelled the attacks and pushed the enemy back, maintaining control of his front and pinning them down; meanwhile Augereau continued his maneuver.
Up to this point, Napoleon’s plan had worked almost perfectly.
But the situation changed when the Austrians, recognizing that defeat was imminent, launched a desperate counterattack. Roughly four thousand men surged out of Arcole and charged along the narrow dike in an attempt to disrupt the French forces pinning them. The sudden blow threatened the French momentum, even killing Robert in the process, but the effort came too late. Masséna, having already defeated the earlier attacks and facing little pressure, still had fresh troops available. Napoleon immediately shifted his forces toward the crisis, and not only Masséna’s but nearly all the available units he had, an example of his classic concentration of force applied at a tactical level.
Napoleon’s decision to concentrate his forces in that moment was based from his observations of the Austrian line, which he concluded was beginning to give way. Fire from the guns at Albaredo diminished, skirmishers appeared to pull back in certain sectors, French troops gained limited entry into the village, and a number of prisoners were captured. Seizing the moment Napoleon committed everything against the counterattack. French morale surged, cries of “Vive la République!” echoed across the field, and Masséna’s men moved directly into the threatened sector. The Austrian counterattack was smashed, struck in the flank and driven back.

With the Austrian counterattack defeated, the French resumed their advance on all fronts. Augereau regained momentum at Arcole, Masséna pressed forward, and coordinated attacks converged across the battlefield. The Austrians were forced back not only from the bridge linking the Alpone to Arcole, which they had defended so stubbornly, but also from the rear of the village under Augereau’s pressure. Their position was now on the verge of collapse.
Sensing this, Napoleon delivered one final trick; psychological warfare.
As daylight faded, he dispatched twenty-five cavalrymen to ride around the Austrian flank, sounding trumpets at several points. The Austrians, believing a large force was moving to cut off their retreat, fell into confusion. Morale broke.

Arcole finally fell. Masséna crossed the bridge into the village as Augereau entered from the opposite side. Austrian resistance collapsed, the enemy retreated, and French forces pressed the pursuit. Napoleon had won the Battle of Arcole through masterful coordination, concentration, and deception.

(Observation, Adaptability, Bluffing, Psychological-Warfare: Demoralization)
After his victory at Arcole on 18 November, Napoleon did not immediately pursue Alvinczy. While he was always attentive to the tactical situation, he also had to weigh broader operational and strategic variables. He therefore shifted his focus to what he correctly judged to be the more serious threat: Davidovich’s Tyrol Corps. As noted earlier, this force endangered Vaubois at Rivoli and, despite earlier delays, had succeeded in pushing him back.
Leaving a screen of observation cavalry to monitor Alvinczy (Coverage).
Napoleon advanced with Masséna through Ronco toward Villafranca, seeking to reestablish contact with Vaubois, who had withdrawn toward Peschiera. This movement was designed to prevent Davidovich from threatening Mantua or cutting French lines of communication. Simultaneously, Napoleon sent Augereau north through the Val Pantena toward Dolcè, with orders to move against Davidovich’s line of retreat.

Faced with these developments and reports that Alvinczy had been decisively defeated, Davidovich chose to withdraw. Although he briefly considered making a stand at Rivoli, the appearance of French columns convinced him that the situation was deteriorating, and he ordered a general retreat.
Unbeknownst to him, Alvinczy intended to link up with the Tyrol Corps and had promised to do so by the end of November, but this message did not reach Davidovich in time. Believing the campaign to be collapsing, Davidovich disengaged entirely.
When Alvinczy learned on 23 November that Davidovich had withdrawn from the campaign, he likewise decided to retreat. This decision brought the third phase of the siege of Mantua to an end, securing a complete Napoleonic victory.
(Planning Coverage, Psychological Warfare: Abusing Insecurities, Strategy Castling)
Siege of Mantua (Final Phase):
After the victory at Arcole, Napoleon’s army was exhausted, having suffered heavy losses. This decline in morale was no longer something he could fully control. What Napoleon could do, however, was improve the army by optimizing how it was led. If he could not directly command the morale of every soldier, he could at least ensure that the right men were commanding them.
This later explains his decision to replace Vaubois with Joubert. Vaubois had been responsible for holding Rivoli and had failed when Davidovich broke through. Even after the position was recovered, Napoleon refused to entrust such a critical sector to him again. He regarded Vaubois as mediocre in character and lacking the ability to command large formations.
Joubert, by contrast, was a commander Napoleon selected for very specific reasons. Napoleon had personally observed him in combat and trusted his own judgment over reputation or hearsay. He knew Joubert’s temperament, courage, and endurance firsthand. In many ways, Joubert reflected Napoleon himself. He was not a pre-Revolutionary career officer, had studied law, rose through merit rather than birth.

These qualities were the ones that probably convinced Napoleon that Joubert was the kind of leader the army needed at that moment.
(Strategy Logistics, Leadership, Emotional Perception & Understanding)
As Napoleon worked to equip, reorganize, and strengthen his fragile army, Wurmser and the garrison trapped inside Mantua were on the brink of collapse, suffering from disease, hunger, and rising deaths. Alvinczi recognized that this was his final chance to rescue Mantua, since any further delay would lead to its fall. Meanwhile, Napoleon faced serious difficulties of his own, especially shortages of equipment and food, forcing his army to survive by plunder, making it an ideal moment for Alvinczi to strike once more.
Alvinczi therefore shaped his strategy around an advance through the Tyrolean mountains to the north. His objective was Rivoli, where the terrain, once broken through, favored the attacker. Although an approach from the east was possible, he had already suffered defeat there, and the flat terrain would have forced him into open-field combat.
However, Alvinczi did not rely on a single line of advance. He devised a complex and carefully coordinated plan to ensure success. While he ruled out a main attack from the east, he still committed troops there, using them to conduct deliberate feints across the plains. These movements were designed to draw French attention away from the true offensive coming from the north through the Tyrol toward Rivoli. Two columns were assigned to this diversionary effort.
      1. Provera’s column marched from Padua to Legnago, crossed the Adige, and advanced toward Mantua.
      2. Bajalich’s column moved from Bassano toward Verona, attacking French positions in the area.
In support: Mittrovsky held the Val Sugana, securing communication between the Tyrol and the attacking forces and preventing French interference with the mountain advance.
The purpose of these eastern columns was not to fight a decisive battle but to deceive. Comprising roughly 18,000 men, the diversion was large enough to appear threatening yet insufficient to win the campaign on its own. Meanwhile, the main army, about 28,000 men, advanced from Tyrol toward Rivoli to exploit the advantages of the terrain.
Amid all this, Napoleon’s own situation was complex.
He had finally received reinforcements and was attempting to secure the southern flank by applying pressure on the Papal States, which temporarily diverted his attention. Even so, his forces were carefully deployed: troops remained before Mantua, Augereau was stationed at Legnago, Masséna at San Michele, Rey at Brescia, and, as noted earlier, Joubert at Rivoli. This distribution allowed Napoleon to detect and respond to any Austrian advance. It highlights the strength of his Strategy Building, by positioning his generals in this way, he ensured that no avenue of approach was left unguarded, and that he was never exposed to surprise attacks from unknown directions.
(Strategy Building, Planning Coverage)
      * Napoleon discovers Alvinczi’s plan
However, Napoleon was not physically present, which meant Alvinczi’s diversions could still mislead him if his generals misread the situation, and initially, they did. When the feints began, confusion spread, and even Napoleon briefly believed the main attack would come from the east. Meanwhile, the true Austrian attack under Lusignan advanced with difficulty through harsh weather but eventually reached Joubert’s rear. At first, Alvinczi’s plan appeared to be working, until Joubert outmaneuvered Lusignan by lighting false campfires and withdrawing slightly. When Lusignan moved to engage, he discovered the deception too late, Joubert had already fallen back to Rivoli. Observing Austrian attempts to avoid frontal assault, turn his flanks, and cut his retreat, Joubert correctly identified Rivoli as the main attack. He informed Napoleon that this was the main Austrian attack and that the other actions were merely diversions. The report gave Napoleon the confirmation he needed. He reacted at once, redirecting reinforcements to Rivoli, warning of a possible sortie from Mantua, ordering Masséna to support Joubert, and preparing to go there himself. This moment marked the concentration of French forces at the decisive point and validated Napoleon’s judgment in replacing Vaubois with Joubert.
(Decision Making)
      * Napoleon creates a Strategic Net even before Rivoli
As Napoleon concentrated his forces at Rivoli, he also positioned them deliberately with an eye beyond the battle itself.
      * The 18th Line was sent to Garda, blocking any Austrian movement toward Peschiera and sealing off their escape routes.

      * During the night, Rey was dispatched to Castelnuovo to secure the southern edge of the battlefield, ensuring that Rivoli could not be outflanked from the south.
He was not merely reinforcing a single point. He was also tightening a net, shaping the battlefield and controlling Austrian options, so that even after Rivoli was won, the enemy would have no freedom of movement.

(Strategy Building, Planning Coverage, Anticipation, Field Envisioning)
         * The Battle of Rivoli
Napoleon reached Rivoli around midnight, between 2 a.m., in extreme cold. The Austrians were already on the field, settling into their battle positions. Rather than acting immediately, Napoleon went to observe. From the pattern of enemy campfires alone, he was able to distinguish five separate Austrian columns.
He noted they stretched between the Adige and Lake Garda
And concluded that the Austrians intended to join on the Rivoli plateau, as they had done before.

If this concentration were completed, Joubert would likely be crushed. From that moment on, the battle became a race against time.
(Observation, Analytical & Critical Thinking, Inductive Reasoning, Info Gathering)
Napoleon’s strategy, therefore, was to prevent the Austrian columns from uniting on the plateau.
From this point onward, the battle became tactical.
He aimed to achieve his objective by holding the key terrain that blocked Austrian concentration. The columns he had identified were positioned behind a semicircular ring of hills surrounding the plateau. Napoleon understood that control of these heights meant control of observation, artillery deployment, and the ability to deny the enemy a coordinated advance. His goal was not to launch an immediate offensive, but to seize the hills first and conduct a defensive battle from this dominant ground.

(Columns on the Center lead by Alvinczi)
Napoleon also dispatched a regiment to the Pontare road to block and defend it against other advancing Austrian columns led by Quasdanovich .

(Columns on the East in Pontare lead by Quasdanovich)
In addition to all this, Napoleon had Masséna, who was still on the way with reinforcements, so another of Napoleon’s objectives was to delay the enemy and buy time until they arrived.
The battle opened early and quickly became a brutal, close-run fight, with both armies attacking aggressively. Napoleon’s right wing was pinned, and even after Masséna arrived the French situation remained precarious. His arrival only brought a brief stabilization before events turned sharply against them. The Austrian columns advancing through the Adige Valley led by Quasdanovich broke through at Pontare, while enemy forces in the center pushed Napoleon back, with some units reaching the plateau. Napoleon was now under pressure from all directions. To make matters worse, a column sent by Alvinczi under Lusignan, which had been maneuvering around the battlefield, suddenly appeared in Napoleon’s rear, cutting off his line of retreat and placing him in grave danger of encirclement.

Everything appeared on the verge of collapse, and all attention turned to Napoleon. It was at this moment that he delivered his famous quote:
“They are ours.”
But why could Napoleon say this when defeat seemed imminent?
Because, beneath the apparent chaos, he clearly understood the true situation unlike anyone else did.
Napoleon’s Insight:
         1. First, regarding his rear: Napoleon recognized that Lusignan’s column was not the decisive threat it appeared to be. The force was isolated, late, still too far away, and poorly supported by artillery. Crucially, it lacked the strength to exploit the temporary cutting of Napoleon’s line of retreat. Napoleon understood a fundamental principle of war: a force that blocks your retreat but cannot act on it becomes a target rather than a danger.

Note: It is also important to note that Napoleon knew Lusignan would arrive late before this confirmation. When he reconnoitred the battlefield before the battle began when he was identifying the Austrian columns, he had also been interpreting the pattern and distance of the enemy, inferred their positions and pace of movement. From this, he concluded that Lusignan would be late.
         2. Second, in the center, where the main fighting against Alvinczi took place, Napoleon had spent the entire battle holding a defensive position along the ring of hills surrounding the plateau rather than attacking. As a result, it was the Austrians who became exhausted. They were conducting repeated offensive assaults uphill while trying to coordinate several advances at the same time. This is why Napoleon had stated before battle that whoever controlled these heights would control the fight. Consequently, even when Austrian units managed to break through and push the French back, they did so in a state of fatigue and disorder, spreading out as their momentum faded.

         3. Third, Napoleon identified the only truly immediate threat: Quasdanovich’s column advancing from Pontare. The Austrians there had broken through and were close to reaching the plateau.
Note: Napoleon probably also had realized he did not need to defeat every Austrian column simultaneously. If he could destroy one, he could then turn his full attention to the others in sequence.
Now with these insights explained, Napoleon’s next actions make sense.
         1. He turned first against the Austrians advancing from Pontare. Napoleon identified a narrow gorge that Quasdanovich’s troops had to pass through in order to reach the plateau and decided to set a trap there.

To do so, he stripped away only the minimum number of troops from the center (where the main fight against Alvinczi was still underway) , a risky move that demanded speed and precision. Napoleon then allowed Quasdanovich to continue advancing, as the Austrian commander believed he was on the verge of victory. When the Austrian columns emerged from the gorge onto the plateau, the trap was sprung. French artillery poured canisters into their dense formations, infantry surged forward with bayonets, and cavalry struck from the flanks and rear. The terrain transformed the position into a deadly kill zone: narrow approaches, tightly packed enemy troops, and overwhelming short-range firepower. As the Austrians attempted to withdraw back into the gorge, a lucky artillery hit detonated an ammunition wagon, triggering panic and disorder. Quasdanovich was forced to abandon the attack altogether.
Napoleon did not wait after defeating Quasdanovich.
         2. The moment Quasdanovich collapsed, he shifted his forces back toward the Austrian center. That center, already weakened, had little cavalry, limited artillery, and was exhausted from prolonged uphill fighting. The result was decisive: all Austrian units that had reached the plateau from the center were driven off. Napoleon had known this outcome was inevitable. Even when the Austrians broke through, their position was unstable and close to collapse.

While many of his troops doubted the situation, Napoleon trusted his insight, which is why he committed fully to destroying Quasdanovich first and then returned to annihilate the center.
         3. As for Lusignan in Napoleon’s rear, events proved Napoleon correct. Lusignan was simply too late. By the time he approached, both Quasdanovich’s column and Alvinczi’s central forces had already been defeated. When Lusignan realized what had happened, he halted, only to find Rey’s division appearing behind him. Cut off and threatened with encirclement, Lusignan escaped with only about 2,000 men.
Napoleon had won Rivoli in a battle that, to observers, looked moments away from disaster, a masterpiece of perception, timing, and battlefield judgment.

They are ours.
(Tactics, Tactical Response, Observation, Deductive Reasoning, Analytical Thinking, Critical Thinking, Insight, Trap Setting, Anticipation, Prediction, Planning Flexibility, Risk Assessments, Adaptability, Convergent Thinking)
After the main battle of Rivoli, Napoleon knew the fighting wasn’t fully over. Two threats remained:
         1. Alvinczy: though defeated, he still had troops north of Rivoli and could regroup for another attack.
         2. Provera: advancing toward Mantua in the south, aiming to relieve the fortress.
To handle both simultaneously, Napoleon split his forces strategically:
         * Joubert was left in charge at Rivoli with Rey’s division added, giving him enough strength to launch a decisive attack against Alvinczy.
         * Massena and Napoleon himself moved south to confront Provera directly and stop him from breaking into Mantua.
During the night, Joubert attacked the Austrians at San Marco before Alvinczy could reorganize. By morning, the French controlled the heights. Once Rey arrived, Joubert launched a coordinated attack and outflanked the Austrians, forcing them into a chaotic retreat. Meanwhile, Provera was nearly close to Mantua, but a vigilant French sergeant spotted his disguised troops just in time. Then Napoleon arrived, and outnumbered by his concentrated forces, Provera tried to seize La Favorita as a desperate move, but was completely defeated and encircled, surrendering his division afterwards.

(Strategy Building, Planning Coverage, Risk Assessments, Anticipation)
By 3 February, the situation at Mantua was beyond recovery. The fortress had no hope left; it was collapsing under disease, famine, and death. This was Wurmser’s final attempt to hold out, and it failed. After Napoleon defeated Alvinczi and then dispatched Provera with ease, surrender became unavoidable. Napoleon treated Wurmser with respect, praising him as a soldier of “courage and constancy.” In the end, 16,000 men laid down their arms, the last remnants of an army that had once numbered 30,000. The siege of Mantua was complete and successful.
The news causes a public spectacle, announced with trumpets, proclaimed publicly to massive crowds, celebrated as a national triumph. Napoleon was no longer just a successful general, he was becoming a symbol of French glory, his reputation now rivaled the politicians of the Directory.
(Influence Building)
The Invasion of Austria:
Napoleon did not remain to enjoy his victory but immediately shifted his attention to the Papal States. Although the Pope had formally signed an armistice with France, Rome was quietly allying with Austria. Napoleon recognized this and on January 22, he ordered the French envoy Cacault to leave Rome within six hours, a calculated act designed to intimidate the Vatican.
(Ordering an envoy to leave was a powerful diplomatic signal. In the 18th century, the presence of an envoy meant that relations were still intact and negotiations were possible; recalling him showed that trust had broken down. By removing his representative, Napoleon was in this context warning the Vatican that France was close to treating Rome as an enemy.)
At the same time, he confronted Cardinal Alessandro Mattei, demanding an end to Austrian and Neapolitan influence. However, he deliberately softened the message by assuring him that the Pope would remain safe and that his religious authority would be respected.
Napoleon deliberately chose not to invade Rome.
He reasoned that if the Pope fled the city, French leverage would disappear, and if the Vatican were attacked directly, it would provoke outrage among millions of Catholics across Europe.
Instead of attacking the Church directly, Napoleon sought to control it from within. On February 1, he issued a proclamation stating that priests and monks who resisted French authority would be punished more severely than ordinary citizens. He framed this policy in Christian language, invoking the principles of the New Testament, with the aim of weakening clerical opposition, dividing compliant clergy from rebellious ones, and reducing popular uprisings inspired by religion. This amounted to psychological warfare against the Church rather than open persecution. Despite the imbalance of power, Papal forces attempted to resist. On February 3, they were defeated at Castel Bolognese, where General Victor easily crushed them, and Ancona soon fell without losses. The Papal troops, though brave, were poorly trained and equipped and stood no chance against experienced French veterans, making their resistance courageous but ultimately futile. By February 17, the Pope capitulated, and the Treaty of Tolentino formalized total Papal submission.

(Logical, Direct & Emotional Manipulation, Strategy Building, Psychological Warfare: Abusing Insecurities, Abductive Reasoning, Emotional Understanding)
Napoleon created a propaganda machine during the Italian campaign. In February 1797, he founded the Journal de Bonaparte et des Hommes Vertueux, an army-run newspaper designed not to report events, but to shape how they were understood. Its famous slogan,“Hannibal slept at Capua, but Bonaparte doesn’t sleep at Mantua”, explicitly compared him to Hannibal while implying he surpassed him. Napoleon grasped a modern truth: public opinion was a battlefield, and he would not leave its interpretation to Parisian journalists. Napoleon personally dictated articles, describing himself in epic, third-person language “Bonaparte flies like lightning and strikes like a thunderbolt” echoing Caesar and Homer. This exaggeration was intentional branding. Within days, the paper even criticized the Directory indirectly, something possible only because Napoleon allowed it and because the government already feared his popularity. The balance of power was beginning to tilt.

In Paris, his victories sparked a wave of celebrity culture: dances, cantatas, banquets, and public celebrations. Even hostile newspapers could not ignore him because he sold papers.
(Mass Manipulation, Indirect Manipulation, Influence Building, Emotional Understanding)
The war, however, was not yet over. Although Napoleon had finally taken Mantua, giving him now immense leverage, the Habsburgs chose to continue the fight rather than seek peace. This decision brought about a change in leadership, with Austria appointing the young military prodigy, Archduke Charles.
But now Napoleon enjoyed having the advantage over the Austrians, unlike at any earlier point in the campaign. He controlled strategic positions, most importantly Mantua, which gave him wide operational freedom. In addition, his army now outnumbered Austria’s. Could Archduke Charles still turn the tide of the war?
         * Napoleon's Strategy to Invade Austria:
Recognizing his clear advantage and aware that the Austrians were preparing a new offensive, Napoleon chose to strike first by carrying the war onto Austrian territory before they could act. This pre-emptive attack will thus be his strategy.
To execute it, he devised a plan based on two separate lines of advance.
         1. Napoleon himself with Massena would lead the main army through Venetian territory, aiming to cross the Tagliamento River (coincidentally moving toward the area where Archduke Charles was positioned), then Capture Osoppo (a fortress controlling mountain routes), force the Pontebba passes, march into the Drave Valley.
         2. At the same time, Joubert would advance through the Tyrolean mountains toward Carinthia, a move intended both to secure Napoleon’s flank and to threaten Austria from a second direction. Then he would advance towards Brixen, push Austrians back beyond the mountain chain, and descend down toward the Drave Valley

Although the two columns had different immediate objectives, they were ultimately meant to converge in the Drava Valley, opening a direct route toward Vienna.
(Strategy Castling, Strategy Foresight, Strategy Flexibility, Planning Coverage & Process)
         * Napoleon’s Contingency for Joubert:
On reflection, Napoleon’s plan was good in theory, yet one element clearly unsettled him. Half of his plan depended heavily on Joubert’s advance through the Tyrol and on his ability to defeat the Austrian forces there. Napoleon considered the risk: what if Joubert were beaten?
Although Napoleon gave him detailed guidance on mountain warfare, advising him, for example, to attack Botzen from the most favorable direction while taking snow, weather, and pass conditions into account, defeat was still possible. In Napoleon’s worst-case scenario, Joubert could be driven back step by step, potentially as far as Mantua.
To prepare for this, Napoleon drafted an extensive contingency plan that anticipated multiple outcomes.
He authorized Joubert, if defeated, to assume both military and political command over all of northern Italy, granting him supreme authority. Joubert’s mission would then be to hold Lombardy, secure Mantua, and prevent Austrian forces from re-entering Italy. He was to position himself between Mantua and the Po River, use the river as his main supply line, and strike Austrian columns as they advanced.

Coverage: Napoleon further strengthened this defensive framework by ordering Sahuguet to concentrate his forces at Ferrara, an example of deliberate defense-in-depth rather than panic.
In essence, Napoleon’s contingency plan provided Joubert with a complete guide on how to hold on after defeat, buying time until Napoleon could return and support him.
(Planning Contingency, Planning Coverage, Planning Applicability, Overall Foresight)
         * Battle of Valvasone (Archduke Charles and Napoleon's first ever encounter):
As Napoleon advanced eastward, he did so on a broad front rather than in a single concentrated column, deliberately forcing the Austrians to disperse their forces. When he reached the Tagliamento River, he found Archduke Charles positioned on the opposite bank.
Note: It is important to know that Archduke Charles’s objective at this stage was to gain time until the rest of his army could arrive. The Austrians were still in the process of preparing their defenses, while Napoleon had already gone fully on the offensive. As a result, Charles initially adopted a defensive posture.
Napoleon quickly identified a favorable crossing point near Valvasone, where the river was shallow and broken by islands that offered cover. His army crossed rapidly and struck the Austrian positions with force. The Austrian line collapsed, and Charles was compelled to retreat, an engagement known as the Battle of Valvasone, which ended in a French victory.

At the same time, Joubert achieved success in the Tyrol and advanced toward the planned rendezvous, meaning Napoleon had no need to activate his contingency plans.
As Archduke Charles withdrew, Napoleon sent Bernadotte in pursuit while directing Masséna to cut off the Austrian retreat at Tarvis and the defenses there. Despite severe difficulties in the terrain, Masséna reached Tarvis and, through bold and skillful action, destroyed the Austrian forces there. When Charles arrived at Tarvis, he found his troops already in flight. Reduced to fragments of his original army, he fell back to Klagenfurt in an attempt to reorganize. However, with Napoleon and Masséna closing in, he was unable to do so and was forced to retreat again. Napoleon then entered the Drava Valley after repeatedly outmaneuvering and humiliating Charles. Joubert soon arrived as planned, and the two French forces finally united.
(Tactics, Observation)
The Austrians attempted to make a stand in the gorges near Neumarkt, where the terrain strongly favored defense. Despite this, the French broke through the center after hard fighting, forcing the Austrians to retreat until the last major natural barrier before Vienna. Napoleon was now on the direct road to the capital. A final Austrian stand failed, and the French advance reached Leoben.
         * The End of the War Of The First Coalition:
At Judenberg, Napoleon and the Austrian envoys agreed to a suspension of hostilities, after which Napoleon unilaterally negotiated peace, signing the Preliminaries of Leoben. Following the agreement, Napoleon withdrew his army into Venetian territory
Napoleon had won the WAR OF THE FIRST COALITION.
Militarily, Napoleon had achieved total victory. Strategically, he was nearing the limits of what success could sustain. Politically, he seized the opportunity to dictate terms, acting as a sovereign rather than merely a general.
This moment marks the turning point when Napoleon ceases to be just a brilliant young commander and begins to emerge as a European power in his own right.
By this point, Napoleon had:
Crossed Apennines and the Alps, defeated Sardinia, 6 Austrian armies, inflicted 120,000 casualties, forced peace on, The Pope, Piedmont, Naples and abolished the Duchy of Modena
