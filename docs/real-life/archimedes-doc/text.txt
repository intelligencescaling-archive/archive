How Archimedes Almost Broke Math with Circles

In the 3rd century BC, Archimedes proved the area formula of the circle. This was a very big challenge back then because a circle is curved and this was 2000 years before stuff like calculus was invented. Back then, they could only find areas of shapes with straight lines, sure some breakthroughs regarding circles had been made by people before him, but none found out how to derive its area. He also identified pi as we know it today.

Archimedes did this by dividing a circle into pieces and realized that the more slices he cut the circle into, the more and more it would resemble a rectangle with lengths of half a circumference and the radius. From what he saw, the circle would be a perfect rectangle with side lengths C/2 and R. So ofc the formula would be A = R*(C/2) aka A = R*Pi*R = Pi*R^2, the formula we know today.

However, this did cause a paradox because well wouldn't an infinitesimal slice of something be 0? How can it be anything other than 0 and if they weren't each 0, wouldn't adding an infinite amount of them give infinity? That is what the ancients had a problem with. Another strange thing was how a collection of infinite things added up to a different number compared to a collection of other infinite things.

Archimedes thus decided to technically use infinity without using infinity. He decided to do his proof instead of a method by Eudoxus, aka the method of exhaustion, showing the area of the circle was neither bigger than the rectangle nor smaller than the rectangle and therefore had to be the exact same size.

Archimedes actually formed the sectors of his circle from a right triangle, not a rectangle, so he technically did it in a way that is harder to visualize for us, according to the creator. Still though, he did it in a way that we would consider unconventional, as most of us would know of only the way to do it with rectangles, not right triangles.

It was only until the 16th and 17th century in Europe that major intellectual figures engaged with the idea of the infinitely small, as his works were either lost or just not that spread, but detractors still existed and the Jesuits wanted to ban the concept from ever being taught.

Keep in mind, this concept, the method of exhaustion and the infinitely small slices of circle that were used by Archimedes were actually sort of precursors to calculus. Newton would develop calculus based on intensive study of the works of Archimedes, which did show the fact that they did have a role of being precursors to calculus and sort of introduced notions that would be used in it thousands of years before. Newton stood on the shoulders of Archimedes.

Also using the method of exhaustion, he created a shape with 96 sides and narrowed the value of pi to be between 3.1408 and 3.1428. With the method of exhaustion, he also used to narrow down the value of the sqrt(3), concluding it was between 1.732026 and 1.732051. That is how close he was.

The Sand Reckoner

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sand_Reckoner

Archimedes set out to figure out the number of grains of sands that would fit inside the entire Universe. However, he soon faced a problem. The first problem was that the counting system of his time just could not create such large numbers. It could go only up to a myriad, aka 10 000.

To solve this problem, he used the fact that by utilizing the word myriad, he can make a number that is called a myriad myriads, or 10^8, so roughly 100 000 000. So Archimedes then called the numbers up to 10^8 the first order and 10^8 itself the unit of the second order. Multiples of the unit became the second order, so for example 10^8*10^8 = 10^16. This 10^16 became the unit of the third order whose multiples were the third order and so on. Archimedes continued naming numbers in this way up to a myriad-myriad times the unit of the 10^8-th order so (10^8)^(10^8).

After having done this, Archimedes called the orders he had defined orders of the first period and called the last one the unit of the second period. He then constructed the orders of the second period by taking multiples of this unit, just like how he did with the unit of the first order and so on. He then arrived at the myriad-myriadth period. The largest number constructed by Archimedes was the last number in this period, which was roughly equivalent to 10^(8*10^16), which would be 10^(80 000 000 000 000 000), an insanely large number, that basically makes the number of atoms in the universe look like nothing in comparison.

He had to make assumptions, that were just out of thin air cuz he obviously could not prove them, but they were: The universe was spherical and that the ratio of the diameter of the Universe to the diameter of the orbit of the Earth around the Sun equalled the ratio of the diameter of the orbit of the Earth around the Sun to the diameter of the Earth.

In order to obtain an upper bound, Archimedes made the following assumptions of their dimensions:
* that the perimeter of the Earth was no bigger than 300 myriad stadia (5.55·105 km, an overestimate by roughly a factor of 40).
* that the Moon was no larger than the Earth, and that the Sun was no more than thirty times larger than the Moon (1.65·107 km, an overestimate by roughly a factor of 10)
* that the angular diameter of the Sun, as seen from the Earth, was greater than 1/200 of a right angle (π/400 radians = 0.45° degrees, an over estimate, but accurate to within 20% of the true value).
Archimedes then concluded that the diameter of the Universe was no more than 1014 stadia (in modern units, about 2 light years), and that it would require no more than 1063 grains of sand to fill it. With these measurements, each grain of sand in Archimedes's thought-experiment would have been approximately 19 μm (0.019 mm) in diameter.

Archimedes Principle

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_principle

In his work On Floating Bodies from 246 BCE, Archimedes would suggest that any object, totally or partially immersed in a fluid or liquid, is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.

Considering the cuboid as shown in the image, we will see that the fluid will exert a normal force on each face, but only the normal forces on top and bottom will contribute to buoyancy. The pressure difference between the top and bottom is directly proportional to the height. Multiplying the pressure difference by the area of a face gives a net force on the cuboid - the buoyancy - equaling in magnitude the weight of the fluid displaced by the cuboid.

By summing up sufficiently many arbitrarily small cuboids this reasoning may be extended to irregular shapes, and so, whatever the shape of the submerged body, the buoyant force is equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.

The formula generated from this can be expressed in the following equation: Fa = ρgV.

Fa is the buoyant force, rho is the density of the fluid and V is the volume of the displaced fluid and g is the acceleration due to gravity.

Of course, Archimedes wrote it more like weight of displaced fluid = weight of object in vacuum - apparent weight of object in fluid. Like seriously, mathematical notation was so much different back then.

He would also create the Principle of Flotation which states that any floating object displaces its own weight of fluid.

Yea, he also created another principle during this work, like the principle we know of was not all he did in this field of hydrostatics.
