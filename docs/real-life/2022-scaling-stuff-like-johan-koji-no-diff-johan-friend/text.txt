Entry into Politics

In July 1919, Hitler was appointed as an intelligence agent in a reconnaissance unit of the Reichswehr. This position involved the task of influencing other soldiers and infiltrating the German Workers' Party (DAP), suggesting recognition by his superiors of his abilities to persuade and manipulate.

Hitler demonstrated a remarkable ability to adapt to various social environments.

During a DAP meeting on September 12, 1919, the party's president, Anton Drexler, was impressed by Hitler's oratory skills.

This indicates that Hitler possessed an innate talent for public speaking and moving the masses. He was a passionate and convincing speaker, capable of evoking strong emotions in his audience.

Drexler handed Hitler a pamphlet titled "My Political Awakening," which contained antisemitic, nationalist, anticapitalist, and antimarxist ideas. Acting quickly, that same month Hitler joined the party and, a few weeks later, on October 16, delivered his first public speech at an event attended by 111 people, including Röhm, who would later join the party as well. Finally, on April 1, 1920, the DAP changed its name to NSDAP (National Socialist German Workers' Party); on the same date, Hitler left the army, and a year later he would be elected leader of the NSDAP.

Reichstag Fire

The Reichstag fire in 1933 has been the subject of numerous theories and speculations regarding its origin and the individuals responsible behind the event. However, the most accepted version, supported by historians like William L. Shirer and Alan Bullock, suggests that Hitler and his inner circle were involved in planning the fire.

Although a communist named Marinus van der Lubbe was found at the scene of the fire, it is believed he was used as a scapegoat by the Nazis to blame the Communists for the attack.

According to this theory, the true mastermind behind the fire would have been Hermann Göring, a prominent Nazi leader and Hitler's right-hand man.

To bolster this hypothesis, it is mentioned that Hitler ordered the elimination of all those who knew the truth about the fire, including the execution of van der Lubbe by the Nazi government.

Furthermore, it is highlighted that Hitler had a well-planned alibi, as during the fire he was dining with Joseph Goebbels, another important Nazi leader. This could be interpreted as a contingency plan to deflect suspicion in case people questioned his possible involvement.

Lastly, Hitler's carefully calculated reaction upon arriving at the scene of the fire is emphasized, showing himself scared and concerned, as if he didn't know what was happening. He seized the situation to directly accuse the Communists of attempting a coup, which not only distanced him from being considered a suspect but also allowed him to use the event to discredit his political opponents.

After the Reichstag fire, Hitler seized the event to launch a smear campaign against the Communists, accusing them of being responsible for the attack.

Strategically using the term "coup," Hitler managed to instill fear in the population and portray the Communists as a serious threat to the stability of the German government.

Under this narrative, Hitler pressured President Paul von Hindenburg to issue the Reichstag Fire Decree, a measure that suspended civil liberties and authorized a "ruthless confrontation" against the Communists. This decree was crucial in paving the way for the establishment of Nazi Germany.

Once the decree was issued, the police, which was already under the control of Hitler's Nazi Party, carried out mass arrests of communists, including all communist delegates of the Reichstag. This had two significant consequences:

Hitler accumulated more power after the decree, as it allowed him to act without the constraints of civil liberties and to pursue his political opponents.
Communist participation in the elections on March 5 was severely hindered, weakening their position and reducing their capacity for opposition.

Night of the long Knives

The President Hindenburg, through General von Blomberg, issued an ultimatum to Hitler: if the state of tension in Germany was not quickly resolved, martial law would be declared, and control of the state would be handed over to the Army. This situation reveals Hitler's precarious position at that time, as he depended on the support of the Army and the president to maintain his power. Hitler was forced to make a crucial decision: suppress the SA and halt the revolution that the leaders of the stormtroopers were pressing for, or risk losing control of the government. The pressure from the Army, backed by the venerable president, left him with no other option if he wanted to survive politically. This dilemma shows Hitler's pragmatic mindset, willing to sacrifice former allies like Röhm (one of his closest friends) and the SA to maintain his position of power. In the end, Hitler made the difficult decision to carry out a purge against SA members (Sturmabteilung).

To carry out this Machiavellian plan, Hitler and his collaborators fabricated false evidence suggesting that Ernst Röhm, leader of the SA, had been bribed by France to overthrow Hitler. These pieces of evidence were secretly concocted by Heinrich Himmler and Reinhard Heydrich under Hitler's direct orders, with the aim of justifying the purge to the public opinion and convincing hesitant Nazi leaders of the necessity to act against the SA.

Furthermore, Hitler claimed to have received urgent messages, probably sent by Hermann Göring and Himmler, warning of an imminent uprising by the SA in both Berlin and Munich. Although these messages are believed to have been greatly exaggerated, Hitler used them as a pretext to justify a violent and decisive intervention against the SA.
Once all the false evidence was prepared, Hitler set a trap for the members of the SA, inviting them to the Hanselbauer Hotel in Bad Wiessee, Bavaria. It was there that Hitler planned to confine them before carrying out the purge. In fact, Hitler secured strategic positions in the hotel to prevent anyone from escaping during the purge.
In addition to the previously prepared false evidence, Hitler and his followers used the homosexuality of certain leaders of the SA, such as Ernst Röhm and Edmund Heines, to further discredit them and legitimize their extermination. During the purge, Hitler and his allies resorted to extrajudicial violence and murder to eliminate not only the leaders of the SA, but also other political enemies, such as Gregor Strasser and Kurt von Schleicher. These cold-blooded murders set a terrifying precedent for the terror that would characterize the Third Reich's government.

After the purge, Hitler manipulated public opinion and covered up the true nature of it. In a speech delivered in the Reichstag, he justified his actions as necessary to defend Germany from an alleged conspiracy by Röhm and the SA. In this way, Hitler used propaganda and information manipulation to legitimize his violent actions and consolidate his power.

Finally, to conclude the Night of the Long Knives, Hitler instructed Joseph Goebbels to initiate a campaign aimed at further improving and highlighting his image and the acts he had carried out.

Annexation of Czechoslovakia

The annexation of Czechoslovakia is fascinating, as Hitler achieved its surrender through manipulation and threats, demonstrating his tactical brilliance. It all began with the meeting between Hitler and Hácha, but it was the German leader who controlled the encounter, making Hácha come to him.

This action demonstrates Hitler's complete mastery of the situation.

Even Hitler intentionally made Hácha wait, despite the Czech president's illness and fatigue due to travel, which was another strategy to weaken him, likely aware of Hácha's advanced age and physical frailty. Furthermore, this tactic sought to psychologically undermine Hácha, making him feel inferior and less relevant. By prioritizing watching a romantic comedy, Hitler also showed his disdain for the situation and his confidence in his position of power.

When he finally met with Hácha, Hitler launched into a furious diatribe, insisting that the only way to protect the Reich was to immediately occupy Czech territory. His goal was to intimidate and overwhelm Hácha, leaving him feeling that he had no choice but to submit to Hitler's demands. He threatened bloodshed if Hácha did not order the Czech armed forces not to resist the invasion, seeking to instill fear and make him feel responsible for the consequences. Additionally, Hitler added the threat of bombing Prague, backed by Göring's confirmation of the readiness of planes in strategic locations to carry out the attack.

This strategy aimed to increase the pressure on Hácha, making him believe that the situation was even more serious and urgent. The combination of waiting, the furious diatribe, threats, and constant pressure led Hácha to an emotional collapse. All this psychological game sought to bend his will and make him more prone to giving in to Hitler's demands.

When Hácha fainted, Hitler called his personal doctor to administer an injection. After recovering, Hácha surrendered, and Czechoslovakia fell into the hands of the Nazis.

Adolf Hitler, in early 1938, demonstrated his cunning and ability to manipulate situations to his advantage by using blackmail to consolidate his control over the German army. In the incident known as the "Blomberg-Fritsch," Hitler cleverly exploited compromising information about the Minister of War, Field Marshal Werner von Blomberg, and the army commander, Colonel General Werner von Fritsch, to force their resignations.

Hitler used a police record revealing that Blomberg's new wife had a history of prostitution, allowing him to pressure Blomberg into resigning. By exposing Blomberg's dirty laundry, Hitler was able to present his removal as a necessary measure to maintain the integrity and morale of the army, thus demonstrating his ability to manipulate public opinion and justify his decisions.
On the other hand, army commander Fritsch was dismissed after the SS (Schutzstaffel) alleged that he had engaged in a homosexual relationship. This accusation, regardless of its veracity, was used by Hitler to get rid of Fritsch, who opposed his plans to prepare the army for war as early as 1938. Hitler's intolerance towards any form of dissent or challenge to his authority is evident in this incident, revealing his obsession with absolute loyalty. For Hitler, anyone who did not fully align with his vision and goals had to be eliminated.
Outcome: After the forced resignations of Blomberg and Fritsch, Hitler personally assumed the title of Commander in Chief, thus taking direct command of the armed forces.

The Blomberg-Fritsch incident is a clear example of how Hitler used blackmail and manipulation to consolidate his power. By exploiting compromising information about his opponents, Hitler was able to force their resignations and present his actions as justified to the public. This also reveals Hitler's psychology, his obsession with loyalty, his intolerance of dissent, and his insatiable desire for power and control.
