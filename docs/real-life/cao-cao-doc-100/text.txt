Collapse of the Han Dynasty

Context: He Jin and the Empress Dowager conspired to eliminate the eunuchs at Yuan Shao's urging. This was largely driven by widespread resentment toward eunuchs among officials and noble families, who viewed them as a corrupt and destabilizing force that had wielded excessive influence at court for far too long.
Cao Cao, however, pushed back against the idea of a total purge. While he acknowledged that some eunuchs were indeed corrupt, he believed a full-scale purge was unnecessary and dangerous. His position was that only the most egregious offenders should be removed, not the entire class. A total elimination would provoke an aggressive backlash. He also cautioned against inviting regional warlords to assist with the purge, arguing that bringing in outside military forces was an unnecessary risk that could destabilize the state. Worse, involving outsiders would likely cause the plan to leak.
As it turned out, Cao Cao's predictions proved accurate on every count. The eunuchs discovered the conspiracy and responded with hostility. He Jin was assassinated before he could carry out his plan. And when the regional warlords were summoned anyway, one of them, Dong Zhuo, marched his army into the capital and seized power for himself, plunging the Han dynasty into chaos.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, p 45.
Chen Shou and Pei Songzhi, Sanguozhi 429, 1.5.

During the coalition campaign against Dong Zhuo in 190–191 CE, Yuan Shao proposed: elevating Liu Yu, a member of the imperial clan serving as Governor of You Province, to the position of a rival emperor, essentially to counter Emperor Xian, who remained under Dong Zhuo's thumb in Chang'an.
Cao Cao pushed back against this. For him, Emperor Xian, whatever his circumstances, was still the biological son of Emperor Ling and therefore the lawful ruler of Han. The coalition fighting Dong Zhuo derived its moral authority precisely from the claim that it was defending the legitimate Han order against a usurper. Installing a second emperor would shatter that justification entirely, the coalition would no longer look like loyalists rescuing a captive ruler, but rather like yet another faction exploiting the chaos for its own ends, no different from Dong Zhuo himself. It would hand their enemies a powerful argument and fracture the already fragile unity of the alliance.
There was also a deeper suspicion at work.  From Yuan Shao’s argument, it appears that Cao Cao had recognized that Yuan Shao's proposal was less about solving a political problem and more about personal leverage, thus ambition; a compliant emperor of Yuan Shao's own choosing would give him a tool for dominating the coalition and eventually the court itself.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, pp 57-58.

Consolidation Era (The Rise of Cao Cao)

Black Mountain Campaign
Context: When Cao Cao was appointed as Administrator of Dong Commandery, he faced an immediate crisis: relentless raids from the Black Mountain bandits, a force tens of thousands strong and hardened by years of insurgency. At this stage, Cao Cao was still a minor warlord, with a fragile power base. One of the bandit leaders, Poison Yu, launched a raid directly against Dongwuyang, Cao Cao’s capital.
Many assumed Cao Cao would rush to its defense. Instead, he refused to take the bait. Rather than defending, he drove his forces rapidly into the Taihang Mountains to strike the enemy’s home bases. Cao Cao thus compels Poison Yu to abandon his offensive and return to his base, but it was late, when he finally arrived Cao Cao not had defeated various Black Mountain groups, but also the Xiongnu chieftain Yufuluo at Neihuang in the south of Wei commandery
Dongwuyang was saved without a siege, and the Black Mountain bandits were expelled from Dong Commandery.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, p 62.

Context: Another major threat appears, which are Remnants of the Yellow Turban Rebellion, a massive bandit army (possibly hundreds of thousands).
While out on a scouting mission, he unexpectedly stumbled into the enemy’s main camp. With only a 1000 men, far too few for such a force, he was defeated. Yet the setback brought a crucial advantage: intelligence. From Bao Xin, who would later fall in battle, Cao Cao learned that although the rebels were vast in number, they were desperately under-supplied, surviving only through plunder. Bao Xin advised him to adopt a defensive strategy.
Cao Cao took this advice to heart. After the defeat, he reorganized his forces by caring for his men to build morale, instituting strict discipline through clear rewards and punishments, and refusing large direct clashes. Instead, he focused on ambushes, surprise strikes, and relentless day-and-night skirmishes. This war of attrition steadily broke the Yellow Turbans’ strength. In time, the rebels collapsed. Cao Cao pursued them into Jibei, and in the winter of 192/193, he secured their mass surrender: 300,000 soldiers and 700,000 dependents. From these he selected the best fighters to form a permanent unit, the famed Qingzhou Troops, who would become the backbone of his armies.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, pp 64-66.

After repeated defeats, the Yellow Turban remnants were worn down and desperate. Cao Cao offered what they lacked: safety, legitimacy, and stable livelihood, far preferable to their endless cycle of plunder. The result was a mass submission, with the rebels reorganized into the Qingzhou Troops, now serving directly under his command.

Context: With a strengthened army and a stable base of manpower, Cao Cao transitioned from survival to expansion. His growing power now allowed him to act offensively, where personal grievances and political opportunity could be used to justify broader campaigns.
After Tao Qian was implicated in the death of his father, Cao Cao seized the opportunity to launch an invasion.
In truth, the killing had been carried out by an officer named Zhang Kai, who then fled south of the Huai, but Cao Cao used the event as a pretext for “vengeance.”
Playing this role, he struck into Tao Qian’s Xu Province, bypassing towns and driving straight into the heart of enemy territory. His offensive was ruthless and rapid. He stormed ten cities, crushed Tao Qian’s army at Pengcheng, and forced Tao Qian to retreat to his capital. Cao Cao pressed forward mercilessly, leaving devastation in his wake, towns emptied, inhabitants slaughtered, and even animals wiped out. Although he soon withdrew to Yan Province without completing the conquest, Xu was left shattered. Within weeks, Cao Cao returned and renewed his assault, even besieging Tan, Tao Qian’s capital. Victory was nearly within reach, but events at home derailed the campaign. His trusted lieutenant Chen Gong and old ally Zhang Miao turned against him, appalled by the scale of his massacres, and betrayed him. The betrayal forced him to abandon the offensive, and it also drew Lü Bu into the conflict as a new enemy.

In late 195, the fifteen-year-old Emperor Xian of Han fled in desperation. As in his earlier arguments over legitimacy with Yuan Shao, this shows that Cao Cao understood that whoever controlled the emperor would gain immense political legitimacy. His initial efforts to bring the emperor under his influence failed, as Xian was protected by Yang Feng, Dong Cheng, and Han Xian. However, Cao Cao eventually prevailed through the strategy of Dong Zhao, who engineered a separation between the emperor and his escorts and arranged their ambush and elimination. From that point onward, the emperor became Cao Cao’s puppet, ruler in name, but captive in reality. Installed as a nominal sovereign, he served as a tool of Cao Cao’s authority.
(Influence Building)
Cao Cao’s tuntian system resettled refugees, surrendered bandits, and displaced peasants onto abandoned land, where they farmed with state-provided oxen and tools while also defending themselves. Instead of paying taxes like in the old Han system, they handed over a share of their harvest (50–60%) directly to the government, bypassing landlords and corrupt officials. This gave people stability, tied them personally to Cao Cao’s regime, and turned wastelands back into productive farmland. At the same time, it provided a steady, reliable food supply for his armies, the lifeblood of war. In effect, Cao Cao became the largest landholder in northern China, with the people as his tenants. It was a nice move because he solved famine, rebuilt war-torn territory, created loyal subjects, and secured military logistics all at once.

Peripheral Isolation Strategy
Context: As previously mentioned, Cao Cao was at this moment consolidating power by leveraging Emperor Xian as a source of political legitimacy. What is easy to overlook, however, is that from the very beginning, as far back as his Black Mountain campaign, Cao Cao was in an environment completely hemmed in by rival powers, each threatening him from a different direction. He was not dealing with a single enemy but with multiple competing warlords. This reality made his strategy far more fragile and complex than it might appear on the surface. Using Emperor Xian as a political instrument could only work if every surrounding threat was kept in check, one unchecked rival could unravel everything. Yet directly confronting each enemy through open warfare was neither practical nor efficient given his resources. So rather than attempting to defeat his rivals one by one on the battlefield, Cao Cao develops a Strategy, a more subtle one, where he sought to keep each threat neutralized, occupied elsewhere, turned against one another, or simply kept at a distance, buying him the time and stability needed to quietly consolidate his own power base.
1. Yuan Shu
Yuan Shu had already begun to undermine his own position through a series of blunders, like when he tried to declare himself as emperor. This act ruined his legitimacy in the eyes of everyone. Cao Cao thus did not need to defeat this enemy, just make sure he falls and remains isolated. Thus Cao Cao worked to cut off any potential support. He formally recognized Sun Ce's authority under the Han banner, and then maneuvered Lü Bu away from him, ensuring that Yuan Shu would face his eventual downfall.
2. Lü Bu
Lü Bu was a dangerous threat to confront head-on. Cao Cao thus does 3 things here.
1. He showed Lü Bu with prestigious titles and honorifics to keep him placated, while quietly denying him anything that would translate into real political or territorial power, like for example the governorship of Xu, which was dangled but never granted.
2. Cao Cao also puts Liu Bei into position as a proxy adversary, forcing Lü Bu to drain his resources fighting yet another rival rather than turning his attention toward Cao Cao himself.
3. Cao Cao lastly monitors Lü Bu's camp, by planting informants, ensuring he was never caught off guard.
3. Sun Ce
Sun Ce had previously been aligned with Yuan Shu as mentioned before, but Cao Cao moved to bring him into his own fold. He granted Sun Ce official titles and even sealed the relationship through a marriage alliance, giving him the prestige of being recognized as a Han general. But Cao Cao also  simultaneously installed his own loyalist, Chen Yu, as administrator in the heart of Sun Ce's own territory, a quiet but deliberate move to undercut his authority from within. This forced Sun Ce to expend energy proving his worth and fighting to assert control over land he nominally already held, keeping him too preoccupied with his own affairs to pose any serious threat to Cao Cao.
4. Liu Bei
Though comparatively weaker at this stage, Liu Bei occupied a useful strategic position. His reputation as a loyalist of the Han made him politically valuable, and his forces, once reinforced, could be directed toward contested regions. By appointing him to governorship and supplying him with troops, Cao Cao enabled Liu Bei to re-establish himself in Pei, where he functioned as a counterweight to Lü Bu.
5. Yuan Shao
Lastly, Cao Cao’s most formidable opponent, Yuan Shao. However, his attention was divided by ongoing operations against Gongsun Zan in the north, and he hesitated to engage immediately. Cao Cao avoided confrontation, even humbling himself by lowering his own rank to placate him. This bought him precious time to consolidate Xu and eliminate the lesser threats first.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, pp 102-117.

Context: There came a moment when, despite his earlier gains, Cao Cao was in dire peril. His crushing defeat against Zhang Xiu had left him exposed, and his advisers Guo Jia and Xun Yu warned him of the danger: if Yuan Shao in the north and Lü Bu in the east ever combined their forces, Cao Cao would be caught between them and annihilated. War had not started yet, but his strategist informed Cao Cao the position he was in, and how it could affect him later on, if the situation turned dire.
Cao Cao's Strategy: Isolate and Destroy Lü Bu
Cao Cao's objective therefore would be to eliminate Lü Bu before any alliance with Yuan Shao could be forged, even if it was not against him. After all, Yuan Shao was still not a threat, and he was too busy in the north. But Lü Bu could not simply be charged directly (As mentioned before, he was much stronger than Cao Cao.) Thus Cao Cao reasoned that he first had to be isolated, stripped of any potential support, and left with nowhere to turn. To accomplish this, Cao Cao embarked on a campaign to seal every flank and neutralize every threat before making his final move.
Cao Cao’s Planning Coverage:
1. Diplomatically: To the west, Cao Cao deployed diplomacy rather than war. He sent Zhong Yao to negotiate with the frontier warlords, and through careful political maneuvering, extracted their sons as hostages, effectively buying their loyalty, or at least their neutrality, and ensuring they would not cause trouble while his attention was directed elsewhere.
2. Through War: In the south however, he turned on Yuan Shu, whose domain was already decaying from internal rot. Cao Cao delivered a sharp, decisive blow, routing his forces and driving him back across the Huai River. Yuan Shu never recovered, fading into irrelevance and posing no further threat.
But the south was not entirely clean. Zhang Xiu, the man who had previously humiliated Cao Cao in battle, still held Nanyang, and this could not be left unresolved. Unlike the other fronts, this one could not be settled through diplomacy or a single sharp strike. It had to be fought through. Cao Cao first seized Wan city, pushing Liu Biao's supporting forces back to the Han River and severing that connection. He then pressed the siege against Zhang Xiu at Rang. But the situation deteriorated rapidly when Liu Biao's reinforcements arrived, catching Cao Cao compressed between two hostile armies with no clean line of escape.
The Battle at Anzhong (198):
Rather than panicking or doubling down on a failing position, he improvised a small plan. He broke off the siege and retreated, but as the pursuing armies chased him, they ran straight into ambushes he had seeded along the road bleeding the enemy until they finally cornered him. And then finally, when he found himself cornered on what he himself described as a "death ground", terrain with no avenue of withdrawal he stopped and turned to fight. This was his plan all along, knowing his men had no escape in that position, he weaponized that desperation, transforming the absence of hope into raw, savage fighting courage. A psychological trick that Han Xin famously did at Battle of Jingxing, where fighting with one's back to the river left soldiers no choice but to win. The gamble paid off. Cao Cao reversed the situation entirely, inflicted severe casualties, and stabilized the front.
The result was not a sweeping conquest, but it didn't need to be. Zhang Xiu retreated into a defensive posture, Liu Biao withdrew from open engagement, and Nanyang was effectively neutralized. All his flanks were now secure. The stage was set for the second Phase.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, pp 106-108.

The End of Lü Bu
With his perimeter sealed and his rear protected, Cao Cao unleashed himself against Lü Bu.
By 198, Lü Bu had driven Liu Bei (who remember was fighting against Lü Bu because of Cao Cao) out of Pei and occupied his territory, unsettling the smaller warlords of the region. Cao Cao marched east, absorbed Liu Bei's scattered remnants into his force, and advanced on Xiapi.
Lü Bu planned to bleed Cao Cao's army in the river valleys and strike when they were spent.
But Cao Cao gave him no such opportunity, he drove straight through, linked up with Chen Deng's forces, and hammered Lü Bu's armies in engagement after engagement, stripping away his strength battle by battle until Lü Bu had no choice but to retreat behind the walls of Xiapi.
Then advised by his strategists, Cao Cao diverted the Si River, the very waterway Lü Bu had intended to weaponize against him, and used it to flood the city. Xiapi became an island. With no relief coming and no way out, the siege turned inward. Suspicion spread through Lü Bu's camp like a disease. When Lü Bu, under the pressure of isolation and paranoia, began lashing out at his own officers and accusing them of conspiracy, he made his fatal mistake, he turned his suspicion into a self-fulfilling prophecy. His officers, now genuinely threatened, opened the city gates themselves and handed Cao Cao the victory.
In the end, as everything collapsed around him, Lü Bu knelt before Cao Cao and begged for his life, but Cao Cao would execute him.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, pp 106-108.

The Guandu Campaign

Guandu Campaign Preparations
After Yuan Shao finished off Gongsun Zan in the north, Cao Cao inferred correctly that he would be next. So even before the war began, he started preparing carefully. He placed forward positions along the Yellow River, but anchored his main defense further back at Guandu. His choice of Guandu shows very good reasoning as Yuan Shao held a massive numerical advantage, and in open terrain that kind of superiority is nearly impossible to overcome, he could simply outflank and overwhelm (and Cao Cao probably foresaw this.)
But Guandu neutralized all of that.
1. With marshland on one side and waterways and flooded ground on the other, Yuan Shao's enormous army had nowhere to spread. All that manpower got funneled into a narrow front, where Cao Cao could concentrate his smaller force and hold the line. The numbers were still there, but Yuan Shao couldn't use them properly.
2. Then there was the terrain itself. Guandu sat on elevated ground, which meant any assault on Cao Cao's position required attackers to push uphill while defenders rained down on them. That kind of fighting bleeds the attacker far more than the defender, so every charge Yuan Shao ordered would cost him disproportionately.
3. Finally, the logistics. Guandu was near the Bian River, connected to the canal network, and had Xu close behind as a major supply base. Cao Cao's supply lines stayed short and secure, while Yuan Shao's stretched long and vulnerable the further he pushed.
What makes this so impressive is that the Ward hadn't even started yet, and Cao Cao had already thought through all of it. He anticipated the threat, chose his ground deliberately, and built a defensive system designed to turn Yuan Shao's greatest strengths against him.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD, p 124.

Pre-Guandu Strategic Consolidation
With Yuan Shao actively building alliances and regional figures defecting to his side, Cao Cao probably recognized that marching north immediately would leave him dangerously exposed. His policy was therefore to first systematically neutralize every surrounding threat through a combination of military force, political maneuvering, and strategic appointments.
In terms of dealing with people Cao Cao would do the following:
1. Liu Bei had recently turned against Cao Cao and allied with Yuan Shao. Before he could pose a serious threat, however, Cao Cao launched a lightning campaign and swiftly defeated Liu Bei at Pei, capturing both Xiapi and Guan Yu. Notably, Cao Cao did not simply neutralize this threat, he turned it into an opportunity. By treating Guan Yu well and granting him command, he effectively gained a capable ally from a former enemy, while Liu Bei was left with no choice but to flee to Yuan Shao.
2. Cao Cao likely also kept Liu Zhang and Zhang Lu in mind as potential threats, though both he and we can probably infer that he considered them too geographically distant to be an immediate concern.
3. As for Zhang Xiu, who had previously fought against Cao Cao, this situation proved relatively straightforward to resolve. Zhang Xiu personally submitted to Cao Cao after his adviser Jia Xu persuaded him that doing so was in his best interest.
In terms of securing his flanks, Cao Cao would do the following:
1. Cao Cao also stabilized the west and secured his rear:
   * Xiahou Dun was given control of Henan
   * Zhong Yao was tasked with stabilizing Chang'an
2. There were also territories that still needed to be secured, particularly in the north. Sui Gu had allied with Yuan Shao, but Cao Cao dispatched Cao Ren, who crushed him in battle. Cao Cao then appointed Wei Chong to govern the region in his place.
3. On the eastern flank, there was Zang Ba, who had previously been allied with Lu Bu. This situation proved easy to resolve, as Zang Ba willingly submitted to Cao Cao. Cao Cao shrewdly took advantage of this by granting him regional autonomy, while also ordering him to launch attacks into Yuan Shao's territory. This move not only secured the eastern flank but also served to distract Yuan Shao and force him to spread his forces thin.
4. Finally, there was Yan , the homeland of the Yuan family, and therefore a region filled with their supporters and sympathizers. To deal with this, Cao Cao dispatched Man Chong, who destroyed around twenty enemy camps and lured local leaders to a meeting, where he had them killed, effectively eliminating any organized resistance in the area.
Much of this was not strictly pre-planned; rather, it consisted of tactical actions carried out in the name of his Strategic policy of neutralizing threats and securing his position. However, this strategy clearly formed part of his Guandu Campaign Preparations plan.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–128 AD, p 124.

The Guandu Campaign

Context: Yuan Shao launched a massive propaganda effort against Cao Cao, portraying him as corrupt and villainous. This shook Cao Cao’s allies and gave Yuan Shao both the moral advantage and greater resources. Yuan Shao made the first move, advancing his huge army to Liyang, south of Ji Province, and immediately sending Yan Liang to strike at Boma, a vital stronghold of Cao Cao north to the Yellow River.
Cao Cao rapidly identified what Yuan Shao wanted to do, and the importance of Boma, as the stronghold controlled movement in the Yellow River.
Cao Cao responded, but rather than rushing to defend Boma directly, Cao Cao used deception. He marched his army north toward Yan Crossing, deliberately creating the impression that he intended to cross the river and strike at Yuan Shao's flank. Yuan Shao took the bait and diverted forces to counter the perceived threat. Cao Cao then abruptly reversed course, moving rapidly with a small, mobile strike force back toward Boma. The sudden attack caught Yan Liang completely off guard, his army was destroyed and he was killed in the engagement, relieving the siege entirely.

The Battle of Guandu (OPERATIONAL SITUATION)
Context: By the time autumn arrived, the opposing forces of Cao Cao and Yuan Shao had fully concentrated around the Battle of Guandu, establishing fixed positions for what both sides understood would become the decisive confrontation of the campaign.
For roughly two months, neither army committed to a full-scale battle. Instead, they engaged in a prolonged phase of skirmishing, probing attacks, and logistical warfare. Small detachments clashed frequently, and both sides attempted to disrupt each other’s supply lines through raids and harassment. Despite these continuous efforts, the situation remained strategically deadlocked: neither Cao Cao nor Yuan Shao managed to gain a clear operational advantage.
Desperate to break the stalemate, Yuan Shao devised a strategy “The Southern Strategy”. He would send Liu Bei south to Runan to rally Yellow Turban insurgents and foment a rebellion, with the ultimate goal of threatening Xu City.
It was a genuinely smart plan.
* If Xu City were threatened or captured, Cao Cao’s logistical base and lines of communication would be compromised.
* This would force Cao Cao either to withdraw from Guandu or divide his already smaller army.
However, Cao Cao responded rapidly. As he would send Cao Ren to move against Liu Bei before the latter could consolidate support or generate significant disruption. Liu Bei’s contingent was swiftly defeated.
BUT MORE IMPORTANTLY, it seems Cao Cao appeared to have inferred the reasoning behind Yuan Shao’s move. Even though Liu Bei did nothing to Xu City, Cao Cao correctly inferred that the city itself was the true objective behind Yuan Shao’s move. Recognizing this, Cao Cao took a precautionary measure and kept Cao Ren stationed to protect Xu City not because there was an immediate threat, but because he anticipated that Yuan Shao, having committed to this strategy, would almost certainly try again.
Indeed, Yuan Shao soon would try again. This time, he dispatched Han Xun on a wide flanking maneuver, advancing through difficult marshland terrain with the aim of striking Xu City directly rather than indirectly. But Cao Cao, who had now kept Cao Ren stationed in the region, was ready. And as a result, Han Xun’s force advanced unknowingly into a trap.

What was Cao Cao’s Strategy at Guandu?
Cao Cao faced an army more than double his size, Yuan Shao commanded around 110,000 troops against Cao Cao's 40,000. This explains why Yuan Shao was consistently on the offensive and held the initiative, while Cao Cao adopted a defensive and reactive strategy. Rather than engaging where Yuan Shao was strongest, Cao Cao carefully retreated, drawing the enemy deep into terrain of his choosing. This stretched Yuan Shao's supply lines and forced him into overextension. Cao Cao then entrenched himself at Guandu, using its fortified position to neutralize Yuan Shao's numerical advantage — an army that cannot break through fortifications gains little from its size. Cao Cao also secured his flanks politically and geographically, building alliances and eliminating potential threats via campaigns. In effect, Yuan Shao was walking into a trap: Cao Cao had chosen the terrain, controlled the operational tempo, and countered every move made against him. Cao Cao's ultimate goal appears to have been a logistical victory, harassing and trying to destroy Yuan Shao's supply lines. Xu You's defection accelerated this, but it is likely Cao Cao would have won regardless, at greater cost and over more time. The sources claim he lost only 8,000 men to Yuan Shao's 80,000, figures that may not be fully accurate, but it does tell us Cao Cao managed the entire campaign with few losses.

My personal defense on the Red Cliff Situation
I would argue that his southern campaign of 208, often reduced to the outcome of the Battle of Red Cliffs, actually reflected coherent and rational decisions made from Cao Cao, rather than recklessness. As Crespigny shows, Cao Cao entered this campaign from a position of near-complete strategic security, the north had been consolidated after the defeat of the Yuan family, the Huai region stabilized through agricultural colonies, and the west pacified through diplomacy, while even potential threats like Ma Teng were neutralized through political maneuver rather than costly warfare. Within this context, his intervention in Jing Province was not optional but necessary, because the death of Liu Biao created a volatile succession crisis in which Liu Bei, an ambitious and legitimate figure, could have unified the region and formed a powerful southern bloc. Cao Cao’s rapid advance therefore reflects rationality. The results initially vindicated him, Jing collapsed with minimal resistance, Liu Zong surrendered, and Cao Cao secured Jiangling and access to the Yangzi, even demonstrating foresight by constructing a navy for the campaign, even before this war started (Contingency). And his pursuit of Liu Bei at Changban further shows he did want to end Liu Bei. The later setback at Red Cliffs must be understood within the constraints of the situation: Cao Cao’s largely northern army was operating in unfamiliar riverine terrain, suffering from fatigue, disease, and the integration of unreliable newly surrendered troops, while facing a coordinated coalition under figures like Zhou Yu and Zhuge Liang. The famous fire attack by Huang Gai exploited these structural weaknesses, and some traditions even suggest Cao Cao withdrew due to illness and logistical strain regardless, indicating that retreat was already a rational necessity. In this light, his withdrawal was not a failure but a good risk assessment, preserving his core power base rather than wasting it in an unsustainable campaign. Ultimately, the campaign did not destroy Cao Cao’s position, it merely checked his expansion.

Context: After The Battle of Red Cliffs, the political landscape of China underwent a profound transformation. The decisive defeat shattered the possibility of immediate unification and instead led to the fragmentation of power among three dominant factions. This division marked the formal emergence of a prolonged tripartite rivalry known as the Three Kingdoms period.
Cao Cao publicly announced he was marching against Zhang Lu in Hanzhong, but this was a deception. His real objective was to pressure the northwest warlords, particularly Ma Chao and Han Sui, into revealing where their loyalties truly lay.
The announcement created an inescapable dilemma. The most practical route to Hanzhong passed through the northwest, near Chang'an and the Wei River valley — territory controlled by semi-independent warlords. If they allowed Cao Cao's army to pass, they would effectively surrender their autonomy. If they refused, they would openly expose themselves as rebels.
They chose rebellion,  which was precisely what Cao Cao wanted, manipulating them into revealing themselves without direct confrontation.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 295.

The Battle of Tong Pass-Huayin

Cao Cao now faced a coalition led by Ma Chao and Han Sui, positioned at Tong Pass.  a natural chokepoint, extremely favorable for defense. Knowing a frontal assault risked defeat, Cao Cao devised a plan.
Phase 1 — Pinning Force: He kept his main army stationed in front of Tong Pass. This fixed the enemy in place, led them to expect the main attack there, and prevented them from redeploying elsewhere.
Phase 2 — Maneuver Force (the real attack): He dispatched a detached force under Xu Huang and Zhu Ling with orders to cross the river at a different point, move north, cross again at Puban, and establish a bridgehead behind enemy lines.
Once this position was secured, Cao Cao moved his main army to join them and began advancing south again, now effectively outflanking the coalition.)
Note: Cao Cao later explained, if he had attempted to maneuver elsewhere immediately, the enemy would have guarded every crossing and prevented his movement. By threatening the obvious route, he controlled enemy expectations.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 296
(Planning Formulation>Misdirection)
As he started maneuvering across, the enemy reacted faster than expected and attacked before the plan was complete. Cao Cao did not lose his composure. He boarded a boat with Xu Chu and withdrew, while soldiers released animals to distract the pursuing enemy. He reached the other bank and rejoined his army.
The plan changed. Where before he had been pinned in front of the enemy, he was now maneuvering around them and regaining the initiative.
He soon encountered another obstacle, a second river crossing, blocked by the enemy. This time, Cao Cao feigned an assault at one location to hold the enemy's attention, while quietly constructing a bridge elsewhere under cover of night. When the enemy struck, believing they could stop him, they walked into a prepared trap and were repulsed. Then Cao Cao crossed successfully.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 298.
(Adaptability>Trap Setting>Misdirection>Bluffing>Mental Fortitude)
Now came one of the most important moves, and it relied more on psychology than direct fighting. The opposing coalition was not a unified force, but a loose alliance of warlords who already had reasons to distrust one another. Cao Cao understood this and chose to exploit it.
He met Han Sui openly and spoke with him in a warm, familiar way, in full view of the others. This alone was enough to raise suspicion. Shortly afterward, he sent Han Sui a letter that appeared to have been altered or corrected in several places, making it look as though something had been hidden.
The content of the letter mattered less than its appearance. When Ma Chao and the other leaders saw it, the natural reaction was to question Han Sui’s loyalty: What was being concealed? Was there a private agreement?
Without making any direct accusation, Cao Cao had planted doubt within the coalition.
At the same time, Cao Cao started bluffing:
* Adopting a defensive posture
* Constructed fortified camps
* Even engaged in negotiations that appeared conciliatory.
As recorded in historical accounts, this display of apparent weakness encouraged the enemy to believe they held the advantage
Now Cao Cao having manipulated the enemy into distrusting each other, which made coordination more difficult, but also making them overconfident by feigning weakness, he chose this perfect moment to attack, not when the enemy was at full strength and unity, but when they were already under pressure and uncertainty. He sent light troops forward to engage and distract them, then struck decisively at their flanks with his best forces, contributing to the collapse of their resistance.
The coalition broke. Several leaders were killed; others fled. Cao Cao secured control of the entire region, including the strategically vital city of Chang'an.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, pp. 299-300.

Context: . The alliance between Liu Bei and Sun Quan was never built on trust, only on shared fear of Cao Cao, and both men ultimately wanted the same territory. When Liu Zhang, ruler of the rich and defensible Yi Province, panicked at Cao Cao's northwestern advance and invited Liu Bei in as a military ally, he handed Liu Bei exactly the opportunity he needed. Rather than acting as a loyal contractor, Liu Bei spent years quietly building political support among local elites and officers, delaying actual fighting, and waiting for the right moment. When tensions finally broke into open conflict, Liu Bei with Zhuge Liang, Zhang Fei, and the defecting Ma Chao, forced Liu Zhang’s surrender. With Yi secured, Liu Bei was no longer dependent on anyone, he had become a true third power. Sun Quan, having watched Liu Bei block his own western ambitions and then claim Yi while still holding Jing, turned hostile.
Context: By this point, Cao Cao had stabilized most of his northern and northwestern frontiers, which allowed him to shift his attention toward one of the most important strategic regions of the age: Hanzhong
Hanzhong was not just another territory. For Cao Cao it was the gateway between the north and the Sichuan basin, which had recently fallen under Liu Bei’s control. Whoever controlled Hanzhong could either defend against invasion from the north or use it as a launch point into Yi province.
(Strategy Forecasting>Deductive Reasoning)
Getting to Hanzhong, however, was difficult. The army had to move through the Qin Ling mountains. Cao Cao therefore avoided the most obvious route and chose the old Lianyun Road, or “Linked Cloud Road.” This was smart as instead of striking directly at the strongest line of defense, he attempted to approach from an unexpected direction.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 313.
(Unpredictability>Abusing Insecurities)
But when Cao Cao arrived, he found that Zhang Wei had fortified the pass with walls and picket lines and had around ten thousand defenders.
His first attack failed badly, with heavy losses, and Cao Cao even prepared to abandon the field and find another approach.
But some of Cao Cao’s soldiers, while being recalled from the front, lost their way and unexpectedly came upon one of Zhang Wei’s guard positions. The defenders panicked and fled. When Xiahou Dun reported this, Cao Cao inferred correctly that this was a weak point and resumed the offensive. The defenses collapsed in a night engagement, Zhang Wei was killed while fleeing, and the road into Hanzhong opened.
Zhang Lu later surrendered.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, pp. 313-314.
(Abusing Insecurities>Inductive Reasoning)
After the capture of Hanzhong, there was the question of whether Cao Cao should immediately invade Liu Bei’s Yi province. Sima Yi advised him to do exactly that.
But Cao Cao refused. He replied with: “A man who cannot be satisfied will get Long and look to Shu.” In other words, after gaining one major prize, a greedy man immediately desires the next. Cao Cao was warning against overextension. He had just taken Hanzhong, but pushing deeper into Sichuan would have meant marching into difficult terrain, far from his bases, against an enemy who could fight (Lets not also forget Cao Cao had suffered losses.)
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 314.

My personal defense on the Yi Situation
A lot of people criticize Cao Cao for not attacking Liu Bei. But if Cao Cao failed to win quickly, his main army could become trapped deep in the west, far from its base and other frontiers. Withdrawal through the mountains could have become disastrous. Crespigny explicitly compares this danger to Red Cliffs: Cao Cao had already seen what happened when an overambitious southern campaign stretched beyond its practical limits. A failure in Yi province, the author argues, would have been even worse. So Cao Cao chose consolidation over ambition. This was not cowardice. It was strategic restraint. He secured Hanzhong and withdrew north, returning to Ye by early 216. However, his advanced position remained vulnerable. Liu Bei’s men quickly counterattacked in the surrounding regions. Huang Quan defeated the tribal leaders who had submitted to Cao Cao, and Zhang Fei surprised Zhang He in Ba commandery, driving him back to Nanzheng. This showed that Cao Cao’s conquest of Hanzhong did not automatically translate into control over the broader approaches to Yi. Liu Bei was already recovering and restoring the frontier.
(Risk Assesments)
While Cao Cao was still campaigning in the northwest, Sun Quan seized the opportunity and attacked Hefei once more, reportedly bringing 100,000 men against a garrison of just seven thousand, commanded by Zhang Liao, Li Dian, and Yue Jin.
Cao Cao, however, had anticipated the possibility. He had left a contingency in the form of instructions with the Protector Xue Ti, to be opened only in an emergency. The orders read:
"If Sun Quan comes, General Zhang and General Li go out to fight, General Yue keeps guard; the Protector does not fight."
1. “General Zhang and General Li go out to fight”: It is hard to understand without context, but basically Cao Cao never intended for the initial sortie to win a pitched battle, its purpose was to strike hard enough to shake enemy morale and disrupt their momentum.
2. “General Yue keeps guard; the Protector does not fight” (Contingency): But Cao Cao also planned for the possibility that it might not work. Should the bluff fail, Yue Jin and Xue Ti were to hold the city, prevent a breakthrough, and keep the defense from collapsing, buying time until relief could arrive.
After some internal debate, the commanders followed the plan. Zhang Liao led eight hundred volunteers in a sudden, ferocious assault that broke through the enemy lines, and in a remarkable act of improvisation, personally challenged Sun Quan from within his own army. The effect was devastating. Sun Quan, stunned, ordered a withdrawal, and as the Wei forces gave pursuit, he was twice caught in desperate straits at a river crossing, saved only by the fierce loyalty of his personal guard.
What makes this plan impressive is that Cao Cao devised the plan in advance, without being present, and it unfolded almost exactly as he had envisioned, which means somehow he understood Sun Quan would be shocked, stunned and finally humiliated.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, pp. 316-317.

In 216, Cao Cao took the title King of Wei, marking another step in his formal elevation above ordinary ministers of Han.
(Influence Building)
Cao Cao launched one more campaign against Sun Quan, reaching the Ruxu area, Sun Quan took a defensive position near his fortress at Ruxu on the Yangzi. Cao Cao attacked in the second month, but achieved little on land. After only a few weeks, he withdrew his main army.
However, this campaign differed from previous ones. Cao Cao did not simply withdraw completely. Instead, he left Xiahou Dun in command of 26 substantial army corps, including strong commanders such as Cao Ren and Zhang Liao. Cao Cao was not necessarily trying to conquer Sun Quan immediately. Instead, he created a permanent pressure system on the lower Yangzi frontier.
This was effective. Sun Quan’s Yangzi defenses meant he did not face immediate destruction, but the presence of such a large Wei force constrained him. He could not freely move west against Liu Bei or into Jing province without risking raids or attack from Cao Cao’s forces.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 318.
(Strategy Building>Indirect Manipulation)
Under this pressure, Sun Quan would later send an embassy to Cao Cao asking for terms. The two sides restored friendly relations and renewed the idea of a marriage alliance. Cao Cao then withdrew his troops and left only garrisons as before.
Then the Hanzhong campaign would start, where Liu Bei initially fails but adapts by abandoning direct assaults and executing an operational outflanking maneuver through difficult terrain, culminating in the Battle of Mount Dingjun where Xiahou Yuan is killed and Wei’s position collapses. Cao Cao, delayed by internal instability and unable to concentrate his forces, arrives too late to restore the situation and simply chooses the best option, which is to withdraw, marking a decisive strategic shift in which he loses the initiative in the west and Liu Bei emerges as a fully independent rival power.
Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 125–228 AD, p. 428.
