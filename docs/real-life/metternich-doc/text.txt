CHAPTER I
THE SON OF A LIAR CHATTER: A YOUNG COUNT HEARS THE STORM OF REVOLUTION
(1773-1794)

Klemens von Metternich (I'll spare his very long full name for you) was born on May 15, 1773, in Koblenz, son of Franz George von Metternich and Maria Beatrice von Kanegegg.
The Metternich family had a very ancient lineage dating back to the Carolingian nobility, and for centuries they had held prominent positions in the city of Trier. However, they could be considered lower-middle-ranking nobles within the aristocratic sphere of the Germanic Empire. Apart from his father and an ancestor who was a commander in the Thirty Years' War, no Metternich had held offices outside the archbishopric of Trier or Mainz. Their importance was limited also due to the modest properties they owned (just a ruin and a castle near Trier, and some lands in Bohemia). Klemens' father had been the first in the family for a long time to attempt an ambitious career and had managed to obtain the role of ambassador of the Archbishop of Trier (who, we recall, was still a prince-elector within the now antiquated Holy Roman Empire) at the imperial court of Maria Theresa of Austria.
Throughout his childhood and early adolescence, the young Klemens lived in Koblenz, a small city in the archbishopric, cared primarily by his mother, as his father was often busy with diplomatic assignments. Indeed, those duties increased when, thanks to his wife's closeness to the Habsburg family, he was hired as Austrian ambassador to the Archbishop of Trier, perfectly reversing his previous role. His father was not particularly popular at court yet, so much so that the archduchesses called him a "cheater and a chronic liar", but evidently Beatrix's influence over the Habsburg women was enough to make up for some of Franz's shortcomings. In any case, as mentioned, it was she who oversaw Klemens's education. He and his brothers were educated by Abbot Bertrand, specializing in the study of French and French literature given his mother's, an intelligent and curious woman, fervent passion for Parisian culture. Already in his adolescence, the young Metternich spoke fluent French and preferred it to German until young adulthood1.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
Meanwhile, his father occasionally insisted that he accompany him on business trips, though not to Vienna: at age six, his father took him to Strasbourg (where he was vaccinated against smallpox), at age seven, to a conference of clergymen in Cologne, and at 14, he was introduced to the princes of Paderborn and Hildesheim, on his first visit to Habsburg lands2.
Beginning in 1784, Countess Beatrice assigned another tutor other than Abbot Bernard, Johan Frederick Simon, a generous and sensitive Franco-German scholar who remained in the Metternich household for five years. During this period, he educated Klemens both in the study of philosophy and in physical exercise, making him a good swimmer and horse rider, instilling in him the need for training that remained with him even in old age: even by then, he was described as athletic and fit. Simon's role was partially reinterpreted in a negative light by Klemens starting in 1792, when his former tutor had become a fervent Jacobin and obtained positions with the revolutionary government in Paris. Although Metternich specifies in his autobiographies that even as a teenager, some of his master's ideas seemed insidious and terrifying, this is unlikely to be true, and Simon was the young count's first, indirect, contact with the French Revolution, which would become his greatest enemy.
(Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge-Adversity Capacity: Perseverance)
Given his good grades, at fifteen Metternich was considered ready to enter the University of Strasbourg, Simon's hometown, where he was accompanied by both teachers and where he met his tutor, Maximilian of Zweibrücken, a member of the royal house of Wittelsbach, whose wife was a friend of Countess Beatrix. Upon his arrival, Metternich immediately won the nobleman's favor and soon that of the other students as well (thanks to Simon's advice), and on his matriculation day he was described as "handsome, happy and amiable." In reality, Metternich's true character was vain, a liar, and a boaster, but these were flaws he knew how to mask well3.
(Deception: Acting Skills-Social Engineering: Social Skills/Charisma-Adaptability: Social)
At Strasbourg, one of the best universities in the world at the time, Metternich showed himself to be a young man of great promise: in addition to regular courses, he attended private lessons (studying more than his colleagues) and also devoted himself to learning the violin, an instrument to which he demonstrated a certain sensitivity4. He focused his studies primarily on history, passionately pursuing Koch Montesquieu's courses.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability/Cognitive Capacity-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
While Metternich was absorbed in his university life, an earthquake of indescribable force had struck Paris: first the Tennis Court Oath, then attack at the Bastille, France was plunging into the chaos of the Revolution. A few days after the proclamation of the Rights of Man, confusing news reached Strasbourg (which was part of the Kingdom of France), prompting a stampede of beggars and drunks, easily quelled by Maximilian Wittelsbach. Klemens observed these events with a certain calmness and, unlike most young nobles, did not panic. Although his mother begged him to return to German territory (as his brothers had done), Klemens understood that as long as Louis XVI remained in power, the aristocrats had little to fear; moreover, Strasbourg was on the fringes of the kingdom, and revolutionary ideas had little root in the city5. He was therefore able to continue his studies for another year.
(Adversity Capacity: Impulse Management-Reasoning: Abductive-Emotional Intelligence: Management)
But it was the death of Emperor Joseph II, succeeded by his brother Leopold II, that called him home. Klemens's father spent all his energy to make his family shine during the new emperor's coronation, and while this had no effect on the intelligent Leopold, the same could not be said for his son and heir, Francis, who had been bullied for years by Joseph II and was now happy to get a few courtiers to flatter him, something Franz did very well. Klemens met Archduke Franz during the ceremony, and in the following months, under his mother's guidance, he worked hard to build strong relationships with the Austrian nobility. He did the same during a visit at Frankfort.
(Social Engineering: Influence Building)
After the coronation, Klemens decided to continue his university studies, and since Strasbourg had meanwhile fallen to the Revolution, he decided to transfer to the University of Mainz, where he would complete his studies in law and diplomacy, with some additional scientific courses that interested him. Mainz was in his family's homeland, safe from the fury of the revolution, a place where it was easy to stand out and where the teaching was moderate—these were the reasons that had led him to choose it.
(Thinking: Linear/Associative-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
Thanks to his appearance and affable manners, Metternich achieved a certain success in the high society of Mainz, even among women: he fell in love with Marie Constance de Caumont, an extremely beautiful nineteen-year-old French married noblewoman, with whom he had a relationship for three years (therefore until his marriage), and by whom he remained fascinated for the rest of his life.
(Social Engineering: Influence Building-Emotional Intelligence: Understanding-Adaptability: Social)
In 1792, his studies were interrupted again by the death of an emperor. Leopold II indeed died prematurely after less than two years of reign, leaving the throne to Francis. Metternich returned to attend the coronation, where he even opened the ball with the future Queen of Prussia, Louise.
Although the celebrations were many and magnificent, the clouds of revolution were gathering even more, and by a strange coincidence, on the day Francis presented himself to the world as Holy Roman Emperor, Louis XVI was guillotined. Francis, less wise and capable than his father, organized a clumsy attempt to respond to the war that Revolutionary France had declared against him, and Metternich abandoned Mainz to return to Koblenz, where the Duke of Brunswick, commander of the Austro-Prussian forces, had organized his armies. If it’s true that initially he obtained some successes, the duke was repeatedly defeated by the French armies (remember the disaster of Valmy), allowing the French invasion of Belgium and then as far as the Rhine to happen, an invasion that even occupied some Metternich lands. Mainz also fell. The noble family then joined the coalition army for a time, and for two months Metternich served in the army, though with virtually no results given the limited time available.
His brief stay in the military, however, allowed him to network with some British military personnel of noble origins, which paved the way for his first official diplomatic assignment in 1794: a posting on behalf of his father in London in order to obtain a British loan. There Metternich received a unique welcome for a diplomat of his age, thanks to his exceptional ability to make himself appreciated. He even met King George, dined with London's most important families, and was flattered by the Prince of Wales, who called him “one of the handsomest and most charming men I have ever met”5.
(Social Engineering: Charisma/Social Skills-Adaptability: Social)

CHAPTER II
THE DIPLOMATIC CAREER: METTERNICH AT DRESDEN, BERLIN AND PARIS
(1794-1808)

While Metternich was dawdling in London, the situation on the continent was deteriorating: a new revolutionary offensive had broken the coalition's defensive lines, and by then even Koblenz had fallen. The Metternich family had fled and fallen into disgrace at the emperor Francis's court since the father, Franz, who was responsible for the diplomatic administration of Austrian Belgium, had proven largely incapable of fulfilling his duties and had seen his jurisdiction swept away by the French. When Klemens joined his family in Vienna, his career seemed to be on the verge of collapse, having fallen along with his father's credibility1.
It was the mother, Countess Beatrix, who saved the family's fortunes, thanks to the long-standing relationships she had established with the Habsburg court when she was young, particularly with the Kaunitz family, the lineage to which the recently deceased Austrian chancellor had belonged. Despite the fragile situation the Metternichs found themselves in, Beatrix managed to persuade the Kaunitzes to marry young Eleonora, a scion of the family, to Klemens. While the girl immediately fell in love with the diplomat's beauty, he showed her sincere affection, but her unappealing appearance apparently never led to the marriage being founded on love.
(Social Engineering: Charisma)
Eleonora's father, Prince Kaunitz, was disgusted by Metternich's political machinations and wanted to prevent the marriage, but his daughter's infatuation (Klemens had skillfully courted her) and Klemens's position slow recovery in Viennese salons convinced him to accept on two conditions: Eleonora would continue to reside with the Kauneitzes, and Klemens would not return to his diplomatic career until his death. After the marriage, Metternich became a true Austrian nobleman of high rank and managed to win the favor of several Viennese aristocrats, such as the vain Prince de Ligne, whom he skillfully approached by appearing to be his avid listener.
(Social Engineering: Influence Building/Social Skills/Seduction-Deception: Acting Skills)
Meanwhile, he continued his studies, taking courses in physics, geology, chemistry, and medicine2. In January 1797 the first child of Klemens and Eleonore, a girl, was born.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
A few months later, his father-in-law died, and Metternich was able to return to his diplomatic role. Shortly thereafter, his father Franz asked him to serve as secretary at the Congress of Rastatt, a meeting organized to compensate the nobles who had lost the lands ceded to France following the Treaty of Campo Formio, imposed by Napoleon at the end of the Italian Campaign. The Congress had been planned by France as a potential burial ground for the obsolete Holy Roman Empire, but since Napoleon never showed up, it was a futile diplomatic move, which nevertheless served Austria to buy time to strengthen ties with the Russians and the British. The Second War of Coalition ended in another defeat for Austria nevertheless. In the war’s year (1800) Metternich avoided public service since he and Eleonore lost two children, a boy and a girl, and he limited himself to persecute his social life in Vienna and his scientific studies. In 1801 yet the emperor Francis personally encouraged him to accept a diplomatic post, offering him three possible occupations, of which Klemens chose the role of Austrian ambassador to Saxony (in Dresden), which was close to the Metternich family's Bohemian estates.
(Adversity Capacity: Mental Fortitude)
Before leaving for Dresden, Metternich wrote The Instructions, a short text that analyzed the political events from 1790 to 1801 with cynicism and judgment. The work focused less on the implicit political condemnation of the French Revolution, and more on the responsibilities of various European nations in the defeat of the two coalition wars. It also went into the possibilities that might affect Austria now: the loss of Austrian Belgium was seen by Metternich almost as an advantage, given that it was difficult to defend, and it was now necessary to reorganize the defeated and isolated German states under the banner of Vienna, forming a common German front that could link up with British naval power3.
(Thinking: Analytic/Critical/Convergent-Reasoning: Abstract)
He hoped to bring these ideas to the ears of the Elector of Saxony, but the latter proved to be an incompetent and idle man, and Metternich's experience in Dresden was particularly unproductive given the lack of any decisive political will within the state. His only noteworthy activity in the city appears to have been his long list of lovers, one of which he also impregnated and the daughter was recognized even by his wife Eleonor.
Finally in 1803 Klemens was recalled from his position in Dresden to replace the Austrian ambassador to Berlin, a much more prestigious and politically valuable position that would have allowed him direct contact with the King of Prussia, in a delicate moment since the Third War of Coalition was about to start and Austria would have been again on the frontline against Napoleon. In the same year the Metternich father obtained from the emperor the title of prince (from count) and a chair in the Reichstag (the imperial parliament), significantly increasing the reputation of the family among the court of Vienna.
Metternich was well received in Berlin, partly because he already knew Queen Louise, who treated him like a friend, but he quickly realized that King Frederick William was trying to have little to do with the ambassadors. Metternich's intention was to push the Prussians into the Third Coalition with Russia and Austria, but the king's positions were very different, and even Austrian Chancellor Cobenzl disagreed with that policy, as it attempted to soften relations with Paris.
(Strategy: Building-Planning: Formulation/Aim)
The news of Napoleon's coronation was met with dismay and indignation by Klemens, who attempted to use his influence with Cobenzl to prevent Bonaparte from being recognized as emperor by Austria. However, decisions had already been made, and within a short time, since the Holy Roman Empire was about to be dissolved, Francis I became Francis II, new Emperor of Austria. Metternich deeply feared that within a short time, French ambitions would involve the occupation of Germany itself beyond the Rhine. If Vienna still did not agree with his position, he understood that the Russians did, thanks to his contacts with the Russian ambassador in Berlin. Metternich was enthusiastic when London and St. Petersburg signed the Third Coalition and when Vienna also joined, albeit belatedly. His task of convincing the Prussians to join, however, was impossible4, and indeed he failed: Frederick William had received from Napoleon the promise of Hanover in exchange for neutrality, and the Austrians or the Russians did not seem capable of promising anything as good; Metternich had no cards to play at all.
The situation seemed to change somewhat when Alexander I of Russia approached Prussia, both to lead his army and to convince King Frederick William to accept the alliance. Pressure from the Russian army made the sovereign more receptive, and he then negotiated with Metternich and Alexander, who convinced him to enter the war at least if the French did not accept some of the Coalition's peace proposals. Metternich attempted to include more clauses in the agreement, fearing that the Prussians would withdraw too easily from the pact5, but Emperor Francis persuaded him to desist. The idea of the prince proved to be farsighted in this case, as the unfolding of events would demonstrate.
(Foresight: Accuracy-Counteraction: Trap Evasion-Strategy: Shielding-Field Crafting: Risks Calculation)
Just a few days after reaching the agreement with Prussia, a French offensive nearly led to the occupation of Vienna, and the coalition's resistance was swept away on December 1, 1805, when Napoleon achieved his absolute masterpiece at Austerlitz. The Prussians had not intervened in any way, despite the French rejection of peace proposals, nor even in the face of the collapse of the coalition front, thus losing international credibility. Metternich would grow closer to Tsar Alexander, with whom he shared the necessary political line to impose against France.
After the defeat of the Third Coalition, Metternich gained support in Vienna, as the emperor recognized that his initiative had been aimed solely at strengthening the allied front and that other politicians (such as Cobenzl) had been a drag and needed to be replaced. Key to the new shift in Austrian policy was the appointment of Archduke Charles, the emperor's brother, as commander-in-chief of the armed forces. Furthermore a now political ally of Metternich (tho a future enemy), Philippe Stadion, was nominated Foreign Minister.
(Social Engineering: Influence Building)
In the spring of 1806, thanks to his newfound fame and his disdain for the Prussians, he was granted the embassy in St. Petersburg, at the court of the Tsar with whom he seemed to be so much in accord.
However, he was never sent because Napoleon had requested the replacement of the Austrian ambassador in Paris and had specifically requested a Kaunitz (although this statement seemed confusing), and since Eleanor's brother was too inexperienced, his brother-in-law Klemens was chosen. Metternich reluctantly renounced the embassy to Russia, knowing that in Paris he would have greater political scope, not to mention the fact that the role was the most prestigious of all Austrian ambassadors.
While Metternich was on his way to Paris (a journey that was prolonged due to some political incidents between Austria and France which provocated the close of the borders) Francis II definitively renounced the title of Holy Roman Emperor, dissolved the empire, and from it arose the Confederation of the Rhine, a group of Germanic states under French influence.
Finally, in Paris, our man was received by Talleyrand. Their first conversation was largely ceremonial; Metternich, who was younger, spoke more, while Talleyrand remained calm and amiable. Both viewed the other favorably.
In the very days of his arrival, Metternich committed himself to gathering as much information as possible in the French capital from the Austrian bureaucrats who had replaced him during the trip, namely Baron von Vincent and the secretary von Floret, thanks to whom he was able to gain a somehow complete idea of ​​the political situation in France.
(Field Crafting: Info Gathering-Thinking: Speed)
The first meeting with Napoleon took place a few days after the one with Talleyrand. Metternich described Napoleon as a rude man (he hadn't removed his hat when they met) and detected in him the first signs of post-Austerlitz overpretentiousness6. Napoleon would need further meetings to truly understand the young Metternich's abilities, but the latter understood his interlocutor more quickly, just as he also understood that he no longer had any respect for the Austrian honors and army, as he wrote to Vienna a few days later.
(Emotional Intelligence: Perception/Realization-Sensory: Observation)
Thanks to a dinner with Lord Yarmouth, the British ambassador in Paris, Metternich realized that not only would the British forces not accept any peace with the French, but that London had pushed for the return of Hanover to King George III in order to incite the Prussians. Metternich then predicted that Prussia, already annoyed by the creation of the Confederation of the Rhine, would likely organize a war against France. Napoleon had failed to understand the same and was taken by surprise by the mobilization of Prussian troops towards the border.
(Foresight: Depth/Accuracy-Reasoning: Abductive-Field Crafting: Info Gathering)
Both he and Talleyrand left Paris for almost a year in order to follow the campaigns in Germany and Poland, which culminated with the decisive victory of the French. While Metternich was worried due to France’s overpower on the continent, he couldn’t hide his happiness for the humiliation of the country that betrayed the alliance with Austria not even two years ago.
As the Fourth Coalition War tore apart Central and Eastern Europe, Metternich conquered Parisian high society in a way never before seen from an ambassador. Within five months, he had become the man of the moment7: not only because of his extraordinary seductive power (regardless of the presence of his wife and children in the city with him) which had captivated the Parisian ladies, but also because of his elegant manners, the luxury with which he lived his public life, and his intense participation in salons, cultural life, and entertainment.
(Social Engineering: Influence Building/Charisma/Seduction-Adaptability: Social/Enviromental)
Metternich apparently avenged Austerlitz by cuckolding a large number of generals of the Grande Armé. His most notable victim was undoubtedly Joachim Murat, whose wife, Caroline Bonaparte (Napoleon's sister), became his lover, driven by her boredom with her husband. Metternich naturally selected his mistresses among those ladies who could pass on important information to him, and Caroline did so, providing him with extensive information on Empress Josephine, her brother's mistresses, and the political intrigues of Napoleon's court. After his affair with her, Metternich took General Junot's wife as his lover. In her case, he was so skilled at concealing the affair that not even the French secret police, who were constantly spying on him, learned of it before he himself revealed it. However, Caroline, jealous, compromised Metternich's relationship with Junot’s wife.
(Deception: Concealment-Field Crafting: Info Gathering-Social Engineering: Seduction)
While Metternich was entertaining Parisian high society, two fundamental changes reshuffled the cards on the table: Talleyrand was replaced by Champagny as Foreign Minister, worsening relations with Metternich and Austria. More importantly, Napoleon and Tsar Alexander signed the Tilsit Agreement, which ended the Fourth World War and divided the European continent between France and Russia. While the news was perceived as a catastrophe in Vienna, Metternich did not give in to pessimism. In his view, although Austria was now effectively surrounded by the Grand Armé (the French had also acquired Poland, and their influence in Germany had further increased following the humiliation of Prussia), the European order created by Napoleon could not last long, since it was not only artificial, but governed exclusively by his personal military prowess. But above all, Metternich foresaw that it was only a matter of time before France and Russia would face each other again, and at that moment both would turn to Austria, which would be the decisive pawn on the chessboard.
(Foresight: Depth-Reasoning: Abductive-Thinking: Linear/Critical/Convergent)
Meanwhile, despite his diplomatic efforts with Champagny, the French occupied three fortifications on the Austrian border. His failure was once again due to the fact that Austria was then perceived as a toothless tiger and unable to mount effective resistance to Paris's demands. Aware that his political opponents might use the inevitable defeat against him, Metternich spent eight hours writing a solid self-defense immediately after his meeting with the French Foreign Ministry8.
(Counteraction: Trap Evasion)
In the following months, however, the Prince's actions bore increasingly positive results. Napoleon intended to force Austria to break diplomatic relations with England, but the ambassador's actions succeeded in slowing down and ultimately derailing the emperor's demands, thanks also to the cooperation of the Austrian ambassador in London.
(Counteraction: Trap Evasion/Improvisation)
During the same period, Metternich and Talleyrand grew significantly closer, with the latter expressing his intention to defend Austria's role as a power by virtue of its importance in the European geopolitical balance. Although Metternich appeared enthusiastic about his words and promises, in reality he knew that the elder statesman could only be partially trusted. Nevertheless, he considered him the only true representative of a pro-Austrian party at Napoleon's court. It was Talleyrand himself who informed him, behind the emperor's back, that France had two large-scale intentions: the first was the partition of Turkey, the second an expedition to India, which, however, seemed like a utopia. Metternich then hastened to warn his foreign minister of French intentions, and above all that Vienna must attempt to obtain the largest possible effect, since opposing the dismemberment of the Ottomans was impossible if Napoleon were the one deciding so. At the same time, however, he also informed the Russian ambassador in Paris, playing on Russian concerns over the dissolution of Constantinople. Metternich's goal was to strengthen Austro-Russian relations in order to secure an alliance, and although he succeeded in the first attempt, the second was impossible at the time, given that Tilstin was too recent and Russia was already engaged in fighting Sweden, not to mention that the humiliation of Austerlitz had left indelible marks.

A few days later, Metternich was summoned by Napoleon, who presented him with a less precise outline than Talleyrand had already described. The prince pretended to know nothing about it and remained pleased with the proposals, enterntained at Napoleon's attempts to keep matters, on which Metternich himself was already well informed, vague. In any case, the English invasion of Portugal and Spain ruined Napoleon's plans and Turkey remained outside of future wars.

Metternich was impressed by the war in Iberia, and it prompted him to suggest that Vienna light the fuse of German anti-French partisan sentiment.

The emperor was increasingly tense, and Archduke Charles' military reforms had attracted French attention, if not concern. Metternich's concerns were justified: as soon as he returned to Paris, Napoleon demonstrated his willingness to threaten the Austrians, so much so that during his thirty-ninth birthday celebrations, while greeting various ambassadors, upon reaching Metternich he asked, directly and bluntly: "How is Austria's rearmament going?"9. Such a lack of diplomacy left both Metternich and Talleyrand perplexed, but the Rhinelander tried in every way to smooth over tensions, given that he considered the Austrian army not yet ready for war and lacking the Russian military support, which was essential for him.

Subsequently, despite all their efforts, Austria and Emperor Francis were excluded from the negotiations at the Erfurt Congress, in which Napoleon and Alexander I wanted to have a virtual confirmation of Tilstin. Metternich himself was unable to attend, which could only underscore Austria's isolation. However, Talleyrand informed him that Napoleon's attempts to convince Alexander I to join the French in waging war against Austria had failed. The situation grew significantly in tension, and Metternich was called back to his country by Sideon.
Upon returning to Vienna, Metternich found the city and the nation preparing for war. His own observations and information were crucial in convincing Archduke Charles (and subsequently Emperor Francis) to declare open hostilities. Metternich summarized them in three points: the French nation was beginning to lose its sense of approval for Napoleon's constant wars, the Russians would not follow Austria, and Napoleon could only muster 206,000 soldiers, most of whom were under-recruited, having been conscripted from Poland or Germany. Charles thought that, although the Austrian financial problems (maybe covered by England?) and the absence of the Tsar’s support, a possibility of win was concrete if Napoleon would have been caught in surprise.
Returning to Paris after delivering his information, Metternich learned from an unknown source (perhaps Talleyrand?) the disposition of some French regiments. He immediately communicated that to Stadion, although he entrusted the letter to Saxon couriers to prevent the Austrians from being intercepted en route.

Despite what Metternich feared, he was not arrested violently, much less shot. On the contrary, he was able to live peacefully in Paris for a few weeks until he was actually arrested, albeit only formally, and used as an exchange for the French embassy in Vienna.

CHAPTER III
FROM A LACKEY TO NAPOLEON'S CONQUEROR:
THE COMPLEX VICTORY OVER THE REVOLUTION
(1809-1814)

The war was lost, once again. Although Austria performed far better than in 1805 (Charles even managed to defeat Napoleon at Espern), the predictions of French fatigue were overestimated, and the Austrian second army led by Archduke John was too easily swept away. Metternich returned to Vienna while it was virtually under French occupation, finding the hated Champagny governing it. Thanks to his connections with Berthier, the Rhine prince obtained the abolition of a tribute imposed by Champagny on all Viennese, and he also had the courage to refuse two talks with Napoleon at such a critical moment, realizing that the emperor wanted to use him as an emissary of peace.
(Adversity Capacity: Stress Management/Mental Fortitude-Reasoning: Abductive)
After Austria's collapse at Wagram, Emperor Francis and Archduke Charles decided there was nothing left to do but sue for peace. Francis decided to immediately replace Stadion with Metternich as Foreign Minister, but Metternich astutely suggested he wait until the peace treaties were concluded: immediately removing Stadion, who had been the war's greatest supporter, would clearly make Austria's already tenuous situation completely subjugated and seeking surrender. His appointment was therefore postponed until the armistice was signed.
(Reasoning: Inductive-Thinking: Associative-Psychological Warfare: Reinforcements)
Metternich, however, participated in the peace negotiations and attempted in every way to defend what little Austria could still hold on. His diplomatic actions seemed far too fluid to the Emperor Franz and Stadion: Metternich argued that for the time being the Empire could only accommodate the continental system created by Bonaparte and attempt to replace Russia as France's primary foreign interlocutor. On the one hand, he had to withstand Napoleon's outbursts, and on the other, face the distrust of those at the Austrian court who believed it would be better to honor the monarchy in a struggle of self-destruction against Napoleon rather than humiliate themselves so much1.
(Strategy: Building-Planning: Aim-Adversity Capacity: Stress Tolerance-Thinking: Critical)
The rift between Metternich and the Habsburg court, however, arose because the diplomat was attempting to negotiate with the French the strengthening of an independent Polish state ruled by a national dynasty. While Stadion and the Emperor considered this unacceptable, Metternich was actually doing so because it could create a potential future ally for the Empire, in an anti-Napoleonic spirit. After that Francis started to ignore the prince and he was left out of the final negotiations, being re-ammitted in the government once they were over, finally obtaining the role of Foreign Minister.
(Planning: Contigencies/Aim)

While they were in Italy, Francis's wife Maria Ludovica died, a fact that not only eliminated an important opponent of Metternich at court, but also allowed the minister to organize another imperial marriage, between his sovereign and the daughter of Maximilian of Württemberg, a marriage that allowed Austria to improve its relations with the small southern German principalities, weakening Bavaria, which in recent months had been claiming territories taken from it during the Congress of Vienna.

Metternich's efforts in the peninsula, however, were poorly rewarded: the Piedmontese, with whom the Austrians bordered, refused to cooperate in the creation of a Habsburg-led Italian confederation, Emperor Francis continued to oppose Metternich's proposed reforms of decentralization and autonomy for the Kingdom of Lombardy-Venetia, and he was also receiving information about the formation of ever-increasing secret Italian nationalist circles throughout the region. Pessimistic about the possibility of achieving significant results with his current policy, Metternich established a secret police force based in Milan specifically dedicated to monitoring and gathering data on Italian patriotic movements4.

Upon returning to Vienna, Metternich was struck by the death of Countess Julie Zichy-Festetics, a woman he had deeply loved, but whose mistress he had never managed to become due to her fidelity to her husband. The countess's death plunged Metternich into deep mourning, accompanied by physical pain from the various journeys he was undertaking at the time, including a second to Italy, during which he faced criticism from the population against the corrupt government that had just been installed, and to Portugal, to accompany Francis's daughter to her marriage to the crown prince. Despite the difficult period, Metternich continued to work hard to draft a plan of imperial reforms that could revitalize the administration of the most problematic provinces, Italy first and foremost, but also Galicia and Illyria.

Metternich's main fear was that Italian nationalists would one day turn to the House of Savoy, which ruled Piedmont, to advance the dream of Italian unity, something that could only be avoided by ensuring a just government for the Lombardo-Venetians that could represent them, and not make them feel oppressed.

The three documents prepared by Metternich were presented to Francis in the autumn of 1817. Thanks to the death of the empress, the minister's prestige at court had further increased, but the reforms he was proposing went far beyond the powers normally held by a foreign minister. Metternich proposed the creation of four chancelleries in the outer provinces of the empire to ensure greater representation for non-German peoples. He also called for the creation of a ministry of justice separate from the chancellor's powers. Finally, he proposed the establishment of an imperial assembly that could bring together representatives from all the provinces. The absolutist nature of imperial power was not questioned, but its decentralization was suggested. Francis ratified only a part of Metternich's proposed reforms: the assembly and the ministry of justice were not approved, while a chancellery was granted in Italy. It was the first and last time Metternich attempted to reform the imperial administration.

Also in October 1817, disturbing news arrived from Dresden: in the Saxon city, a major intellectual festival had seen students publicly burn copies of the Vienna Congress treaties and chant for liberation from reactionary tyranny. Metternich was quite concerned, but not surprised: for two years, a dense network of spies he had organized in Germany had been keeping him informed of the movements of German liberals at all the universities of the German Confederation. He reassured the French minister Richelieu (Talleyrand's successor) about the danger of a German revolution and gave permission for the Austrian propaganda machine (active since 1813, headed by his faithful political ally, Gentz, an expert in journalism and communications) to attack the student demonstrations in Saxony5.
(Field Crafting: Info Gathering-Psychological Warfare: Demoralization)
Restricting German neo-Jacobism was as important as clipping the wings of Italian nationalists. For this very reason, Metternich ordered the Austrian ambassador in Berlin to pressure Frederick William III of Prussia to resume the fight against liberalism with greater vigor. Frederick was the leading Protestant king in Germany, and Metternich was convinced that most of the students involved in the uprisings were Protestants. He then warmly welcomed Castlereagh's proposal to reunite the members of the Quadruple Alliance, as agreed upon at the Congress of Vienna. During the meeting, his goal was to try to convince Tsar Alexander to exert less influence on Giovanni Capodistria, his new minister, who was sympathetic to the spread of neo-Jacobin ideas both in Russia and throughout Europe.

Six months passed between the English proposal for an international meeting and the actual Aix Congress. The delay was caused primarily by Metternich's successful attempts to prevent the Tsar from inviting the Spanish and representatives of the minor German courts to the meeting. During those six months, Metternich's father died, and he developed severe rheumatism and back problems.
(Adversity Capacity: Mental Fortitude)
During those six months, Metternich also visited the Diet of the German Confederation in Frankfurt, where it was working too slowly and inefficiently. His contribution was decisive, and within a short time, as if smoothing over a jammed mechanism, the Confederation approved his proposal to create a federal army. The Diet had completely changed its face during his brief stay in Frankfurt6.
(Social Engineering: Persuasion)
The Congress of Aix was another diplomatic success for Metternich, who achieved all his intended objectives. France was cleared of the occupying Allied troops, the war indemnity it was forced to pay was confirmed, and most importantly, it was possible to reconcile Tsar Alexander with conservative positions, weakening the position of Capodistria at the Russian court. Since Metternich had come to the negotiations with clear ideas and thoroughly studied proposals, the congress lasted only a month, and the only flaw he encountered was that Castlereagh, with whom he had by then a very solid alliance/friendship, informed him that from that point on their relations would be slowed by the London cabinet due to a British government reform.
(Planning: Invincibility/Formulation-Social Engineering: Influence Building)
Upon his return to Austria, Metternich had to deal with the aftermath of the murder of von Kotzebue, a German playwright (a Russian spy) who had been killed by a young liberal fanatic in Mannheim. This sparked protests and outcry throughout Germany, and Gentz ​​urged Metternich to apply even more pressure against German university students in order to suppress dissent. However, the minister did not see placing the universities under direct imperial control as an effective solution, although he agreed that a tougher fight against the neo-Jacobin uprisings was necessary. He concluded that the students were a symptom, not a cause, of the discontent and decided that placing the press under greater censorship would have better results. However, he also believed that Prussian involvement and approval for a united policy against the liberal uprisings was necessary, and for this reason he visited King Frederick William III. Thanks to the prestige recently acquired at Aix, Metternich allowed himself to raise his voice with the sovereign, who cowered before him, aware of Austria's superior position and, above all, of the fact that Metternich had been the first to warn him, right there in Aix, of the need to unite a common anti-liberal efforts, but that he had not heeded.
A series of meetings were organized in Carlsbad (in Bohemia, therefore in the Austrian Empire), in which the Prussian ministers firmly approved Metternich's proposals for joint Austrian-Prussian action to limit liberal teaching in universities and censor the press. Thanks to Prussian submission, Metternich faced no obstacle in ensuring that the decisions taken at Carlsbad were approved by the Diet of Frankfurt and therefore valid in all the principalities of Germany. It was one of the peaks of Austrian influence in Central Europe and one of Metternich's greatest triumphs, and he was hailed by the European courts as a visionary in the fight against revolutionaries and a shield for the whole continent. He had become not only a political leader, but also a moral guide in Europe7. Metternich had been able to defend Austria's new imperial dignity and maintain control over Germany without having to revive the Holy Roman Empire and thus weaken the prestige of the Habsburgs.

To prevent the Tsar from meddling in the German affairs he had just decided, as King William of Württemberg hoped, Metternich exploited the feuds and jealousies that were dividing the Russian court8. Austrian diplomatic maneuvers succeeded, and Alexander I blocked Koper's attempts to provoke a political crisis in Southern Germany.

A short and subsequent conference in Vienna between German princes saw the successes of the Treaties of Carsbald confirmed, despite opposition from Bavaria and Wuttemberg. "A word from Austria in Germany is as important as an inviolable law" wrote Metternich after the end of the conference9.
During this period, Metternich devoted himself, in a free hour in the evening, to the study of scientific instruments, to reading novels and travel books and to the study of contemporary history10.

On May 15, 1820, during the conclusion of the German Conference in Vienna, Metternich's fifteen-year-old daughter, Clementine, died of fever. It was his second child who died prematurely, and Metternich, who was extremely fond of his daughter, attempted to overcome the grief by working even harder. Less than three months later, however, another daughter, his favorite, Marie, was struck by the same illness as her sister and died within weeks. Metternich was devastated by the second loss in such a short space of time.
“From now on, duty will completely replace my joy in life”.
He remained alone in Vienna, as his wife and other children moved to Paris, convinced that the Viennese climate was causing their children's ailments.

A few days after these personal tragedies, Europe exploded. In Spain, a constitutionalist insurrection ravaged the country, forcing King Ferdinand to compromise. When the Tsar learned of this, he was furious, having championed divine right in Spain, and demanded that the Quadruple Alliance form an immediate intervention army. However, the United Kingdom had no intention of further destabilizing the Iberian theater, and Metternich attempted to broker a compromise between the two sides. With great difficulty, he succeeded, and although an invasion of Spain was avoided (advised against by Wellington himself), the Alliance front remained politically committed to opposing the new liberal developments in Spain. Metternich, however, could not have known that this fuse had been only the first of a great explosion.
(Strategy: Adaptability-Adversity Capacity: Stress Tolerance)
When news of Madrid reached southern Italy, some army contingents launched a liberal revolution against the Bourbon king, forcing him to grant a constitution similar to the Spanish one. Metternich did not expect the political situation in the South to be so unstable, and once again he was forced to navigate the English demands (Castlereagh was pushing for Austria alone to quell the revolt) and the Russian-Prussian demands (which called for a joint effort by the Alliance). To try to reach a solution, Metternich agreed to participate in the Conference of Troppau, proposed by the Tsar. His fear of being isolated, however, became acute when Castlereagh refused to attend personally and declared that England would not intervene against the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (the new name for the Kingdom of Naples). The only point Metternich could make was that there was no rush to resolve the situation in Naples, since his spies had informed him that the revolutionaries were disputing among themselves and therefore the formation of a liberal government would take a long time11. The main goal during the conference, however, was to once again limit Capodistria's influence on the Tsar, in order to curb his impulses.

In the days leading up to the Conference, he met with both the Tsar and Capodistria. Alexander appeared more submissive than usual, and Capodistria, too, had an affable temperament and attempted to establish a closer and more friendly relationship with Metternich. However our hero remained wary: his reports from London had informed him that Capodistria had obtained great popularity in England the previous year thanks to his personality and his liberal-moderate ideas. As much as he laughed with him, he was certain that in all of Europe he was the most dangerous man to the order he had painstakingly built12.

Metternich opened the conference with the proposal that although it was the Quadruple Alliance's duty to limit the spread of neo-Jacobinism, no great power could directly interfere with another state unless the latter became a problem for it. Simply put, Austria, as a member of the Alliance and Italy's guardian power, had the right to intervene in Naples, while its allies did not.

Capodistria resisted this formulation, emphasizing that it was the responsibility of the sole Alliance to make decisions on the continent and that Austria could not move without the approval of the other three Allies. Capodistria's opposition, however, was overcome, firstly thanks to the submission Metternich had obtained from Prussia to Carsbald. Secondly, however, it pushed his sister Pauline, who was related to the Tsar by marriage, to draw him further along the Austrian lines. Finally, Metternich's own relationship with Alexander had progressively improved in recent years, and finally the Tsar ordered Capodistria to find a compromise with Metternich.

Troppau was, in short, another diplomatic great success for Metternich. The only concession he made to Capodistria was that the Austrian occupation army be accompanied by ambassadors from the three Allied powers. Furthermore, although Castlereagh had received the news coldly, Metternich understood that his reaction was not motivated by personal convictions, but by parliamentary pressure in London. Therefore, at least partially, there was British support, or at least neutral approval. Given the worsening bad weather and the wait for the arrival of the King of Naples to the negotiations, Metternich and Alexander I remained quite a long time in Troppau, where they further bonded. Metternich was now closer and more influential on the Tsar than the Russian ministers themselves. To gain the Tsar's friendship in this way, Metternich had once again underlined the religious value of the political union of the Quadruple Alliance, focusing on a point that was particularly crucial for Alexander's ideology.

When King Ferdinand of Naples finally arrived in Laibach, negotiations continued. Metternich's European authority at Laibach’s Conference was expressed with unprecedented force. The attempts in Capodistria were crushed, the English delegation demonstrated no resistance against him and the Tsar was equally compliant with Austrian directives. It was more important for Metternich to contain the French proposals, which intended to replicate what the Austrians were organizing, but against liberal Spain. Metternich managed to curb the counterrevolutionary aspirations of the French monarchy, but just as the congress dragged on, in March 1822, news arrived that even in Alessandria, Piedmont, some military contingents had risen up to demand a constitution from the king. While Metternich was achieving increasing diplomatic successes, these were taking away time from immediate action, and this led to a slow buildup of revolutionary winds. In reality, however, the outbreak of a revolution in Northern Italy was part of the Austrian foreign minister's plan: he hadn’t halted the coup d’etat in Piedmont despite being informed of it through his spies. 170,000 men were immediately deployed by the Austrian army, half of which were to cover Naples and half of Piedmont. This would not only have further strengthened Austrian influence in Italy, but would also have provided the Austrians with a pretext for more aggressively combating the Italian liberal secret societies (known as the Carbonari), which had increased their activities in recent years. Obtaining the Tsar's approval had been necessary to allow Austria to concentrate a dual army in Italy without leaving its eastern and northern borders exposed, and also to ensure protection against potential crises with the Ottomans, not to mention that the whole decision was legally approved and supported by all the European main powers.
In essence, the entire organization of Troppau and Leibach and the prolonged fights with Capodistria had been part of a plan intended to suppress the revolution in Italy, strengthen Vienna’s authority there, and establish the Austrian Empire as the leading nation of the Alliance bloc, and so the most prestigious nation in Europe.
When his political opposition in Vienna, led by Stadion, protested that financing a dual campaign in Italy would be impossible to sustain for the state coffers, Metternich responded that he had already secured from the Alliance that the Kingdom of Sardinia (Piedmont) and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies would pay the costs of the war. The success was complete, and Metternich also garnered further praise at home, culminating in his appointment as State Chancellor, a position held only by his wife's grandfather, von Kaunitz, before him, which made him superior to all the ministers of the Empire.
