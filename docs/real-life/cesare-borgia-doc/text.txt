CHAPTER I
SON OF SINS: THE YOUNGHOOD OF CESARE
(1475-1492)

Whilst there is little doubt about Cesare Borgia’s date of birth, the place of his birth is uncertain. Some suggest Subbiaco, others Rignano (the more likely possibility);1 some even point to Valencia, in Spain. Having ruled out this last possibility, it can be said that Cesare was born in the very region where he lived and fought for the rest of his life: central Italy. He was the son of the then ambitious Spanish Cardinal Rodrigo Borgia (or Borja, as in the original form), who would later go down in history under his papal name of Alexander VI, and his Mantuan lover Vannozza Cattanei. The illicit nature of his parents’ union was considered so little of a scandal in the Church of the late XV century that Cesare was recognised almost immediately by his father, who took charge of organising his education with the intention of setting him on a path to the priesthood (the military career had already been taken by his elder half-brother, Pier Luigi).
His education, which had to be of the very highest standard to meet the requirements of the upper echelons of the clergy, began at a very early age and was guided by the finest teachers in Rome at the time, including Spannolio of Majorca, a member of the Roman Academy. Rodrigo was the most far-sighted father in the world, and his determination to introduce his son to political and cultural circles at an early age was clear from the outset: by the age of six, Cesare was already accumulating ecclesiastical offices, becoming a canon of Valencia Cathedral, archdeacon of the collegiate church of Jativa (his father’s birthplace), treasurer of Mallorca Cathedral, archdeacon of Tarragona Cathedral and various other titles.2 Obviously, the child had no control whatsoever over the aforementioned administrations, whose substantial financial resources were, however, absorbed by the Borgia family and by his father in particular. At the age of thirteen, the humanist Paolo Pompilio presented him with a copy of his philological work on the metre of Christian literature, a gesture which, however much it may have been motivated by a pathetic desire to flatter the Borgias, might confirm that Cesare had already acquired some knowledge of the work’s subject matter, at least in broad terms.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge)
In 1489, at the age of just fourteen, Cesare was enrolled at the University of Perugia to study canon law. The boy who arrived there was far removed from the typical ambition of his family and came across instead as a devout, reserved and modest young man, more inclined to listen than to speak. His brief spell in Perugia, lasting less than two years, was cut short when he was informed of his appointment as bishop. At the age of just fifteen, Cesare was indeed made Bishop of Pamplona by Pope Innocent VIII at the request of Rodrigo Borgia, who had become for some years the influential vice-chancellor of the papal court. This yet another office, disproportionate to his young age, was intended to secure a further windfall for the Borgia coffers, but it also had the even more important task of establishing the authority and status of the very young Cesare. By presenting himself as a bishop, he officially began his political career. In the wake of his episcopal appointment, the young man wrote a letter to the clergy of Pamplona described as a masterpiece of modesty, in which he named the treasurer of Toledo as his vicar and the administrator to whom they should turn.3 In effect, he was telling Pamplona that he would never see them in person.
(Fluid Intelligence: Learning Ability-Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge/Knowledge Application-Deception: Sophystry)
The time had not yet come to set his political rise in motion, and Cesare had to continue his university studies, this time at the Faculty of Law in Pisa, within Medici’s territory. The move to a different university had been a decision made by his father, driven by the hope — which came to fruition — that in Pisa Cesare might befriend the scion of the de’ Medici family, his contemporary Giovanni (the future Leo X, one of the most important popes of the Renaissance). A profound transformation in Cesare’s character had characterised the period of his move to Tuscany, to the extent that the Perugian image of a mild-mannered and pensive young man was replaced by that of a worldly gentleman, already ambitious and eager to excel. Even his friendship with Giovanni de’ Medici concealed a spirit of rivalry, in which Cesare emerged victorious in the academic sphere (he graduated earlier with excellent grades),4 despite the Florentine’s great intelligence. Not only, but also socially Cesare appeared more brilliant compared to the son of Lorenzo. Banquets, parties and various entertainments filled the days of Borgia and his ever-growing court, as he was now keen to establish himself and demonstrate his imperious nature.
(Crystallized Intelligence: Knowledge-Social Engineering: Influence Building/Charisma)
A trait that would continue to define him in the future, even as a sixteen-year-old Caesar built up his own private retinue by combining a handful of trusted and capable men with a rabble of petty scoundrels, swordsmen, swindlers, gamblers and loose women. The reason he did this was not merely for the pleasure of surrounding himself with riff-raff, but rather his desire to always stand out from the crowd when appearing in public. By gathering insignificant people around him, Caesar could thus shine all the brighter, whilst behind the scenes he forged more significant alliances.4
(Social Engineering: Influence Building/Charisma-Reasoning: Abductive)
However, matters of greater importance than the degree in Pisa were looming in 1492. Granada, the last Arab stronghold in Spain, had fallen, and Columbus’s ships were preparing to set sail for the New World. The death of Lorenzo de’ Medici (whom Cesare met at least on an occasion), Giovanni’s father, was not the only event to destabilise the Italian peninsula: in Rome, Innocent VIII passed away in July. A month later, Rodrigo Borgia won the election of the College of Cardinals and presented himself to the Roman people as Pope Alexander VI. For the Borgia family, this obviously changed everything.
Cesare was in Siena when news reached him that his father had been elected Bishop of Rome. He was gambling at the horse races and had just secured a victory by cheating: he had in fact ordered his jockey to let go of the reins in the final stretch so that the horse would lighten its load and run faster.6
(Tactics: Creativity/Timing)
Alexander didn’t wait much to start his nepotistic politics: just two weeks after his election Cesare was nominated Archbishop of Valenza, making him de facto co-patriarch of Spain even if he was not even eighteen at that moment. Three weeks later Cesare finally reached Rome, filled with ambition and desire for greatness.

CHAPTER II
THE IMPRISONED CARDINAL: WILL OF POWER UNDER THE CHURCH
(1493-1498)

Having arrived in Rome to serve his father, Cesare took up residence in what would later become the Palazzo of the Penitenziari. Here, in March 1493, he had a brief meeting with Andrea Boccaccio, Bishop of Modena and ambassador of the Este court in Rome, who had perhaps come there to try to obtain precious information from the Pope’s son. This meeting, which was quick because Cesare was about to set off on a hunting trip, is of particular interest to us because it provides a clear and comprehensive portrait of the young Archbishop of Valencia, who was just eighteen years old at that time. Boccaccio describes Cesare as a handsome young man, full of cheerfulness and charm, extremely modest, permeated by nobility and in possession of a high intellect. He also notes the swiftness of his movements whilst riding his horse, the result of the long, intensive training sessions he undertook as an outlet for his energy (Cesare was highly skilled not only in hunting, but also in fencing and the handling of all kinds of weapons). Finally, the very young bishop is compared with his elder half-brother, the Duke of Guida, and Boccaccio has no doubt that he is superior to him in affability and character, even though Giovanni Borgia was older and already active as a military leader.1

In the months that followed, Cesare’s political activity was limited and he mostly appeared in public as the Pope’s shadow: he attended his sister Lucrezia’s wedding (the first of many), accompanied the Spanish ambassadors in Rome and attended his father’s political meetings, content to remain a listener. In short, he kept quiet, absorbed important information, and began to acclimatise to the political environment.

Cesare did not care for the ecclesiastical life he had been forced into, and indeed he would free himself from it as soon as he could, but his father was determined to advance his son’s clerical career as far as possible. In September 1493, evidently feeling strong enough, Alexander VI appointed his son a cardinal. This move sparked fierce criticism not only for its clear nepotistic motive (which was, in fact, the norm), but also because Cesare was universally known to be the son of an illegitimate, indeed scandalous, relationship, and therefore theoretically could not have been granted the cardinal’s red hat. In his papal bull, Rodrigo presented him as the son of his mistress Cattanei and her then lawful husband, but in a secret bull addressed to Cesare himself, he documented the true details regarding the young man’s paternity. Giuliano della Rovere, the future Julius II and bitter enemy of the Borgias, threatened to resign from the College of Cardinals in the face of such an affront, but the Pope stood his ground and, in the end, the majority of the cardinals sided with him.
Cesare’s appointment as Prince of the Church had already drawn Europe’s attention to him. A few weeks after the appointment, King Charles VIII of France contacted him. Annoyed that Alexander VI had not appointed a single French cardinal in his bull, the French monarch asked the very young Borgia to intercede with the Supreme Pontiff, in exchange for guaranteed future support for any of his objectives in France.2 Cesare, however, being a keen observer, knows that one must be cautious when dealing with Carlo, given that in the preceding months he had even attempted to exert pressure in Rome to secure recognition of his claims to the Kingdom of Naples. After all, the French invasion and the outbreak of the Italian Wars are just around the corner.

In 1494, in fact, the situation deteriorated further and the tension between Rome and Paris became seriously dangerous. Alexander VI rejected the House of Anjou’s claims to the Neapolitan throne and, indeed, sent his nephew Giovanni to Naples to crown the new Aragonese heir. Not only that, but he arranged for one of his very young sons, the twelve-year-old Goffredo, to marry the daughter of the new King of Naples. The outrage was immense, and Charles VIII threatened to convene an ecumenical council to depose him, to incite the French clergy against him and eventually to invade the papal territories. The Borgia Pope, however, was not deterred; on the contrary, he remained firmly entrenched in Rome, whilst Giuliano della Rovere gave his full support to the French, as the Duchy of Milan would soon do as well. Even the Republic of Venice urged the King of France to teach the new, arrogant, Bishop of Rome and his Spanish allies in Southern Italy a resounding lesson.
In these troubled times, the Pope needed capable advisers and executors more than ever, and Cesare soon became one of his most trusted men. He was sent to Naples as ambassador to negotiate an alliance with the new King Alfonso II, achieving a diplomatic success, as he wrote personally to his brother Giovanni. In July 1494, he took part in the conference at Vicovaro to organise defences against the now imminent French invasion. In any case, these were crucial weeks and months for Cardinal Borgia, who worked with great determination and stood out amongst his brothers and cousins, drawing ever closer to his father and becoming his right-hand man.3 Whilst he is certainly not the one devising the grand strategy of the papal court (Rodrigo’s experience and political acumen are far too great for him to need such support), it is nonetheless true that the nineteen-year old Cesare is already, at this stage, his father’s inspirer and, at times, his instigator, and is also the most energetic hand through which his decisions are carried out.

Events, however, unfolded rapidly. The defence plan organised by Alexander VI and Alfonso II, which included an attempted coup in Genoa, failed. Not only had the Duke of Milan, Ludovico il Moro, allied himself with Charles VIII, but other Italian princes had laid down their arms peacefully and allowed the French army to pass through their territories. Finally, the Medici regime in Florence, which was close to the Borgias, had fallen. As if that were not enough, as Charles VIII drew nearer, more and more Roman families and members of the clergy sided with the pro-French faction. In a short time, the invading army reached Ostia and cut off Rome’s supply lines. It was futile for the papal army to attempt to engage in battle, given the enemy’s overwhelming numerical superiority. However, the Pope did not surrender, not even when the French entered the Holy City. Alexander barricaded himself with Cesare and other trusted men in Castel Sant’Angelo, determined to put up a strenuous defence until his adversaries agreed to negotiate. The King of France threatened to bombard the castle, but the Pope displayed sacred relics on the walls and stood upon them in his papal vestments, and so no one dared to fire. Difficult peace negotiations began, aided in part by the growing rift between the Duchy of Milan and France, in which Cesare also took part. In the end, terms of peace were agreed upon which, whilst harsh, were more favourable to the Borgias than to Charles, given the starting point: the Pope undertook to guarantee the passage of French troops through his territories, pardoned the cardinals who had betrayed him, and handed Cesare over as a legate (in reality, as a hostage) to the King of France for six months. Alexander VI had, however, secured two key points: he was not obliged to grant papal investiture to Charles on the throne of Naples (and thus did not legitimise him), and he had thwarted the plan of Giuliano della Rovere, who sought to persuade Charles VIII to convene an ecumenical council to depose the Pope.
The experience of the French occupation of Rome, however negative, had presented two opportunities, one for Alexander and one for Cesare: the former had observed that some Italian states were now beginning to worry about French dominance on the peninsula (and consequently began to plot in secret to form an anti-French league), whilst the latter had noted with interest the growing sentiment of hatred among the Romans towards the invaders, finally perceiving a change in the usual inertia of the Italian people, which his father had complained about.4 This pseudo-patriotic sentiment would serve him well later on.

Whilst maintaining his affable demeanour, however, Cesare was secretly plotting a small trick against the French: under one pretext or another, he had halted and sent back two of the seventeen carts he was taking as personal baggage, and into those very carts he had secretly loaded most of the precious goods with which he had departed from Rome.5

During their occupation of Rome, the French had in fact ransacked his mother’s house and other properties of Borgia’s allies. Some bands of Swiss mercenaries in the service of Charles VIII and a few groups of French gentlemen had remained in Rome, perhaps awaiting the outcome of their king’s expedition against Naples, perhaps intending to return to their homeland. Things indeed were no better for Charles VIII, who had made a triumphant entry into Naples, but whose army had been struck by a plague and, above all, who now had to contend with a powerful anti-French alliance forged by Alexander VI. Just as swiftly as he had descended upon Italy, so swiftly he attempted to flee away. He tried to secure an audience with the Pope, but the latter ensured that this did not happen, and as the king made his way up the peninsula, the allied army defeated him, making his retreat even more difficult.
With the French threat repelled and their presence in Rome eliminated, the Borgias now set their sights on taking revenge on those Roman barons who had colluded with the invaders and were still resisting Vatican authority. Among these, the Orsini family and their military wing, led by the skilled commander Bartolomeo d’Aviano, certainly stood out. The Holy See’s military operations were led by Giovanni Borgia, the Duke of Gandia and Cesare’s brother, who achieved mediocre results and was forced to accept a modest peace with the Roman nobles. For a year and a half, the roles within the papal family remained firmly as planned by Alexander VI: he as the supreme mind, Cesare as the Church’s controlling arm and his clerical successor, Giovanni as the armed and political arm, and Lucrezia and the other siblings as useful pawns to be exploited through strategic marriages.
In June 1497, Alexander VI convened a secret council of cardinals to deliberate on the coronation of the new King of Naples, Frederick II. In exchange for the Vatican’s sacred investiture of the throne, the Pope had demanded control of the city of Benevento, which was to become a duchy under the authority of the Church. The most delicate aspect of Rodrigo’s decision, however, was that it was Giovanni Borgia who was to obtain that duchy, which would become hereditary. Some elderly cardinals tried in vain to protest yet another desecration of canon law: corruption and threats had forced the majority of the college to acquiesce, and in the end they accepted the Pope’s decision. As the second item on the agenda, Alexander also informed them that Cardinal Cesare Borgia would be the one to crown Frederick II King of Naples, in his capacity as papal legate. The plans of the head of the Borgia family were, however, soon to change: a week later, Giovanni Borgia was assassinated during a night-time ride. Many people, particularly those hostile to the Borgia family, have attempted to accuse Cesare of ordering his brother’s murder, citing political rivalry as the motive; however, the lack of evidence, the bias of the rumourmongers, and the apparently good relationship he had with Giovanni cast doubt on this theory.

(1498-1503)

YEAR 1498: WINDS OF MARRIAGE
On 1 October, Cesare set sail for France to celebrate his wedding, as Charlotte of Naples was living at the court of Louis XII. His arrival in Marseille caused quite a stir among the chroniclers, as did his subsequent journeys to Lyon and Turenne. The splendour displayed by the new Duke Valentino was so extraordinary that it even overshadowed the sovereign himself: banquets with endless courses, exquisitely elegant and supremely fine garments, and a retinue of ladies, knights, horses and ships in his wake. The whole of southern and central France was in an uproar at the appearance of the Pope’s son. The gates of the French societies were already open for him. Caesar’s charisma captivated the hearts of both the masses and the French nobility. An anonymous chronicler, as the Roman procession passed by, remarked that he had seen ‘the most handsome man of the century’. Even Cardinal Giuliano della Rovere, who was living in Avignon at the time, had welcomed him with a friendly demeanour and had, for the moment, buried the hatchet with the Borgias.1
(Psychological Warfare: Reinforcements-Social Engineering: Influence Building/Charisma)
The only person in the whole of France who was not captivated by Cesare was the very one who ought to have been: Charlotte of Naples had no intention whatsoever of marrying the Duke, having already pledged herself to a French gentleman with whom she was deeply in love. Despite pressure from King Louis, who feared that the failure of the marriage proposal might cause the Borgias to change their minds and return to the arms of the Aragonese, and despite the international tension sparked by her sheer stubbornness, the girl did not give in. Frederick II, on the other hand, supported his daughter's decision, fearing that Cesare might seek to depose him from the throne, even though the former cardinal had promised to place his sword at his disposal for the defence of Naples. Ultimately, they had to yield to the princess’s whims; one could certainly not force the daughter of another king — and a guest at that — into a marriage against her will. Louis XII, however, was quick to present another suitable match to Cesare: Charlotte of Albret, his niece and the sister of the King of Navarre. She did not bring the same prospects as her Neapolitan namesake, but with the initial plan having fallen through, Cesare agreed that she was the best option left in France. He gritted his teeth and swallowed his pride, just so he could finally get down to business. Eventually, the union between the two would become a happy, although short, marriage.

Neither ally, however, trusted the other; Louis, in particular, doubted the good faith of the Pope’s family. And he was not wrong at all. The secret strategy of the Pope and Cesare was to exploit the king’s military support to secure the creation of a kingdom for the Duke Valentino, and then to reconstitute an anti-French league to drive the invaders out from the peninsula as soon as possible. The double-dealing planned by Alexander VI was brilliantly put into practice at the French court by his son.

It was during this period that Cesare fought the most important of his pitched battles: the Battle of Calmazzo. While leading around eight thousand men to reach central Emilia, he was intercepted by seven thousand men from the conspirators' league, led by Vitellozzo Vitelli. The battle initially favored Valentino, although he had been caught by surprise: he ordered a heavy infantry assault and with it routed Vitellozzo's vanguard, then occupied a high ground from which he gained a strategically superior position. The traitor then attacked in turn with his heavy infantry, which would have broken Borgia's defenses if he had not skillfully ordered his light infantry to launch a flank attack that took the enemy flank. Vitellozzo seemed on the verge of surrender when another five thousand troops, coming from the south, rushed to his support and surrounded Cesare's forces. At that point, he ordered a retreat, in which he lost many men.
(Tactics: Execution/Timing)
Cesare went back to his safe positions and his response then took three directions.
First, he tried to gain time, and to do so he had to slow down the enemy's action. He therefore sent Roberto Orsini, one of the few Orsini family members who had remained loyal to him and his father, to negotiate with the conspirators, promising them pay raises if they returned to his service. He also informed them that the intercepted letter from the Pope (which he evidently already knew about) was a forged document produced by the Pope's rivals in Rome.
(Deception: Misrediction/Sophistry-Planning: Formulation/Process/Coverage-Counteraction: Tactical Response-Thinking: Speed)
Secondly, in order to prevent the conspirators from allying themselves with Florence, he sent a proposal for a military alliance to the Republic and invited one of their ambassadors to discuss it.
(Counteraction: Trap Evasion)
Third point: arm yourself as much as possible. The fortresses in his dominions were strengthened and the recruitment of new soldiers increased.
(Strategy: Castling)
On October 6th, however, things seemed to be going badly for him. News reached him that Urbino had fallen due to the treachery of some pro-Montefeltro nobles, who had risen up and killed Caesar's envoys, also thanks to the conspirators' support. Within a short time, the Duchy of Urbino had fallen under his control, allowing his enemies to advance.
The Allied forces totaled over ten thousand men, perhaps twelve thousand. They were therefore numerically superior, and felt they were winning the battle. Bentivoglio troops invaded Caesar's fortifications in Romagna, while forces in the Duchy of Urbino cut off supply lines and prevented Caesar's soldiers from retreating. The conspirators' brilliant strategy had locked the Valentino in a vise.
Meanwhile, Machiavelli reached Cesare as Florentine ambassador. His republic had in effect already decided how to proceed: it would not ally itself with the conspirators or the duke, but would simply wait and see the outcome of the battle. Machiavelli was surprised to see Cesare remain calm and determined despite the worsening situation (even Venice was now facing the threatening situation, waiting for him to make a false move). While Cesare's confidence was partly a bluff, his determination was not. He declared himself certain of victory. Machiavelli perceived that not allying with him had been a mistake on the part of the Republic.

Meanwhile, his army continued to grow. He hired mercenaries from Switzerland, recruited other men from his own territories, received troops from his brother-in-law d’Albert on behalf of the King of France, and obtained military aid from Pisa. Since these troops were strangers to him, Caesar did everything he could to befriend them and endear them to his cause: he spent time and trained with them. He also ordered the troops barricaded in indefensible positions to flee and try to reach him. In any case, he was already refining his plan to massacre the conspirators.16

By this point, Cesare's army had grown so massive that the conspirators began to fear they could no longer face him in open battle. A new Colmazzo would have been disastrous for them, especially given the superior artillery Borgia had gained. For this reason, some of them began attempting to initiate peace negotiations with him. Cesare put a brave face on things and seemed to receive these proposals with lukewarm approval. In reality, he had already identified the most volatile member of the conspiracy with whom he could begin to put his plan into motion: Paolo Orsini. It was he who presented himself to Cesare on October 25, 1502, thanks to the mediation of his cousin Roberto Orsini. Cesare welcomed him with great friendship and smiles and proposed a peace agreement: his generals would regain all their titles and possessions, receive a raise, and be immune to his influence; he even offered the name of Louis XII as a guarantee. Paolo Orsini, enthusiastic about such favorable concessions, immediately signed the treaty and assured that his companions would do the same shortly.
(Manipulation: Direct-Deception: Misrediction/Acting Skills/Sophistry-Strategy: Building)
The agreement proposed by Valentino was, however, rejected by the other conspirators, who criticized the ease with which Paolo Orsini had been persuaded, and who resumed their attacks on the duke's positions. He then sent Paolo Orsini, his unwitting Trojan horse, new peace agreements, this time more binding on him. The King of France's guarantee was in fact of little use, given that he and Valentino were close friends. This move, too, was actually intended to divert the attention of the more intelligent traditors, who this time agreed to engage in discussions. Paolo Orsini was easy prey to persuade, but not Vitellozzo, and even more so Baglioni. The fake first proposal was intended precisely to make the second more palatable.

On November 9, the conspirators accepted the new, modified conditions. A month later, the Bentivoglio of Bologna secretly signed separate pacts, mediated by the Duke of Ferrara and the Republic of Florence, with whom they allied themselves with Cesare in a subordinate position, allowing him the use of some of their garrisons.
(Planning: Process-Strategy: Building)
Thanks to the Montefeltros' isolation, engineered by Cesare within the conspirators' circle, Guidobaldo da Montefeltro was forced to abandon the reconquered Duchy of Urbino peacefully, without the Borgia having to fire a single shot. Indeed, it was his own former companions in treachery who had pressured him to swiftly carry out Valentino's orders. The same happened in Camerino with the Varano family. They were doing his own job, without knowing their fate would have been far worse.

At that time, Romagna was experiencing a food crisis due to speculation by some merchants, who had withdrawn basic foodstuffs to raise prices and profit. This was no simple mercantile organization, however: at the head of these economic operations was Ramiro de Luca, one of the most powerful officials in Cesare's administration. To further gain popular support, the duke had these merchants arrested and distributed grain at a ridiculously low price, earning him acclaim in many cities of Romagna. He then ordered Ramiro's arrest, took the evidence secretly gathered over months to the court and found him guilty of treason, having him publicly beheaded and quartered in Cesena.17 The news of  the execution of his former subordinate was spread directly by the Valentino to impress in all of Italy his fame of justice’s prince, when it was surely been some times that he knew about the unfair actions of Ramiro.
(Manipulation: Mass-Deception: Info Control-Strategy: Building-Psychological Warfare: Reinforcements-Social Engineering: Influence Building/Leadership)
While Ramiro's body still hung in the square in Cesena, Cesare ordered his generals, now returned to their ranks, to organize the occupation of Senigallia, a city under the control of the Della Rovere family. The only one who didn't answer his call was Baglioni, who was ill in Perugia. On December 28, Borgia, who was in Pesaro, received letters from Vitellozzo, the two Orsini, Oliveretto da Fermo, and other commanders informing him that Senigallia had already been taken except for the fortress. They therefore invited him to join them and settle in the city.
Was their invitation sincere, or were they, like their leader, plotting something? We can't know for sure, but Cesare believed they were up to something, and he recognized that this was the perfect moment to finally carry out the revenge he had already begun with his false promises a month earlier. The traitors had to die.
(Planning: Formulation-Counteraction: Trap Evasion)
Caesar arrived in Senigallia two days later. He gave the order to withdraw the troops from the occupied city and to close the city gates except the one he was to enter himself. Vitellozzo seemed worried at these orders, but Caesar's warm words and Paolo Orsini's belief in peace forestalled his escape.
(Deception: Sophistry-Strategy: Building-Counteraction: Tactical Response-Field Crafting: Setting Traps)
The meeting with the conspirators once again demonstrated the Duke's acting skills. He appeared affable and cheerful, embracing them. The previous evening, having gathered his eight most faithful companions, he had given them the orders they needed to carry out in eliminating the generals. When the company reached the city gates, the conspirators attempted to escape Caesar's presence, saying they had to return to their troops camped outside the city. If they had devised a plan to eliminate him, this was the moment they would reunite to deliver the final blow. Caesar, however, insisted that they accompany him into the city to discuss its administration. A moment of hesitation on their faces was fatal, because the drawbridge was quickly lowered, and they could no longer refuse to follow their master. They entered the city and followed him to the Bernardini palace. They passed through a courtyard, climbed some stairs, and once inside, eight men emerged from hiding places, declaring them the duke's prisoners. Not even an hour later, their troops camped outside the city were routed by Cesare himself, destroying the remains of the conspirator army.

After brief discussions that were supposed to be a trial, the conspirators admitted their treasonous intentions and were killed one by one by Michelotto Corella. Machiavelli had just arrived in Senigallia when the massacre began. Compliments from all over Italy arrived to the Valentino for his brilliant deception.
