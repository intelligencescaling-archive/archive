Misconception of  ayanokoji 's antifeat 50s scoring and other misconceptions of others scaling system

Vol. 0 + Rest

Ayanokoji Kiyotaka's character has been around in the SCD community as a whole since its inception and has been greatly discussed. However, there hasn't been any meaningful consensus regarding his scaling. Usually, it's due to the other side not accepting his Koji scaling and falling into various pitfalls in SCD or being generally ignorant. Other times, it's due to his scalers themselves, who also get his scaling wrong. The general idea of his scaling is shaped by others, though.

However, despite being overdiscussed for the wrong reasons, I still see many misconceptions about his scaling and anti-feats that I don't believe anyone should think are valid. People also use claims about writing as a last-ditch effort to invalidate his scaling, feats, and schemes.

So, I'm going to explain a few misconceptions so that these disingenuous takes don't cover his scaling as a whole. I will not give my opinion, and even if I do, it's going to be neutral. It's up to you to decide his scaling range depending on your roster.

***Scoring 50s shows ayanokoji can't survive irl based situation & setting***

This is one of the biggest misconceptions I've seen repeatedly. **Deliberately** scoring 50s doesn't downscale Ayanokouji, especially when you consider that, if he had selected 50 as his entrance exam score, he would likely have been placed in Class B or C based on his abilities. The same applies to Horikita, whose entrance exam scores would likely have placed her in Class A or B.

The series literally reveals that entrance exam scores and interviews are just a smokescreen. ANHS scouts its student candidates based on its own criteria, with the goal of creating ideal citizens and elites for Japanese society.

Later, in Volume 1, we get hints that Ayanokoji deliberately scored 50s on his preliminary exams. He answers odd-numbered questions correctly and even-numbered questions incorrectly, despite differences in their difficulty.

Volume 1's literal premise is that Ayanokoji is hiding his abilities from the readers, raising the question of why he's doing so.

Volume 2 further questions why Ayanokoji would deliberately score 50s across the board and go out of his way to help others if he supposedly wants to avoid trouble. Chabashira explains this:

\[“Why do you think Ayanokouji purposefully got scores of 50 points
across on the board on his entrance examination? Why do you think
Ayanokouji is helping you? Why do you think that Ayanokouji doesn’t put
himself forward as a superior student, despite having superior ability? Is
Ayanokouji Kiyotaka really someone who ‘dislikes trouble’?”

“That’s…”

If he truly wanted to prioritize peace and tranquility, then why score 50
points in all subjects and allow himself to draw this much attention? Had he
deliberately poked his nose into this incident as well? I wondered if he ought
to have been carefully monitored, like many other students. As Chabashira-
sensei said, his behavior didn’t fit the pattern of someone who “disliked
trouble.” That unconscious realization must have been the cause of my earlier
discomfort.

\]

And it's not like Ayanokoji has bad social skills when Horikita herself acknowledges that he's quite good at socializing.

This implies that he's deliberately playing dumb around others, as suggested toward the end of Volume 2. Don't take it at face value, even as readers; it's an implication of the unreliable narrator trope, nothing else. This also ties into the significance of the persona theme in both Volumes 1 and 2.

Later, Volume 3 showcases the persona Ayanokouji maintains throughout his semester, while subsequent volumes continue to emphasize the conflict between his persona and his actual self, as well as how he controls his persona around others to facilitate his schemes.

For example, in the X strategy, Ayanokoji replicates the persona that Ryuen expects from X while concealing his own movements and misleading Ryuen throughout the entire process.

There are many other notable examples showing that he's quite good at socializing overall. The series is literally set in a social environment, so saying that Kiyo lacks social awareness and skills is a misinterpretation that doesn't make sense. Socializing is one of his strongest suits. From the beginning and throughout the volumes, the series shows him navigating social dynamics despite presenting himself as someone who's bad at them.

Taking his backstory into account further reinforces . One of the things Ayanokoji did in the White Room was experiment with social interactions among the other children to gain information  while maintaining the appearance of being under the researchers' control.

The White Room curriculum also included various subjects related to social interaction and psychology, which Ayanokoji was capable of studying. Combined with his rapid learning, acting ability, and the experiences he accumulated between leaving the White Room's fourth generation and entering ANHS, his demonstrated social skills provide further evidence of his competence in realistic social environments while maintaining the guise of being a background character.

Simply because Ayanokoji doesn't seek the spotlight or place himself at the center of every situation doesn't mean he lacks social skills or the ability to function in realistic settings.

***Ayanokoji operate in small scale so his scaling bad***

According to people who use the C-System scale, whether intentionally or unintentionally, operating on a small scale nerfs Ayanokoji's scaling.

>C System involves scaling characters based on the domain of their effective outsmarting.

[https://scdnexus.fandom.com/wiki/Character\_Rankings](https://scdnexus.fandom.com/wiki/Character_Rankings)

I find the claim even more contradictory by its own definition. ANHS is a replica of Japanese society in the sense that it wants to create ideal citizens while also creating elites for the future. The first two special exams literally aim to train students for cooperative and real-life situations, both thematically and operationally.

The class structure in ANHS reflects Japanese society, where having superior abilities can allow you to get away with anything, and individualism is penalized or even punished by the system in favor of systemic meritocracy led by elite leaders.

(A lot more can be said about ANHS, such as how class leaders showcase different types of leadership and how small-scale politics exist between classes, etc. This could easily be its own post.)

Nonoa Miyamae (Roshidere Light Novel) is tier 2 in the -c- scale with reasons

>Nonoa Miyamae exemplifies Tier 2 systemic manipulation within a bounded but politically intricate ecosystem. Her capacity to weaponize psychology, optics, and procedural exploits against rival factions demonstrates mastery of interconnected social-institutional systems while operating under strict exposure risk.

>Her schemes consistently escalate from interpersonal manipulation to institutional sabotage, cementing her as a high-tier localized strategist with systemic reach.

I don't know much about Nonoa Miyamae, but by these standards, Ayanokoji is definitely Tier 2 as well, considering how many feats he has that literally break the system's intended outcomes. For example, in Volume 1, he saves Sudo, followed by Volume 2, where he saves Sudo again while maneuvering allies toward the correct path and remaining hidden. He also evaluates Horikita for the student council, deals with Chabashira's counteraction, and the Rumor Mail , etc.

In the series, we're informed that Class D and Year 1 would normally have many expelled students. However, due to Ayanokoji's interference, his year is anomalous compared to ANHS's history.

Taking the WR lore into account, we see that Ayanokoji evaded the researchers' scrutiny throughout his time in the White Room while experimenting with other children. Ayanokoji learned about lying at a very young age and used it to his benefit when interacting with others. This carries over into the main story, where we see him lying and manipulating information with to facilitate his schemes.

That's all when taking the main story into account. If we also take his background story into account, Ayanokoji comes close to touching Tier 2.5.

***Ayanokoji don't have good operational proxy usage so his scaling bad***

This is a MAC (Multi-Agent Complexity) claim that somehow misses the methodology behind Ayanokoji's feats and schemes.

> Multi-Agent Complexity (MAC) measures the number, autonomy, and diversity of agents within a system that a strategist must influence, coordinate, or outmaneuver.

>It’s not just raw headcount—it’s about how independently those actors operate, how divergent their goals are, and how interdependent the network is.

>short: are you outsmarting a room, an organization, or a thousand minds across ideologies?

In Year 1, he was against Class C under Ryuen, Class A's agents under Arisu, and improving Class D through Horikita while helping Class B and sowing the seeds of his plan for Year 2.

In the first island exam, he was against two factions from Classes C and A, and he figured out their schemes while furthering mutual cooperation between Classes B and D, performing a leader swap, etc., all while remaining concealed. The only people who knew what Ayanokoji was doing were Horikita and Arisu.

In the X arc, there were multiple groups, such as the Zodiac Exam teams, the Sports Festival teams, the Paper Shuffle groups, Ryuen and his group, Horikita, Kushida, Chabashira, Manabu and the student council drama, the Rumor Mail incident, year 2 plot basically etc

Taking the wider lore into account, we also have the WR factions, the pro-Ayanokoji and anti-Ayanokoji groups, the Kijima Foundation, etc.

Kiyo literally operates using proxies and pawns. For example, in Volume 1, he uses Kushida. In Volume 2, he manages relationships between three classes during the court case while helping Horikita without letting her know. He also makes use of Kei's influence, Horikita's actions, the Ayanokoji Group, and the recent island exam, among other examples.

The conflicts involve different ideologies, class dynamics, and politics, which Ayanokoji manages through multiple variables to facilitate his schemes, and there are many more examples.

***Ayanokoji Didn't Face Adversity, Opposition, or Friction, So His Scaling Is Bad***

This might look true, but it's only a surface-level interpretation that doesn't hold up under deeper examination.

I don't understand this criticism when the series literally portrays Ayanokoji as the type of strategist who operates behind the scenes and mainly relies on indirect methods to execute his schemes.

It's not as though he never faces friction or opposition. In reality, he repeatedly involves himself in problems that he must solve, as demonstrated from Volume 1 to Volume 3 and throughout the rest of the series.

He solves problems and helps others grow despite the friction this creates. For example, in Volume 1, he solves class-related issues while continuing to face opposition from his classmates. In Volumes 2 and 3, Chabashira blackmails him, which makes things harder for him. In every volume, there's opposition and friction against Ayanokoji, or there's a problem that Ayanokoji solves.

I think what people mean by "adversity, opposition, and friction" is that they expect him to face direct, face-to-face challenges. That makes sense, as I also believe intellectual adversity brings out the best in any character.

However, Ayanokoji has his fair share of situational and contextual adversity. It's just harder to notice because his character is literally always on guard and eliminates adversity before it grows, as seen in the cases involving Arisu, Nagumo, Ryuen, the WR, etc.

On the other hand, I believe some people want Ayanokoji to be a reactive strategist or a proactive planner who directly confronts his opponents. However, that's simply how his character operates. He's mainly an indirect, proactive character. I don't understand the idea of projecting expectations of direct confrontation onto him and then using their absence to downplay his feats. It's not as though his feats lack structure or methodology.

And taking the lore of his character into account, he did, in fact, face adversity in the WR throughout his years there. People don't find that interesting because it's long-term adversity, both in ANHS and the WR. Though there are also some short-term situations in the series where we see him facing adversity.
