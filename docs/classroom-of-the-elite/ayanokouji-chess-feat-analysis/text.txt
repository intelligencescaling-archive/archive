Feat 2: Calculations which exceeded the calculation capacity of Tsukishiro’s multiple computers:
Now to look at how we can evaluate Ayanokouji’s chess feat without this specific information. Simply put, we take the information Tsukishiro provided and contextualize it.

As seen here, Tsukishiro explained that Ayanokouji had made a move that was “even better than the best move they could have come up with” while having an entire team working on analysis of the game. That team also included the use of multiple computers assisting the evaluation of the game.
Knowing this we can look into what making such a move actually means.
While we don’t know the exact computers Tsukishiro used or the exact chess engine he was running, but given the fact that COTE is confirmed to take place in the future we know that the technology he has access to should be at least relative to our current technology and likely more advanced. COTE takes place at least around 10 years in the future so computer technology should be much more advanced than today’s but since we don't know how powerful computers will be 20 years from now we will create a calculation of just how impressive this feat is by using current technology as a low ball estimate.
For reference on how quickly computers can advance we can look at a chess related example. In 1996 IBM created a supercomputer called Deep Blue designed to play chess. It played a series of matches vs the World Champion at the time Gary Kasparov. Kasparov managed to win but less than a year later they played a rematch which Deep Blue won fairly easily. This was the last time human ability exceeded computers at chess. About 18 years ago that computer could calculate approximately 200 million board positions per second based on its hardware. When a computer plays chess there are 2 main components, the hardware and the software. The hardware determines the calculation ability of the computer, meaning how many positions it can analyze per second. The software takes those analyzed positions and evaluates them. For example if today Stockfish played Deep Blue it would win since it is much better on the software side and evaluates positions far more efficiently.
Deep Blue had 11.38 GFLOPS of processing power which was massive at the time. However, by today’s standards that would be incredibly subpar. Now computing power is measured in TFLOPS with 1 TFLOP = 1000 GFLOPS. An iPhone has around 10-15 TFLOPS of computing power while an average desktop can have 60 TFLOPS or more. This means over about a 15 year span, now an average phone has about 1000-2000x the computing power of Deep Blue while a PC has more than 5000 times the computing power of Deep Blue. At this rate the computers in COTE could realistically have thousands of times the computing power of current hardware in just 10-15 years.

The misconception that many people believe regarding this feat being that Ayanokouji outperformed a supercomputer which could play itself 20 million times a second is laughable. Not only is this number never mentioned in the Light Novels, even computer’s 20 years ago could compute 10 times that. Given that COTE takes place in the future such a number is extremely low. Especially since a chess engine running on an iPhone could calculate anywhere from 200 billion to 400 billion positions per second. This certainly disproves the 20 million moves per second fallacy.
If Tsukishiro was running a chess engine on average computer hardware as of today’s standards (2023) with about 60 TFLOPS of computing power, his machines could calculate about 1.014 Trillion positions per second each at full capacity. For simplicity we will round to 1 Trillion.
So now that we have a better understanding of how much computing power modern computers have and that the ones Tsukishiro used could even be more advanced, the fact that Ayanokouji’s move was better than the best move they could come up with is becoming more impressive. But how do we know that Ayanokouji out calculated those computers when finding his move? To understand this we need to quickly address how an engine determines the best move.
Chess engines can only analyze a certain number board positions given the hardware it runs on and the more powerful that is the more it can analyze. A common way that this is addressed is websites such as Chess.com or Lichess.com host a chess engine connected to an online server. This allows an engine to access an entire system of computers hosted elsewhere. This is how many people can utilize an online engine like that at once since each user is only able to access a fraction of that total computing power. However, running an engine directly on a computer is often more efficient and more powerful than the sliver of the server you can access. However the actual evaluation is done by the software/engine itself. A chess engine starts with a process called alpha-beta pruning to quickly discard objectively bad moves from the start.
In further detail, the process of alpha-beta pruning is used to reduce the total number of calculations they need to do in order to find the best move. The computers create move trees from each possible move that branches out for each possible move from the starting move. If it evaluates a tree as not having a chance of producing a better position than the best one so far it prunes the tree. This helps engines not have to calculate for hours to find the best move. This is how an engine discards objectively bad moves without needing to spend resources to evaluate them. This process is taken into account when looking at how many move combinations per second engines can calculate. That means if an engine calculates 1 trillion positions per second that means 1 trillion “good” positions, not including the bad ones which were discarded. This is important because Ayanokouji and the engine would eliminate the same kinds of moves.
Since I have explained that the engine has a limited amount of calculation power to use the smarter the engine the better/more efficiently it can work through this process. The more moves it can discard here the more in depth on the good moves it can analyze. For example, If a computer has 50 legal moves they can make we can assume 25-30 of those moves are objectively bad. Exactly how bad would depend on each one individually but the more of these the engine can eliminate means it can look at the other 20-25 moves in more depth. If it had to analyze 50 moves it may only be able to look 10 moves ahead but with only 20 moves it can look 15-20 moves ahead. Since in both cases looking ahead would determine the bad moves and the engine would not make them. This process saves the engine time and processing power to be used on better moves. However, by eliminating objectively bad moves that doesn’t mean the engine calculates fewer moves. It just means it looks deeper at better ones.
Additionally, it is possible that Tsukishiro was using something similar since connecting a chess computer to a server to boost processing power is a common practice. However, while making such an assumption would mean Ayanokouji would have needed to make a MUCH larger calculation there is no evidence Tsukishiro used a server so it won’t be included in the calculations for the feat.
The next stage involves taking the moves which are not objectively bad and rating them. This rating involves looking at how the pieces are placed, what pieces are left on the board, and how those pieces can interact with others. Then the engine looks ahead as far as it can based on its calculation power and will find the path where the farthest move it can calculate has the best rating. A computer will determine that a move which gives a short term loss for long term benefit is better than short term gain. Meaning that a chess engine will always prioritize long-term benefit when analyzing moves because those moves give the best chance to ultimately win the game.
So from this we know how the chess engine determines what it believes to be the best move each time and we can see how Ayanokouji would be able to find a move that was better than its best move. The engine would evaluate the position and find its “best move” it thinks Ayanokouji should make, then Ayanokouji makes his move which would be different from what the engine decided, meaning the engine thought its move was better than the move Ayanokouji came up with. Afterward once the game progressed it would then realize that Ayanokouji’s move was better than the best move it could come up with at that point. This would be the only way Ayanokouji’s move could be better than the engine’s best move and ensure that Tsukishiro would know that. If the engine didn’t later realize his move was better Tsukishiro wouldn’t know and wouldn’t have been able to tell Ayanokouji about it later.
So in order for Ayanokouji to make a move better than the engine, he either needed to calculate a higher number of total board positions than the combined computers were able to, meaning he could analyze the game at a higher depth than the engine. Or he could have evaluated the position better and more efficiently than the engine could, leading to finding a better move than the engine at equal depth. However, Ayanokouji finding his move by evaluating better than the computers would be impossible if he didnt’ at least match the calculation of the computer (although he wouldn’t need to exceed it) because as explained earlier the engine will always prioritize long term benefits in its moves. If Ayanokouji found a “better” move but without calculating a similar number of positions and therefore calculating a lower depth than the engine his move may seem better in the short-term but wouldn’t be better overall. For example a move that appears to win a piece from your opponent looks good at first, but if that move was actually a sacrifice from the opponent to win something better a few moves later that move isn’t actually the “best move”. That same principle applies here, without matching the depth of the computers, even if Ayanokouji exceeded their evaluation level he would still need to match the calculation. That being said, this method of making his move is less likely for several reasons. Firstly, computers are designed to process data with known variables and they can do this far more efficiently than humans, if Ayanokouji is able to do this it would put his thinking, reasoning, and analysis skills far into the realm of superhuman. Second, Chess is a game where each player has perfect information which makes computers ideal for analyzing it. This means that each player knows every possible move they and their opponent can make at any given time. Because the engine is rating each possible move based on this complete set of information the engine’s ability to rate each move is perfect based on the given information. This is a main reason why if 2 chess engines play each other the game will end in a draw. Since each engine can evaluate the game from their own point of view and the opponents it can determine the best move every time. The only way to break the tie is to calculate more positions than the opponent. This is the difference between victory and defeat for chess engines. Third, because Tsukishiro knew that Ayanokouji had made a move better than the engine he must have gotten that information from his computers. Meaning the computers must have later calculated that Ayanokouji’s move was better than the one they came up with. This further supports Ayanokouji calculating more positions than the engine since after that move as the engine continued to calculate in the game it would begin to evaluate Ayanokouji’s position better after a few moves due to not correctly identifying the best move several turns ago.
Finding his move through sheer calculation is the only one that makes sense and optimally at that, it would explain how Tsukishiro knew about how good his move was and that the engine would have figured out that Ayanokouji’s move was better in hindsight.
