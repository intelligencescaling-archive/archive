Before I start, I want to clarify something: the debunk is actually about the post made on Reddit. Obvious_Ad_6250 sent me the link and said: “Hey, give your take on this,” and that ended up as the debunk. I don't blame them for anything; after all, they asked for permission to post it, as usual. However, I admit fault for not reading the doc's full explanation and knowing the whole context before speaking. That was my mistake. But that doesn't mean this feat gets saved because of it.

Well, starting from the beginning, let's talk about the core of the feat. What I understand is that Ichinose deliberately admitted to having slept with Ayanokouji (something risky for her image and the rules) as a play to gain credibility, and thus make her later lie about the White Room more believable in order to shake Amasawa.

The problem starts in the second image: https://imgur.com/a/AbM08K7

If you read the text right above the sentence he underlines, the narrator contradicts him completely. The exact sequence is:

1. Ichinose doesn't deny having slept with him and says: “You told me not to lie, Amasawa-san. Remember?”.
2. Immediately afterward, the narrator clarifies: “Ichinose had sensed the malice in Amasawa's words, but, as a senpai and a partial friend, she still tried not to hurt her”.
3. Only after Ichika insists with ill intent does the text say: “However, because the other party had ill intent, Ichinose decided to face the enemy head-on after reconsidering her decision”.

Scan:

It is logically impossible for Ichinose to have used her sexual admission as “credibility bait” to set up the White Room bluff when, at the exact moment she made that admission, she was still trying to be kind and, according to the narrator himself, hadn't even decided to go on the offensive. I feel like a future intention is being projected backward here. She didn't deny the relationship out of pride/sincerity (or to cut off Ichika's jealousy) while still acting as a patient senpai; she only changed her stance afterward.

So let's move on to the next point.

He criticized my use of “internal monologue” (when it is limited third-person narration about Ichinose's thoughts) and argues that she could indeed deduce the seriousness of the subject because Kiyo and Tsukishiro were discreet and because in Y2V8 she searched for information and found nothing. The problem is in image 3: https://imgur.com/a/NFthcTY

The text literally reveals what Ichinose believed the White Room was: “presumably ‘some high-level intensive school’”

Knowing that an academy doesn't show up on Google (Y2V8) only indicates that it is a private or little-known place, not that it is an illegal human-experimentation facility whose mere mention causes national-security panic. Ichinose thought it was a simple prep school or elite academy: therefore, she did not understand at all the true gravity or the taboo nature of the term.

Especially given the setting of the series: a private school with no connection to the outside world. A school that raises no suspicion and is the best in the country. So it wouldn't be strange for institutions that copy that model to exist. Which makes sense in their world.

Also, look at Ichinose's supposed “contingency plan” in Image 3:

That reasoning is very weak: seconds earlier, Amasawa had laughed in Ichinose's face, accusing her of making things up and overestimating her own worth. If Ichika hadn't been from the WR, she would have said “White Room? Sounds like something you made up,” and the bluff would have failed instantly. The play only worked out of pure luck, and that is the strongest reason for this feat's weakness, because the two words Ichinose happened to overhear on the uninhabited island (Y2V4) turned out to be Ichika's one exact blind spot.

Let's continue with the claim that in “0 lines” Ichinose is shown acting reactively, and he accuses me of a chronological contradiction because Ichinose looks around a page before Ichika says “nobody at this school knows more about him than me”

Right before Image 2, which was left out of the cropped scan, but which is further up in this doc, the narrator says she acted “even if the true intention of the claim wasn't clear, she decided it was better to face it head-on without running away.”, and in Image 3 it explains that Ichinose deduced Ichika's connection at that very moment from the arrogant way she was speaking (“from the way Amasawa spoke, as if she knew him better than anyone, Ichinose supposed...”). Deducing something on the fly from what your rival has just said and changing tactics in the middle of the conversation is the textbook definition of being reactive and improvising.

This is reinforced in the following scan:

Ichika had already mentioned the secrets and her superiority right before Ichinose's gesture: “Yeah, well, probably even more than you Ichinose-senpai, yeah? He's the
type to keep a lot of secrets, you know?”
.

And looking around the Keyaki Mall on a Saturday at noon (full of students having lunch) before saying “There are no more secrets between me and Ayanokouji-kun.” is basic acting and common sense. If you're pretending that someone entrusted you with their greatest secrets, you don't shout it out without checking whether there are people listening around, because you would ruin your own performance.

Continuing with the debunk, they try to correct me by saying that Ichika's goal in that scene was not to investigate WR threats, but only to “tease / toy with” Honami.

Admittedly, that is my own interpretation, based on Amasawa's actions as a stalker of Koji. I'll grant you that point. However, that makes Ichinose's feat worse.

The very scan he brings says: “She’d only intended to tease Ichinose” and “...Shoot. I'm the one getting carried away here.”

That is, Ichika wasn't even in a serious, analytical or defensive mode. She completely underestimated her, reducing her to a “busty senpai” (“big-boobed senpai”), dropped her guard entirely and started joking around.

The quality of a feat is measured by the resistance and seriousness of the opponent. Catching someone off guard with an unexpected name-drop while they are playing with you with their guard down is infinitely less impressive than manipulating a focused rival. It was more a mistake of arrogance and carelessness on Ichika's part than a display of brilliance from Ichinose.

Also, the ending is the strangest, because he tries to downplay the fact that Ichinose already knew the term “White Room,” arguing that they were just two words she barely made out on the island and that she didn't even know how to write it in katakana (ホワイトルーム) as opposed to the kanji she imagined (白い部屋)

But the fact that she didn't know how to write it in katakana is irrelevant. This was a psychological duel between Ichinose and Ichika; possessing hidden information that your opponent assumes it is impossible for you to have is a huge advantage. Ichika's whole shock reaction is not due to some brilliant deduction by Ichinose, but to the impact of hearing an ultra-secret keyword that Ichinose obtained by pure chance while spying on Tsukishiro and Shiba in Y2V4. Ichinose had only one bullet in the chamber, fired it blindly hoping it would mean something to Ichika, and was lucky enough to hit.

However, the following part does catch my attention:

He devotes an extensive block to explaining how Honami supposedly profiled Ichika, deduced her relationship with the White Room, assessed that she was not allied with Tsukishiro or Shiba because of Takuya Yagami's expulsion, and calculated the deadly danger involved in mentioning the term.

However, the narrator in Y3V1 reveals exactly what Ichinose's thought process was at the end of the scene, refuting each of those assumptions:

1. Ichinose did not suspect any link between Ichika and the White Room until the very last moment of the chat, solely because Ichika arrogantly boasted that nobody at the school knew Kiyotaka better than she did.
2. The text literally explains that Ichinose dropped the term “White Room” without even knowing whether Ichika knew it: “On the other hand, if Amasawa had never heard the term, Ichinose could have used it to imply that she knew more than she did. Either way, the result would favor Ichinose.” It is a basic positive-sum bluff (“if she knows it, she gets scared; if she doesn't, she thinks Ichinose knows a secret she is unaware of”), not a complex psychological reading.
3. In the document he claims that Honami was fully aware of how lethal the White Room is and that she assessed the agendas of Shiba, Tsukishiro and Yagami. However, the novel literally states what Ichinose thought that place was: “‘White Room,’ presumably ‘some high-level intensive school’.” As we already saw before, Ichinose doesn't even associate the term with the deadly incident on the island or with a secret organization; she believes it is simply an elite prep academy. The whole block of inferences in the document lacks support in the work.

He also inflates some categories with feats that are, well… questionable.

For example, indirectly admitting that she slept with Kiyotaka was not a calculated sacrifice to set up a bluff that didn't yet exist in her mind. It responds to her new possessive and direct side regarding her bond with him, exactly the same attitude she shows chapters later toward Karuizawa Kei when she deliberately hints that something “secret and special” happened between them.

And the most serious one is in Sensory, Social Awareness and Risk Competence.

For example, comparing the simple gesture of looking around before telling a lie with Kiyotaka's superhuman sensory abilities is a disproportionate exaggeration. Furthermore, the document's argument contradicts itself: if Ichinose had been exercising elite Risk Competence in a crowded shopping mall at lunchtime, she would have checked her surroundings before admitting that she slept with Kiyotaka (a serious violation of school rules) and before revealing her seduction strategy, not after. Looking to the sides right before saying “There are no more secrets between Ayanokouji-kun and me” is a basic theatrical device to give weight and credibility to the lie she was about to drop.

With all this, I want to arrive at a single conclusion:

The outcome of the scene confirms that there was no such unilateral “destruction” of Ichika:

After Ichinose leaves, Ichika is neither intimidated nor neutralized; instead she smiles excitedly, acknowledges that Ichinose is no longer just a naive girl, and decides to take the third year seriously. Meanwhile, Ichinose leaves believing she discovered that her kouhai attended a “high-level intensive school”.

The problem here is that the central premises directly contradict the character's explicit thoughts in the novel; this doesn't demonstrate a better reading of the work, but a sustained overinterpretation outside the text. In SCD this may be normal, and especially in COTE where Kinugasa writes as a mystery author, but when it contradicts canon itself, it becomes a problem.
