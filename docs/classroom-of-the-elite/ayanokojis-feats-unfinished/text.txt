In the academic class exam, Koji identified a loophole in the rules and restrictions during the test. While it was explicitly forbidden to talk, use cell phones, pens, or notes during the test, a gap existed in the rules that allowed for non-verbal communication between students as they left the classroom one by one to solve problems. Students were taking a test individually in the classroom, but they had to wait in the hallway when they were not actively working on a problem. As students left the classroom to submit their completed problems or take a break, they briefly passed each other in the hallway. This created a momentary opportunity for interaction. To take advantage of this situation without violating the no-talking rule, students devised a system of non-verbal communication using hand signs. These hand signs were used to indicate which problem the student should focus on when they re-entered the classroom. For instance, if a student wanted to convey that the 55th problem needed attention, they might use a specific hand sign, such as showing their right hand with two pairs of fingers crossed twice. Similarly, they could use hand signs to communicate any problem number to the next student. Importantly, the act of using hand signs to instruct another student on which problem to solve did not violate the rules against cheating. Since the communication was limited to indicating which problem to work on and did not involve discussing answers or solutions, it was considered a permissible form of communication. By using this method, students could concentrate on solving problems more effectively without having to verbally communicate or disrupt the no-talking rule, thus maintaining the integrity of the test conditions. This loophole allowed students to work together indirectly, ensuring that each student could tackle the problems that suited their strengths and weaknesses.
