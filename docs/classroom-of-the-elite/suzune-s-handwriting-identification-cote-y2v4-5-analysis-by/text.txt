In Japan, calligraphy (shodō) is considered a reflection of one’s mental state and character (kokoro). That is why it is said that “the line does not lie.”

In the case of kanji, where stroke order (hitsu-jun) and pressure are fundamental, concealing “beautiful” handwriting presents unique challenges. Even if one tries to write poorly or differently, subconscious elements betray an experienced writer:

Flow and speed: A person with beautiful handwriting has natural fluency. When trying to hide it, they tend to write more slowly, producing microscopic tremors or unnatural pauses that Suzune could detect.

Kanji structure: Skilled writers instinctively maintain balance between radicals and negative space (yohaku). Even when distorted, the proportional structure often remains.

Stroke pressure (fude-atsu): The pressure applied at the start (tome), during turns, or at the end of strokes (hane or harai) functions like a fingerprint. It is extremely difficult to fake erratic pressure when one’s hand is accustomed to precision.

Stroke order: In Japanese, stroke order is sacred. Someone trained in calligraphy follows it through muscle memory. Even if they attempt to hide their identity, perfect stroke order reveals education and practice that most ordinary people lack.

There were still ways to falsify handwriting:

Using the non-dominant hand: This appears childish and sloppy, but often retains the original writer’s directional habits and stroke order.

Changing the slant: Easy at first, but as the hand tires, it reverts to its natural angle.

Imitating another style: This requires such a high cognitive load that the writing becomes rigid and loses the natural feel of kanji.

Leaving absolutely zero traces would only be possible if the person had specific training in forgery or forensic graphology—highly unlikely for a high school student—or if they used tools that limited fine control, such as a thick, worn marker. That was not the case here, as they used ordinary pens.
