Pillar One: Exploiting Heuristic Horizon Blindness

To understand why the engine flagged Ayanokōji's winning move as "inferior", we have to understand how engines actually evaluate a board.

________________

Engines do not know they are winning in a conceptual sense. They use an evaluation function that assigns mathematical weights to the board (e.g. a Queen is worth 9 points, a Rook is 5, controlling the center adds +0.5, etc.). Because an engine cannot calculate to the end of the game due to the sheer size of the search tree, it calculates to a specific depth limit (its horizon), stops and tallies the points.

________________

* If Ayanokōji initiates a massive positional sacrifice - say, giving up a Rook and a Knight to permanently damage Sakayanagi's pawn structure and open a fatal diagonal - the payoff for that sacrifice might not materialise for 30 moves.

* If the computer’s calculation horizon stops at move 20, it only sees that Ayanokōji lost 8 points of material for nothing. The evaluation function instantly drops his score and flags his move as a blunder.

* Ayanokōji operates on goal directed behavior. That leads me to believe that he isn't essentially scoring points, he is establishing a win condition. He saw past the machine's horizon because human conceptual understanding doesn't rely on short term mathematical tallying.

Pillar Two: Forcing-Move Bottlenecking

 A counter argument you might see is: "Even if he saw the goal 30 moves away, the branching factor of chess means there are trillions of variations in between. He still had to calculate them all."

 This is mathematically false. Ayanokōji bypassed that trap of endless possibilities through Forcing Moves.

Utilising Game Theory allowed me to validate this:

 In game theory, the complexity of a decision tree is determined by bd, where b is the branching factor (the average legal moves per turn, typically ~30 in a middlegame) and d is the depth (the number of turns).

* A normal 10 move sequence generates a search space of 3010 nodes - impossible for a human to calculate.

* However, if Ayanokōji plays a sequence composed entirely of forcing moves (checks, capture threats, and mate threats), he can dictate the pace of the board. Sakayanagi is forced to respond to the immediate threat to survive.

* By doing this, Ayanokōji reduces the branching factor, b, from 30, down to 1 or 2. The math changes from 3010 to 210.

He didn't calculate millions of useless branches. He used Forcing-Move Bottlenecking to choke out Sakayanagi's legal responses, collapsing a complex game into a narrow, linear tunnel that led directly to his predetermined terminal node.
