Analysis on suzune's kiyotaka self exposure tactic by ganve(y1v11.5,y2v2)

During the spring break, Suzune and Kiyotaka made a promise to compete against each other to see who could achieve the higher score in a written exam subject.

If Kiyotaka won, Suzune would join the Student Council. If Suzune won, Kiyotaka would, without holding anything back, use the abilities he had been hiding over the past year for the benefit of her class.

It had already been announced that even students with an “A” rating in Academic Ability would struggle to score above 90 points. With an exam of such difficulty, a draw was virtually impossible—neither of them could realistically achieve a perfect score. Suzune considered this exam the ideal stage for their competition.

To ensure there would be no excuses, she made a point of reminding Kiyotaka that he had to study properly.

On the day of the exam, the chosen subject was mathematics. Suzune scored 87 points, an impressive result considering that even an A-ranked student like Yukimura barely managed to reach 84 points. The exam was so demanding that the 80-point threshold itself posed a major barrier for most students, regardless of the subject.

After all, approximately 10% of the exam covered concepts beyond the first-year curriculum, not to mention the inherent difficulty of the problems themselves. No one in the class managed to score above 90 in any subject— except for Kiyotaka, who achieved a perfect 100 points in mathematics, winning the bet beyond any doubt.

Suzune had given everything she had and was satisfied with her result, especially since she didn’t even understand what the final questions were about—topics such as measure theory and Lebesgue integrals.

The class was in an uproar. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka had achieved a perfect score on an exam designed to be impossible for second-year students like them. No one else had managed anything close. Not Sakayanagi. Not Ichinose. Not  Suzune.

Yukimura, proud of his strong academic performance and one of Kiyotaka’s closest peers, was the most confused of all. Karuizawa attempted to offer a few vague explanations, but none of them convinced him. With several girls blindly backing Kei, the conversation quickly escalated into an open argument.

Hasebe, Miyake, Maezono, Shinohara, Ike, Miyamoto, Mikami, and others joined in with their own theories: that he had used a method similar to last year’s exams, that he was a mathematical prodigy who had been hiding his abilities, or even that the exam itself contained flaws.

Hirata tried to calm the situation by suggesting that those with club activities should leave to avoid being late to their extracurriculars, but the tension did not subside.

It was in that atmosphere that Suzune decided to intervene when Kiyotaka returned to the classroom.

Neither Yukimura nor any other second-year student had achieved a perfect score on that exam. So how was it possible that Ayanokōji had done it?

Suzune voiced the question aloud. Although she directed it at Yukimura, it was obvious that everyone was thinking the same thing.

Yukimura did not avoid answering. He admitted that the exam had been chaotic even for him. The final questions were, without exaggeration, impossible. And yet, Ayanokōji had solved them with no apparent difficulty. What was most unsettling was not just the result, but his attitude: he did not boast, did not explain anything, showed no pride at all. He simply remained silent.

That only made everything seem even more suspicious.

Eventually, Yukimura put into words what many were afraid to think: maybe he had cheated. Maybe he had known the answers beforehand.

Suzune did not dodge the accusation. She confronted it directly. She admitted that, from an outside perspective, anyone would think the same. If she did not know the truth, she would be suspicious as well. But she firmly stated that this was not the case.

To explain Kiyotaka’s perfect score, she said, they had to go back to the spring of the previous year, when everyone had just arrived at the school.

She reminded them that she and Kiyotaka had been seatmates, and that from very early on, she had realized he was exceptional academically—more so than herself.

Yukimura objected immediately. According to the official records, Kiyotaka had always been average. A C rating in academic ability. Nothing remarkable.

Suzune replied that this, too, had been part of a strategy.

From the very beginning, Kiyotaka had possessed the ability to achieve perfect scores—at least in mathematics. Knowing this, she had asked him to deliberately hold back during the initial exams. Not to harm the class, but to maintain a low profile. A trump card. Insurance for the future. That was how his academic ability came to be evaluated as a C.

To many, that explanation had holes. If Kiyotaka had performed at his best from the start, the class would have earned more points. Yukimura could not understand the logic behind hiding such talent.

Suzune pointed out that if he had stood out from April onward, figures like Sakayanagi, Ichinose, or Ryūen would have set their sights on him immediately. And at this school, drawing too much attention was dangerous. He would have been labeled an obstacle—a potential enemy. It would not have been strange for someone to attempt to expel him before he became a larger problem.

She reminded them of the “Class Poll.” Even while being completely average, Kiyotaka had been at risk of expulsion. Had he been a dominant figure from the beginning, the strategies used against him would have been far more precise—and far more lethal.

That led to the key question: if hiding his strength had been so important before, why reveal it now?

Suzune explained that the situation had changed. The class was no longer the same. They had grown as a group—stronger, more united, more capable of making correct decisions. Now, they could protect their own.

She used Sudō as an example. Before, he had been a burden. Now, he had improved both academically and physically to the point of surpassing even Yukimura in the overall evaluation. In the past, no one could have protected him. Now, everyone would do so without hesitation.

The same applied to Kiyotaka. If someone attempted to target him now, the entire class could respond.

Even so, doubts lingered. Was Kiyotaka only exceptional in mathematics? Or was he still hiding something else?

Suzune did not answer. She knew she could not dispel all the suspicions of the emotionally charged Class D through logic alone, so she left space for Hirata to step in.

Calmly, he explained that “an unknown enemy is more dangerous than one who is fully revealed.” If even their own classmates did not know the true extent of Kiyotaka’s abilities, the other classes would have no way to accurately assess him. Any investigation would be futile.

Suzune nodded. Since attention was inevitable, the best course was to take advantage of it—to turn Kiyotaka into an enigma. An undefined threat.

Kei redirected the focus toward Suzune, praising her intelligence and reinforcing the narrative that everything had been done for the sake of the class. Without needing instructions, they understood the role they needed to play.

Gradually, the distrust began to dissipate. Even Yukimura stopped pressing the issue.

Thanks to Suzune’s  improvisation, the groundwork had been laid. From this point forward, even if Kiyotaka revealed more of his true strength, it could always be explained as something he had been hiding from the very beginning.
