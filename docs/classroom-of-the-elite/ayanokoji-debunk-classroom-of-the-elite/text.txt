Ayanokoji debunk.

Let’s talk about **Senpai’s old test-buying plan** from Volume 1 — the one Kiyo came up with. Honestly, **what’s so smart about this plan?** It’s actually kind of funny how, when Kiyo explained this plan to Kushida, she reacted like: *“Wow, you’re really something else.”*
Hey girl, did you really find that impressive? Is it **actually** that intelligent?
In the Zodiac Exam, **Kiyotaka and Class D were extremely lucky** because Ichinose and the other students in the group weren’t very smart.Kiyotaka’s so-called “analytical and deductive abilities” during the cruise arc are completely based on basic logic — all he did was track down who leaked the VIP information to Ryuuen using the context of Classes A, B, and D.

To me, it just looks like the author is **trying really hard to make Kiyotaka seem smart** by having other characters praise him — but in reality, there’s **nothing impressive or intelligent** about it. It’s a completely basic idea. Let’s be real: **any high school student could have come up with that**, and most students have done something similar **at least once** in their lives.

And then there’s the **school’s intention** behind the test — it’s just **nonsense and ridiculous**. What exactly are students supposed to learn from this? It reminds me of the **first Chuunin Exam in Naruto**, where the written test wasn’t about knowledge, but about evaluating the **ninja’s information-gathering skills**. Did the COTE author just copy that idea? *Lol.*

In **Naruto**, it made sense — they had special and creative ways to gather information, like useful justu that could apply to real missions later on. But what about COTE? What are the students supposed to gain from this test **if not actual academic knowledge?** Buying old test papers from senpai? Come on — **that’s just stupid**. If this is considered a “skill,” then it’s a **worthless skill** that has no real value for the future. It’s such a **basic, ordinary move** that even someone with an IQ of 90 could easily come up with it.

So buying old test papers from a senior is now considered a **“smart and impressive” tactic**? *Lol.* In real life, I didn’t even have to *buy* them — I just got them for free.

I didn’t see anything clever in Kiyo’s so-called strategy here. All I see is the author **trying to make Kiyo look smart** by rigging everything in his favor in a **ridiculous and unrealistic** way.

If someone had already come up with the idea to **ask everyone in the group to show their phone screens**, and the class or person who refused to do so would **most likely be the VIP**, then why didn’t they implement that strategy right from the beginning? Why wait until near the end of the exam?

If Ichinose or anyone in the group had implemented this idea earlier:

* Kiyo wouldn’t have had time to prepare a countermeasure, because **Sotomura and Yukimura from Class D didn’t know there was a VIP in their class**, so they would have shown their phones.
* Kiyo likely wouldn’t be as stupid as those two, so the ones who wouldn’t show their phones would be Class A, Kiyo, and Kei from Class D. Ichinose already knew about Class A’s strategy, so their refusal to show phones would be understandable.
* But **why wouldn’t Kiyo and Kei show their phones?** It could only mean **one of them is the VIP**, and so they’d be narrowed down early. The remaining time would be extremely disadvantageous for Class D.

If Kiyo wanted to reuse the trick of **swapping phones with Kei**, it would have been exposed because **Ichinose already knew Kiyotaka’s phone number**.

In the light novel, **Ichinose vaguely suspected Kei** was the VIP due to her facial expression, and there was still quite a bit of time left before the exam ended, so it’s very likely **Class B, C, or A would have identified the VIP and ended the test**. (They could deduce Kei is the VIP because Kiyo won’t show his phone to avoid exposing Kei, while Kei’s face shows anxiety and fear.)

→ If Kiyo were truly an intelligent character, he would have **predicted this situation** and asked the Class D members to **reveal to him if they were the VIP**, persuading and explaining the logic behind it. If Kiyo knew Class D had the VIP, then when Ichinose implemented the phone-checking strategy to corner the VIP, **Class D could just refuse to show their phones** and the others in the group wouldn’t be able to identify the target.

(Even the **author couldn’t fully utilize the potential of the mind game** he created. **Kiyotaka isn’t a genuinely smart character created by an intelligent writer** — he’s just extremely lucky because the plot always favors him.)

Another thing is that **Kiyo, the other students, and even the author himself didn’t fully understand the true nature of the test** (I’m not talking about figuring out the VIP assignment pattern based on names).

Let’s summarize the rules of this test:

* **Outcome 1**: All group members submit the correct VIP identity between 9:30 pm and 10:00 pm → Everyone in the group gets 500,000 points, and the VIP gets 1,000,000 points.
* **Outcome 2**: No one submits an answer during that time window OR someone submits the wrong answer (except the VIP) → Only the VIP gets 500,000 points. Others get nothing.
* **Outcome 3**: A non-VIP student from a different class correctly identifies the VIP *before* 9:30 pm → That student earns 500,000 points, and their class gains 50 class points. The VIP’s class loses 50 class points.
* **Outcome 4**: A non-VIP student from a different class gives a **wrong** answer *before* 9:30 pm → Their class loses 50 class points, while the VIP gains 500,000 points and their class gains 50 class points.

Only the **first answer submitted** by any group member decides the result of that group’s test.

→ It’s clear that **Outcome 1 is the most beneficial** for everyone and carries the least risk.
Characters like Ichinose and Kiyotaka think that aiming for Outcome 1 involves high betrayal risk — but that’s only because they haven’t **thought deeply enough**, and the **author of COTE wasn’t intelligent enough** to fully exploit the mind game he created.

Outcome 1 is triggered when an answer is submitted between 9:30 pm and 10:00 pm.
Outcomes 3 and 4 are triggered by submissions *before* 9:30 pm.

→ So, to achieve Outcome 1 safely, **the VIP just needs to reveal themselves to the group at exactly 9:30 pm**.

And don’t tell me something like “the test ends at 9:00 pm so blah blah…” — there are plenty of ways for the VIP to contact the others in their group. The time window for Outcome 1 is 30 minutes long (9:30–10:00 pm), which is more than enough. Since “only the first answer received from any group member determines the test result,” it’s sufficient to just contact **at least three students from the other three classes** in the group.

**Outcome 1 and 2** are draws, while **Outcome 3 and 4** determine which class wins or loses.
To achieve Outcome 3, group members must identify the VIP.
To trigger Outcome 4, the VIP must mislead and cause students from other classes to **guess wrong**.

→ So aiming for Outcome 3 involves the risk of ending up with Outcome 4.

If **Katsuragi** understood this clearly, **Class A wouldn’t have made such a stupid mistake**.
To make Katsuragi’s strategy work, **Class A had to avoid Outcome 4** by not submitting a VIP guess before 9:30 pm. If Katsuragi had clearly explained this huge risk to the Class A students, **none of them would’ve dared to betray** (unless their IQ was under 90).

→ **Kiyotaka’s strategy isn’t impressive at all** — he just got lucky because **his opponents weren’t smart enough**.

About Kiyotaka traitor deduction, his deduction feat really isn’t anything impressive. Ryuuen claiming he knows all the VIPs boils down to two simple possibilities:

1. He figured out the VIP selection pattern by using the known VIPs from his own class.
2. He got info from students in other classes.

That’s it. Nothing high-level going on here.

As for figuring out Kushida was working with him — that wasn’t hard either. If Kushida wanted to ensure her class got a perfect result with no betrayals, that basically means she admitted her role to Ryuuen. Besides, her intentions to sabotage Class D were already blatantly obvious.

And that hug scene on the boat? Absolutely ridiculous. They're supposed to be working together in secret, yet they meet in a very public area? Then when Kiyotaka shows up, they suddenly have to go hide like fugitives? Dumb.

The deduction about Kanzaki is just as basic: he was in the same group as Ryuuen. Class C made a mistake (even though Ryuuen said he knew everything), and Class B, despite having one VIP picked, didn’t lose any points. So it's obvious Ryuuen deliberately let Class B guess wrong to feed them points.

The induction method for finding the VIP pattern was also simple. The zodiac animal’s number for a group matched the position of the VIP’s name in the group list. Once you know the rule, plugging names in and figuring out who the VIP is isn’t hard at all. There was no need to draw it out like it was some genius masterstroke — unless the point was just to stretch the spotlight on the protagonist.

And also about Kiyo luring Manabe. The real issue is that **the condition Kiyo gave to Manabe is shady as hell**, like some back-alley thug move. There’s **nothing trustworthy about it** at all.
(Like, seriously — luring a girl to some isolated place just to beat her up? **That’s some messed-up, borderline psycho stuff.** Of course it would look suspicious as hell.)

Kiyo's predictions were way too obvious — he foresaw everything that happened on the Y2 island.
=> But were the Y2 events even that complicated? The strategies were mid-tier at best. The rules clearly stated that the lowest-ranking team would face expulsion, so of course Kiyo figured out that the others would try to stop him from reaching the designated zone, and the White Room guys would just try to beat him up :))
But honestly, the strategies were so basic — nothing that required much calculation.

That whole “plan” on the deserted island in 2nd ? It was so bland, shallow, and basic. The reason it feels so mid is because the thinking behind it is basic and not even remotely innovative.

The use of space in that “feat” wasn’t some high-level calculation. It was super predictable — once they knew where Kiyotaka wanted to go, they blocked his path. (Is that really clever?) Kiyotaka anticipated that and deliberately changed direction, leading the pursuers into difficult terrain. (Is that really smart?)

Then he got the second-years to help block the enemy’s path so he could escape — like some typical Shounen anime trope where the main character gets cornered, and then his allies show up like, “We’ve got this, go fight the boss!” How is that supposed to be big-brain or high IQ?

Recording Kushida when she reveals her true nature to use as evidence — wow, sounds *so* high-level and creative :)) This kind of "brilliance" is supposed to be high IQ?

That whole plan where Kiyo put the test answers into Kushida’s blazer? Honestly, it was incredibly dumb and pointless.

If Kiyo’s goal was to find out how many blazers Kushida owned, there were way simpler, more natural ways to do it. For example, he could’ve just spilled something dirty on her blazer during PE. When she noticed her blazer was stained, Kei could’ve casually asked, “Do you have another one?” — and boom, they’d know how many she had, all without raising any suspicion.

As Ryuen himself explained, the only real purpose of Kei soaking the blazer was to confirm how many Kushida had. Planting test answers during that moment was basically doomed to fail — Kushida could’ve just washed it, which would’ve destroyed the paper entirely. So even as a method for confirming her number of blazers, this plan was inefficient, overly risky, and drew unnecessary attention.

It honestly seems like the author was just trying *really hard* to make Kiyo look smart, when in reality, his plan was shallow and kind of idiotic. The only reason it “worked” is because the author forced it to work, not because the plan itself was clever or well-thought-out.

Ryuen even told Kushida, “There might be more. Check your personal belongings.” That line sounds like the author speaking through Ryuen to justify Kiyo’s plan. But if Kiyo really did hide the test answers in multiple places, then making Kei spill something on her blazer just becomes even more redundant and meaningless.

The more I analyze it, the more it feels like the *COTE* author doesn’t even have an IQ over 100. He constantly tries to show that the characters are doing something super intelligent, but if you actually think critically about it, the plans fall apart and reveal how pointless and nonsensical they really are.

And if someone tries to argue that Kiyo only did it as a backup in case the paper got discovered elsewhere, that’s a weak excuse. If Kushida *had* found a test paper somewhere, she would’ve become way more cautious about all her belongings, making it even harder to plant anything afterward. In short, hiding the test results in her blazer or personal items was a flawed and pointless plan from the start.

And the fact that Kiyo intentionally put the test answers into Kushida’s blazer or belongings was also a meaningless and terrible plan. If Koji wanted to accuse her of cheating during the test, she could easily claim she was being framed. If the answers were hidden in a spot so hard to access or detect (to the point where even Kushida didn’t notice it), it would actually make her excuse more believable. During the exam, the proctors would have clearly seen that Kushida didn’t act suspiciously. She could claim that Horikita was trying to frame her and reveal the bet that had been made between them — with Horikita’s brother as a witness.

Kushida could argue that Horikita initiated this bet to motivate her to study hard and get a perfect score, and then teamed up with someone from Class C to plant the answer sheet in her belongings, making her a suspect *precisely because of* her genuine efforts. With the bet revealed and considering Kushida’s influence, her words would be far more convincing than Horikita and Koji’s.

Even revealing Horikita’s plan to Ryuen just to get Class C’s test answers was a downright foolish move by Koji. If Koji simply wanted to trap Kushida, he could’ve used a paper filled with important formulas or subject theory. Exposing Horikita’s strategy was extremely dumb and risky. Ryuen having no interest in getting Horikita expelled is an enormous **plot armor** for Kiyo — there’s nothing forcing Ryuen to keep Horikita around, especially when he had the perfect chance to take down “X.”

If Ryuen knew Kiyotaka was “X” (and based on his reasoning skills mentioned earlier, even if he only suspected Koji was a subordinate), he’d be curious to see how “X” would handle the situation or whether he would lose to Ryuen. He would’ve gone all-in on supporting Kushida during the Paper Shuffle exam. If Ryuen knew about the bet between Horikita and Kushida, he’d definitely want to see what “X” would do, and if Horikita lost, Kiyo would be expelled too.

And there’s a massive **plot hole** here. Koji gave Ryuen a deal: "Either give me the questions and answers to the final exam or change the questions and answers that were already sent to Kikyo." The ridiculous part is, Ryuen apparently chose the second option — to change the questions. So why did Koji still have the test answers to frame Kushida? Ryuen even admitted he sent them to Koji during his conversation with Kushida — but why would he do that if he had already changed the answers? It’s completely illogical — just plain plot nonsense that shows how unintelligent the author really is.

Ryuen could’ve easily reversed the trap: send the *old* answers to Koji while preparing new ones for Kushida. And he could’ve done the exact same thing to Horikita — hide test answers in her belongings (maybe during Class D’s PE class), and have Kushida accuse *her* of cheating during the test.

→ Koji’s plan is terrible in every scenario. It’s riddled with **plot armor** and **plot convenience.** Even if we overlook *one* plot armor, another one instantly pops up. His plan only succeeded because the **author wanted it to succeed**, not because it was logically sound or strategically clever. The author simply isn’t smart enough to write a plan that makes Koji win **logically**.

Kiyo’s strategies are extremely weak and uncreative — they’re just repeated over and over again. Even the winning condition is recycled, like the "find the leader" game on the uninhabited island and the "target" game (both involving fake info being leaked so other teams pick incorrectly). It’s all so bland and underwhelming, even though the game rules try to present themselves as “grand” and complex.

On top of that, Kiyo fakes “leaking false info” to the idiot Ryuen and the clueless Takuya so they’ll make the wrong moves. And the entire *COTE* cast just keeps using these cheap tactics to look smart — despite the fact that these tricks are old, overused, and painfully shallow. The only reason they succeed is because their opponents are written to be *stupid*, or the school’s system is so **poorly built** that nonsense like “recording threats,” “getting someone to punch another person and filming it,” or “hurting yourself and blaming someone else” actually works.

The fact that **Kushida and Ryuen never exchanged information with each other** is a major **plot armor** for Kiyotaka. If they were intelligent enough, they could easily piece together the clues and realize that **Kiyotaka is X**, and they would have been fully capable of stopping his mediocre schemes.

* Kiyo was the one who devised the plan to help Sudou’s group in Volume 1.
* Kiyo was the one who investigated and helped Sakura (Kushida knows this).
* Kiyo is the one who knows Kushida’s dark side.
* Kiyo was the leader of Class D during the first uninhabited island test.
* Kiyo witnessed Manabe’s group bullying Kei.
* Kiyo is the one who told Manabe that he saw Kei fighting with Rika.
* Kiyo is the one who sent that message to Manabe (if Ryuen were smart enough, he could easily deduce this).
* Kiyo is the one who asked Kushida: “You’re the traitor, aren’t you?”
* Kiyo showed his true abilities during the relay race.

→ With all this information, there are **more compelling reasons to believe Kiyotaka is X** than to believe he isn’t. The author is just forcing Ryuen to think incorrectly and unnaturally so that Kiyo has the advantage. In reality, Kiyo’s plan to deceive Ryuen makes little sense if analyzed logically. The author simply forces Ryuen to have flawed reasoning, even though the evidence pointing to Kiyotaka being X is far more convincing than any reason to think otherwise.

The idea that this school “trains elite students with exceptional abilities” is just an empty claim from the author. But the real question is — does the author actually have the intellectual capacity to make the characters in that school *truly* feel like geniuses? Based on what we’ve seen, the answer is clearly no.

It’s not just the so-called “average” students who come off as unimpressive — even the “strategic geniuses” in *Classroom of the Elite* mostly rely on petty, dirty tricks that are outdated and shallow. Honestly, even a middle schooler with average intelligence could come up with these tactics:

* provoking someone into hitting you and then reporting it to a teacher,
* stealing exam questions from seniors because you’re bad at studying (ridiculous),
* luring someone into attacking someone else and recording it to blackmail them,
* threatening someone to become your pawn or else you’ll snitch,
* disabling cameras to ambush the main character but *not* considering he might call in witnesses,
* spreading nasty rumors and acting like it’s some big brain “manipulation.”

There’s no real intelligence in these ancient, cliché tactics. These characters might be considered “geniuses” *within* the author's story, but by real-world standards — or even just by comparing them to characters from other mind-game-focused series — calling them amateurs wouldn’t be an exaggeration.
